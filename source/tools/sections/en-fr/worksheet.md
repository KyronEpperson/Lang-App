## LESSON sounds — 1 · Sounds, silent letters and liaison — deck "Sounds and spelling"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
Under every French phrase in this course there is a pronunciation line in English letters. Each syllable gets the same weight: French has no strong stressed syllable, only the last one of a group a little longer.
 Eight vowel sounds
 The line always spells a vowel the same way. Two are new for English speakers: ew (say ee , then round your lips as if to whistle) and uh (the a of about , with rounded lips). French ay and oh are pure: no y or w glide at the end.
 Line ; Spelled ; Example ; 
 ah ; a, à ; la ; 
 ay ; é, -er, -ez ; la clé ; 
 eh ; è, ê, ai ; la mère ; 
 ee ; i, y ; le lit ; 
 oh ; o, au, eau ; l'eau ; 
 oo ; ou ; la roue ; 
 ew ; u ; la rue ; 
 uh ; eu, e ; le feu ; 
 u and ou make different words.
 EX: la rue | lah rew | the street
 EX: la roue | lah roo | the wheel
CARDS NOW: la = the (feminine) [card] || la clé = the key [card] || le lit = the bed [card] || l'eau = the water [card] || ou = or [card] || la roue = the wheel [card] || la rue = the street [card] || le feu = fire; traffic light [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 5)
Nasal vowels
 A vowel followed by n or m at the end of a syllable is said through the nose, and the n itself is not pronounced. The line shows this with a capital N: ahN ( an, en ), ohN ( on ), aN ( in, ain ), uhN ( un ). English speakers tend to merge ahN and ohN ; they are different words.
 EX: le vin | luh vaN | the wine
 EX: le vent | luh vahN | the wind
 EX: le pont | luh pohN | the bridge
CARDS NOW: on = we (spoken); one; people [card] || un = a; one (masculine) [card] || le vin = the wine [card] || le vent = the wind [card] || le pont = the bridge [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 5)
Silent final letters
 Most final consonants are silent. Four are usually sounded: c, r, f, l (think of the word careful ). But -er and -ez at the end sound ay , and a final e is silent, except in short words such as le and je .
 EX: le chat | luh shah | the cat
 EX: le sac | luh sahk | the bag
 EX: parler | pahr-lay | to speak
CARDS NOW: le = the (masculine) [card] || je = I [card] || le chat = the cat [card] || le sac = the bag [card] || parler = to speak; to talk [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Linking words
 Liaison (linking): in a few fixed places, a silent final letter is said before a word that starts with a vowel. The line puts that sound at the start of the next word. A sounded final consonant also runs into a following vowel ( il est sounds like ee leh ), but the line keeps those words apart. Elision: le and la drop their vowel before a vowel and become l' .
 EX: les amis | lay zah-mee | the friends
 EX: l'école | lay-kohl | the school
CARDS NOW: les amis = the friends [card] || l'école = the school

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
Consonants
 French r is made at the back of the throat, close to a light gargle. zh is the s in measure (French j ), ny is the ny in canyon (French gn ), and gh is the hard g of get .
CARDS NOW: (none)

### SECTION 5   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
le, la, les
 Nouns in this course come with a small word in front that means "the": le or la for one thing, les for more than one. Every noun is masculine or feminine (lesson 3 explains), so learn each noun with its word for "the".
 EX: la clé | lah klay | the key
 The line is a crutch: listen first, and hide it once the spelling feels natural.
 Most common mistake: saying every letter you see. Paris with the s sounded → Paris ending on ee .
CARDS NOW: les = the (plural) [card] || Paris = Paris [card]

## LESSON greetings — 2 · Greetings: tu and vous — deck "Greetings and courtesy"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
In France, bonjour is not optional. You say it when you walk into a shop, before you ask a stranger anything, and to the driver when you get on a bus. Skipping it sounds rude, even if the rest of your French is perfect.
 Hello and goodbye
 Bonjour works all day, until about 6 p.m.; then it is bonsoir . Add madame or monsieur in shops. With friends, say salut (hi, and also bye). To leave, say au revoir .
 EX: Bonjour, madame. | bohN-zhoor mah-dahm | Hello, ma'am.
 EX: Salut, Léa! | sah-lew lay-ah | Hi, Léa!
CARDS NOW: Bonsoir. = Good evening. [card] || Au revoir. = Goodbye. [card] || Bonjour, madame. = Hello (to a woman). [card] || Salut, Léa! = Hi, Léa!

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Your first agreement
 To wish someone a good day, say Bonne journée! ; for the evening, Bonne soirée! The word for "good" is bon , but journée and soirée are feminine words, so "good" takes its feminine form, bonne . This matching, called agreement, runs through all of French.
 EX: Bonne journée! | bohn zhoor-nay | Have a nice day!
CARDS NOW: Bonne journée! = Have a nice day! [card] || bon = good [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 8)
Two ways to say "you"
 English has one "you". French has two. Use tu with friends, family, children and classmates. Use vous with strangers, shop and café staff, a friend's parents and anyone clearly older. Vous is also the only way to say "you" to two or more people, even friends. Several phrases change with it:
 ; To a friend (tu) ; To a stranger (vous) ; 
 How are you? ; Comment vas-tu? ; Comment allez-vous? ; 
 Please. ; S'il te plaît. ; S'il vous plaît. ; 
 Excuse me. ; Excuse-moi. ; Excusez-moi. ; 
 And you? ; Et toi? ; Et vous? ; 
 EX: Excusez-moi, monsieur. | ehks-kew-zay mwah muh-syuh | Excuse me, sir.
 EX: Comment allez-vous? | koh-mahN tah-lay voo | How are you?
 When in doubt, use vous . If the other person prefers tu , they will say so. Between friends, the everyday question is simply Ça va? ("Is it going?"), and the answer is Ça va too.
 EX: Ça va? | sah vah | How's it going?
 In Québec, tu is used much more widely, even with teachers; vous is kept mainly for age and respect.
CARDS NOW: tu = you (one person, friendly) [card] || vous = you (polite or plural) [card] || Comment allez-vous? = How are you? [card] || S'il vous plaît. = Please. [card] || Excusez-moi. = Excuse me. [card] || Et toi? = And you? [card] || Excusez-moi, monsieur. = Excuse me, sir. || Ça va? = How's it going?

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Names
 To give your name, say Je m'appelle ("I call myself"). When you meet someone, say Enchanté . A woman writes Enchantée , with an extra e. You can't hear it, but it is agreement again.
 EX: Je m'appelle Emma. | zhuh mah-pehl ay-mah | My name is Emma.
 EX: Enchantée. | ahN-shahN-tay | Nice to meet you.
CARDS NOW: Enchantée. = Nice to meet you. [card] || Je m'appelle Emma. = My name is Emma. [card]

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Thank you
 Merci is thank you; merci beaucoup is thank you very much. The reply is de rien (you're welcome).
 EX: Merci beaucoup. | mehr-see boh-koo | Thank you very much.
 Most common mistake: using tu with a stranger. Excuse-moi, madame → Excusez-moi, madame .
CARDS NOW: Merci beaucoup. = Thank you very much. [card] || De rien. = You're welcome. [card]

## LESSON articles — 3 · Gender and articles: le, la, l', les, un, une, des — deck "Home and routine"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
English has one word for "the" and two for "a" ( a , an ). French has more, because every French noun is either masculine or feminine, and the small word in front of it changes to match.
 Every noun has a gender
 The gender of a thing has nothing to do with the thing itself: a table is feminine, a bed is masculine. There is no reliable way to guess most of them, so learn every noun together with its article (the small word in front), never alone.
CARDS NOW: (none)

### SECTION 1   (has 6)
"The": le, la, les
 Use le before a masculine noun and la before a feminine noun. For more than one, both become les .
 ; Masculine ; Feminine ; 
 one ; le lit ; la table ; 
 more than one ; les lits ; les tables ; 
 EX: la maison | lah meh-zohN | the house
 EX: le lit | luh lee | the bed
CARDS NOW: le = the (masculine) [card] || la = the (feminine) [card] || les = the (plural) [card] || le lit = the bed [card] || la table = table [card] || la maison = the house

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
l' before a vowel
 Before a vowel or most words starting with h , le and la both shrink to l' . This is called elision. It makes French flow, but it hides the gender, so learn these nouns with un or une instead (see below).
 EX: l'appartement | lah-pahr-tuh-mahN | the apartment
 EX: l'école | lay-kohl | the school
CARDS NOW: un = a; one (masculine) [card] || une = a; one (feminine) [card] || l'appartement = the apartment || l'école = the school

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
"A": un, une
 Un goes with a masculine noun, une with a feminine one. They also mean "one".
 EX: un canapé | uhN kah-nah-pay | a couch
 EX: une chaise | ewn shehz | a chair
 EX: une école | ewn ay-kohl | a school
CARDS NOW: un canapé = a couch || une chaise = a chair || une école = school [card]

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
"Some": des
 English often puts nothing before a plural: "chairs", "rooms". French can't leave the space empty. The plural of un and une is des , which means "some", or nothing at all in English.
 EX: des chaises | day shehz | chairs; some chairs
CARDS NOW: des = some (plural of un, une) [card] || des chaises = chairs; some chairs

### SECTION 5   (has 3)
You hear the article, not the -s
 To make a noun plural, add -s , as in English. But that -s is silent: chaise and chaises sound the same. What you hear is the article: la ( lah ) for one, les ( lay ) for more than one. In speech, the article does the work that the -s does in English.
 EX: les chambres | lay shahNbr | the bedrooms
 One small help: nouns ending in -tion are feminine ( la question , la nation ).
 Most common mistake: using le for every noun. le chambre → la chambre . When you learn a noun, learn its article with it.
CARDS NOW: les chambres = the bedrooms || la question = question [card] || la chambre = the bedroom [card]

## LESSON etre-avoir — 4 · être and avoir: I am, I have — deck "Traps for English speakers"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
The two most useful French verbs are être (to be) and avoir (to have). Both are irregular, so learn them by heart. Once you know them, you can already say a lot.
 Who does it: subject pronouns
 English: I, you, he, she, we, they. French has a few more. Je becomes j' before a vowel. Ils means "they" for men or for a mixed group; elles is only for women. Il and elle also mean "it", following the noun's gender. On , the everyday "we", comes in the next lesson.
CARDS NOW: être = to be [card] || avoir = to have [card] || je = I [card] || ils = they (masculine or mixed) [card] || elles = they (all feminine) [card] || il = he; it (masculine) [card] || elle = she; it (feminine); her (after a preposition) [card] || on = we (spoken); one; people [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
être: to be
 English changes "be" three ways (am, are, is). French changes it six ways, one for each subject.
 English ; être ; avoir ; 
 I ; je suis ; j'ai ; 
 you (friend) ; tu es ; tu as ; 
 he, she, it ; il / elle est ; il / elle a ; 
 we ; nous sommes ; nous avons ; 
 you (stranger, or two or more) ; vous êtes ; vous avez ; 
 they ; ils / elles sont ; ils / elles ont ; 
 EX: Je suis à Paris. | zhuh swee zah pah-ree | I'm in Paris.
 EX: Nous sommes à Lyon. | noo sohm zah lyohN | We're in Lyon.
 EX: Vous êtes en retard. | voo zeht zahN ruh-tahr | You're late.
 Tu es , il est and elle est sound the same ( eh ), so the spelling is what shows who it is.
CARDS NOW: Je suis à Paris. = I'm in Paris. || Nous sommes à Lyon. = We're in Lyon. || Vous êtes en retard. = You're late.

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
avoir: to have
 EX: J'ai un chat. | zhay uhN shah | I have a cat.
 EX: Ils ont une maison. | eel zohN ewn meh-zohN | They have a house.
 Listen for the difference between ils sont (they are, with an s sound) and ils ont (they have, with a z sound).
CARDS NOW: J'ai un chat. = I have a cat. || Ils ont une maison. = They have a house.

### SECTION 3   (has 5)
avoir where English says "be"
 Here is the big trap. For age, hunger, thirst and feeling hot or cold, English says "I am", but French says "I have": I have twenty years, I have hunger.
 English ; French ; 
 I'm twenty. ; J'ai vingt ans. ; 
 I'm hungry. ; J'ai faim. ; 
 I'm thirsty. ; J'ai soif. ; 
 I'm cold. / I'm hot. ; J'ai froid. / J'ai chaud. ; 
 You're right. ; Tu as raison. ; 
 EX: J'ai faim. | zhay faN | I'm hungry.
 EX: Elle a vingt-cinq ans. | ehl ah vaNt saNk ahN | She's twenty-five.
 EX: J'ai froid. | zhay frwah | I'm cold.
 Always say ans (years) after the number: J'ai vingt ans , never just J'ai vingt .
 Most common mistake: using être for age. Je suis vingt-cinq → J'ai vingt-cinq ans .
CARDS NOW: J'ai faim. = I'm hungry. [card] || J'ai soif. = I'm thirsty. [card] || J'ai froid. = I'm cold. [card] || Elle a vingt-cinq ans. = She's twenty-five. || J'ai vingt-cinq ans. = I'm twenty-five. [card]

## LESSON pronouns — 5 · Subject and stress pronouns: je, moi, on — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 12)
English has two words for the speaker: I and me . French also has two, but it uses its "me" word in places where English says "I".
 Two sets of pronouns
 The subject pronouns ( je, tu, il …) can only stand right before a verb. In the four places below, French uses a stress pronoun (textbooks also say "disjunctive pronoun"). For "me" or "her" as the object of a verb ("I see her"), French has another set, taught later.

 ils ; eux ; them (men, or a mixed group) ; 
 elles ; elles ; them (women) ;
CARDS NOW: je = I [card] || tu = you (one person, friendly) [card] || il = he; it (masculine) [card] || moi = me (stress pronoun) [card] || toi = you (stress pronoun, friendly) [card] || lui = him; to him; to her [card] || elle = she; it (feminine); her (after a preposition) [card] || nous = we; us [card] || vous = you (polite or plural) [card] || ils = they (masculine or mixed) [card] || eux = them (stress pronoun, masculine or mixed) [card] || elles = they (all feminine) [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 6)
Where the stress pronouns go
 Alone , as a short answer. English says "Me!"; French says Moi! , never Je! 
 EX: Moi! | mwah | Me!
 After a little word like avec (with), pour (for) or chez (at the home of). 
 EX: avec toi | ah-vehk twah | with you
 EX: pour lui | poor lwee | for him
 EX: chez moi | shay mwah | at my place
 After c'est (it is), and in Et toi? (And you?), the friendly way to bounce a question back.
 EX: C'est moi. | seh mwah | It's me.
 EX: Et toi? | ay twah | And you?
 For emphasis , in front of the subject. English stresses the word with the voice (" I'm hungry"); French adds the stress pronoun.
 EX: Moi, j'ai faim. | mwah zhay faN | I'm hungry. (Me, I'm hungry.)
CARDS NOW: avec toi = with you || pour lui = for him || chez moi = at my place || Et toi? = And you? [card] || C'est moi. = It's me. || Moi, j'ai faim. = I'm hungry. (Me, I'm hungry.)

### SECTION 2   (has 3)
on: the everyday "we"
 In conversation, French people say on much more often than nous for "we". The trap: on takes the same verb form as il and elle , not the nous form.

 Both are correct. On can also mean "people" or "you" in general, like English "one". In this course, when a prompt says "we", either one is fine unless it asks for nous or on .
 Most common mistake: using the subject pronoun after a preposition. avec je → avec moi .
CARDS NOW: on = we (spoken); one; people [card] || On est en retard. = We're late. || On a faim. = We're hungry.

## LESSON adjectives — 6 · Adjectives: agreement and place — deck "Shopping and clothes"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
In English, an adjective never changes: a white shirt, white shoes. In French, an adjective agrees with its noun: it takes the noun's gender (masculine or feminine) and number (one or more).
 Most adjectives go after the noun
 English puts the color first: "a white shirt". French puts it after: "a shirt white". Colors, nationalities and most other adjectives work this way.
 EX: la chemise blanche | lah shuh-meez blahNsh | the white shirt
 EX: un pull vert | uhN pewl vehr | a green sweater
CARDS NOW: la chemise blanche = the white shirt [card] || un pull vert = a green sweater

### SECTION 1   (has 8)
Four forms
 Start from the masculine form. Add -e for feminine, -s for plural, -es for feminine plural.
 ; Masculine ; Feminine ; 
 one ; un pull vert ; une veste verte ; 
 more than one ; des pulls verts ; des vestes vertes ; 
 EX: une veste verte | ewn vehst vehrt | a green jacket
 EX: les chaussures noires | lay shoh-sewr nwahr | the black shoes
 An adjective that already ends in -e , like rouge or jeune , doesn't add another one: une robe rouge . A few have their own feminine form: blanc becomes blanche , beau becomes belle .
CARDS NOW: le pull rouge = the red sweater [card] || une veste verte = a green jacket || la robe verte = the green dress [card] || les chaussures noires = the black shoes [card] || rouge = red [card] || jeune = young [card] || blanc = white [card] || beau = handsome; beautiful [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 3)
Sometimes you hear it, sometimes you don't
 The plural -s is silent (it is heard only as a liaison z before a vowel). The feminine -e is silent too, but it can wake up the consonant before it. Vert ends in a silent t; in verte , the t is heard. Bleu and bleue , noir and noire , sound exactly the same, so only spelling shows the agreement.
CARDS NOW: vert = green [card] || bleu = blue [card] || noir = black [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
After être, too
 The adjective agrees even when it comes after être . A masculine noun and a feminine noun together take the masculine plural.
 EX: La robe est noire. | lah rohb eh nwahr | The dress is black.
 EX: Le pull et la veste sont noirs. | luh pewl ay lah vehst sohN nwahr | The sweater and the jacket are black.
CARDS NOW: être = to be [card] || La robe est noire. = The dress is black. || Le pull et la veste sont noirs. = The sweater and the jacket are black.

### SECTION 4   (has 8)
A short group goes before the noun
 A handful of short, common adjectives go in front, as in English: petit (small), grand (big, tall), bon (good), beau (beautiful), nouveau (new), jeune (young), vieux (old). They still agree.
 EX: une petite maison | ewn puh-teet meh-zohN | a small house
 EX: un grand manteau | uhN grahN mahN-toh | a big coat

### SECTION 0   (has 7)

## LESSON partitive — 13 · du, de la, des: some — deck "Food and drink"
### SECTION 0   (has 7)
English often uses no article at all with food: "I'm eating bread", "I want water". French never leaves the noun bare. It picks one of two articles, depending on what you mean.
 Some: an amount of something
 When you mean an amount (some bread, a bit of cheese, any water), French uses the partitive article. Think of it as "some", even where English says nothing. It agrees with the noun: masculine, feminine, before a vowel, or plural.

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
In general: le, la, les
 When you talk about a thing in general, as a whole category, French uses le, la, les . That's the case after verbs of liking: aimer, adorer, détester . English says "I like coffee"; French says "I like the coffee".
 EX: J'aime le café. | zhehm luh kah-fay | I like coffee.
 EX: Je n'aime pas la viande. | zhuh nehm pah lah vyahNd | I don't like meat.
 Compare: Je mange du chocolat (I'm eating some chocolate) and J'adore le chocolat (I love chocolate, all of it).
CARDS NOW: J'aime le café. = I like coffee. || Je n'aime pas la viande. = I don't like meat. || Je ne mange pas de viande. = I don't eat meat. [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
Just de: after a negative and after amounts
 After ne … pas , the partitive shrinks to de (or d' before a vowel). The same de follows words of quantity like beaucoup (a lot) and un peu (a little).
 EX: Il n'y a pas de lait. | eel nee ah pah duh leh | There's no milk.
 EX: Elle mange beaucoup de fruits. | ehl mahNzh boh-koo duh frwee | She eats a lot of fruit.
 Careful: after a liking verb, the negative keeps le, la, les ( Je n'aime pas la viande ), because that sentence is about meat in general.
CARDS NOW: de = of; from [card] || beaucoup = a lot; much; many [card] || Il n'y a pas de lait. = There's no milk. [card] || Elle mange beaucoup de fruits. = She eats a lot of fruit.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Ordering

## LESSON polite — 14 · je voudrais: polite requests — deck "Food and drink"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
In a French shop or café, how you ask matters as much as what you ask for. Learn these as fixed phrases; you don't need to know how the verbs are built.
 Start with bonjour
 English speakers often go straight to the request: "A baguette, please." In France, skipping bonjour sounds rude, so say it first, every time, and add madame or monsieur to sound warmer. Use vous with all staff.
 EX: Bonjour, madame. Je voudrais une baguette. | bohN-zhoor mah-dahm zhuh voo-dreh ewn bah-gheht | Hello. I'd like a baguette.
CARDS NOW: vous = you (polite or plural) [card] || Bonjour, madame. Je voudrais une baguette. = Hello. I'd like a baguette.

### SECTION 1   (has 5)
je voudrais: I'd like
 Je voudrais is the polite "I'd like", and it is what everyone says at a counter. Je veux means "I want": correct French, but it sounds blunt to a server, like a child asking. In the same way, Donnez-moi… (Give me…) without s'il vous plaît sounds like an order. The article after it still agrees with the noun: un croissant , une baguette , and deux croissants with a plural -s you can't hear.
 EX: Je voudrais un croissant, s'il vous plaît. | zhuh voo-dreh uhN krwah-sahN seel voo pleh | I'd like a croissant, please.
 EX: Je voudrais deux croissants. | zhuh voo-dreh duh krwah-sahN | I'd like two croissants.
CARDS NOW: S'il vous plaît. = Please. [card] || une baguette, s'il vous plaît = a baguette, please [card] || un croissant = a croissant [card] || Je voudrais un croissant, s'il vous plaît. = I'd like a croissant, please. || Je voudrais deux croissants. = I'd like two croissants.

### SECTION 2   (has 5)
Can I…? Could you…?
 To ask if you may do something, say Est-ce que je pourrais + a verb in the infinitive, or simply Je pourrais…? with a rising voice. To ask someone to do something for you, say Vous pourriez…? The endings follow the person: je pourrais , but vous pourriez .
 EX: Est-ce que je pourrais avoir l'addition? | ehs kuh zhuh poo-reh ah-vwahr lah-dee-syohN | Could I have the check?

CARDS NOW: L'addition, s'il vous plaît. = The check, please. [card] || Est-ce que je pourrais avoir l'addition? = Could I have the check? || Je pourrais avoir de l'eau? = Could I have some water? || Vous pourriez m'aider? = Could you help me? || Vous pourriez répéter, s'il vous plaît? = Could you repeat that, please?

Ordering a meal

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Finishing up

## LESSON prepositions — 15 · Prepositions of place and time — deck "Travel and transport"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
English uses "to", "at" and "in" for places. French often uses one word, à , for all three, and it changes shape with the article that follows.

## LESSON spoken — 23 · Spoken French: what you'll hear — deck "Spoken French"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
The French you have learned so far is correct everywhere, and it is what you should write. But French people talking to friends drop and squeeze sounds, just as English speakers say "gonna" and "dunno". This lesson is about recognizing those sentences when you hear them. You don't have to speak this way.
 ne disappears
 In relaxed everyday speech, the ne of ne … pas is very often dropped. The pas stays, so the sentence is still negative. In writing, keep the ne . This is one of the most common differences between textbook French and the French you hear among friends, so expect it in many of the negative sentences you hear; in careful or formal speech the ne comes back.
 EX: Je sais pas. | zhuh seh pah | I don't know.

 EX: On y va? | ohN nee vah | Shall we go?

## LESSON imparfait — 24 · Imparfait vs passé composé — deck "Days, dates and weather"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
The passé composé tells what happened: one finished event. French has a second past, the imparfait , for the background: what was going on, what things were like, and what used to happen again and again. English spreads this over "was raining", "used to play" and plain "was".
 Building it
 Take the nous form of the present, remove -ons , and add the endings below. This works for almost every verb, even irregular ones: nous faisons → je faisais , nous prenons → je prenais . The one exception is être , whose stem is ét- .

 tu ; parlais ; étais ;

 Four of the six forms sound exactly the same. Only the spelling shows who is talking.
CARDS NOW: nous = we; us [card] || être = to be [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 5)
Background and description
 Weather, time, feelings and what a place was like go in the imparfait. Three phrases you will use constantly: c'était (it was), il y avait (there was, there were), il faisait (the weather was).
 EX: Il faisait beau. | eel fuh-zeh boh | The weather was nice.
 EX: C'était super. | say-teh sew-pehr | It was great.
 EX: Il y avait du monde. | eel ee ah-veh dew mohNd | It was crowded.
 The same goes for the avoir expressions from earlier lessons. English says "I was hungry"; French keeps avoir and puts it in the imparfait.
 EX: J'avais froid. | zhah-veh frwah | I was cold.

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
Habits
 Where English says "used to" or "would" for a repeated action, French uses the imparfait. Words like souvent (often), tous les jours (every day) and le samedi (on Saturdays) are good signs that you need it.
 EX: Quand j'étais petit, je jouais au foot. | kahN zhay-teh puh-tee zhuh zhweh oh foot | When I was little, I used to play soccer.

CARDS NOW: souvent = often [card] || le samedi = on Saturdays [card] || Quand j'étais petit, je jouais au foot. = When I was little, I used to play soccer. || J'habitais à Lyon. = I lived in Lyon.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)

 In a story, the imparfait sets the scene and the passé composé moves the action forward. Ask yourself: is it a single event that happened, or the situation around it? A useful picture: the imparfait is the film running in the background, and the passé composé is the moment something happens on screen.
 EX: Il pleuvait quand je suis arrivé. | eel pluh-veh kahN zhuh swee zah-ree-vay | It was raining when I arrived.

CARDS NOW: Il pleuvait quand je suis arrivé. = It was raining when I arrived.

## LESSON futur — 25 · Futur simple — deck "Work and school"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
You already know one way to talk about the future: je vais travailler , "I'm going to work". French has a second one, the futur simple , which English translates with "will": je travaillerai , "I'll work". In conversation, je vais + verb is very common for plans. The futur simple is common too, especially for things further ahead, for predictions and promises, and in writing.
 Building it
 Start from the infinitive and add endings that look like the present of avoir : -ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont . Verbs in -re drop their final e first: prendre → je prendrai . Because the endings go on the whole infinitive, you hear an r before the ending in every form: that r is the sign of the future.

CARDS NOW: avoir = to have [card] || prendre = to take; to have (food, drink) [card] || Je travaillerai à Lyon. = I'll work in Lyon.

## LESSON object-pronouns — 26 · Pronouns le, lui, en, y — deck "Shopping and clothes"
### SECTION 0   (has 5)
English puts "it", "them" and "her" after the verb: "I'll take it." French puts these small pronouns before the conjugated verb: Je la prends , literally "I it take". This is the main habit to build. ## LESSON compare — 27 · Comparing: plus, moins, le plus — deck "People and family"
### SECTION 0   (has 9)
English compares with "-er" or "more": "taller", "more patient". French has no "-er" ending. It always puts a word in front of the adjective, and links the second person or thing with que , which plays the part of "than" and "as". The pattern is the same for every adjective, short or long, so there is no choice to make between "-er" and "more".
 more, less, as

 less tall than, shorter than ; moins grand que ; 
 as tall as ; aussi grand que ; 
 The adjective still agrees with the person or thing it describes, exactly as it would without the comparison. After que , use a stress pronoun: que moi , que toi , que lui . French often uses moins where English picks an opposite word: "less expensive" for "cheaper", "less tall" for "shorter".
 EX: Ma sœur est plus grande que moi. | mah suhr eh plew grahNd kuh mwah | My sister is taller than me.
 EX: Mon frère est moins grand que mon père. | mohN frehr eh mwaN grahN kuh mohN pehr | My brother is shorter than my dad.

### SECTION 1   (has 5)

### SECTION 2   (has 4)

 To compare amounts of a thing rather than a quality, put de between plus or moins and the noun, with no article: plus de temps , "more time"; moins de travail , "less work". It is the same de that follows beaucoup .
 EX: Il a plus de temps que moi. | eel ah plews duh tahN kuh mwah | He has more time than me.
CARDS NOW: de = of; from [card] || plus = more; no more (ne … plus) [card] || beaucoup = a lot; much; many [card] || Il a plus de temps que moi. = He has more time than me.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
the most: le plus
 For "the tallest" or "the most beautiful", add the article: le plus , la plus , les plus , agreeing with the noun. "The best" is le meilleur or la meilleure . The article agrees too, so a woman is la plus grande and a group is les plus grands . Where English says "in" after a superlative, French says de .
 EX: Thomas est le plus jeune. | toh-mah eh luh plew zhuhn | Thomas is the youngest.
 EX: C'est la plus belle ville de France. | seh lah plew behl veel duh frahNs | It's the most beautiful city in France.
 Adjectives that normally follow the noun keep that place, and the article appears twice: le film le plus intéressant , "the most interesting movie".
 Most common mistake: forgetting that the adjective still agrees. Ma sœur est plus grand que moi → Ma sœur est plus grande que moi .

## LESSON relatives — 28 · qui, que, où — deck "Likes and free time"
### SECTION 0   (has 5)
English joins a description to a noun with "who", "that", "which" or "where", and often drops the word completely: "the movie I saw". French has three words for this job, qui , que and où , and never drops them. Choosing between qui and que depends on one thing only: what comes right after.
 qui: the next word is a verb
 Use qui when it is the subject of the next verb: the person or thing that does the action. It means "who", "that" or "which", for people and things alike. qui never shortens before a vowel. Think of qui as standing in for the noun before it, in the subject slot of the next verb.
 EX: J'ai un ami qui habite à Lyon. | zhay uhN nah-mee kee ah-beet ah lyohN | I have a friend who lives in Lyon.
 EX: C'est le train qui part à huit heures. | seh luh traN kee pahr ah weet uhr | That's the train that leaves at eight.
 The verb after qui agrees with the noun that qui stands for: les amis qui parlent français .
CARDS NOW: qui = who; that (relative) [card] || que = that; than [card] || Où? = Where? [card] || J'ai un ami qui habite à Lyon. = I have a friend who lives in Lyon. || C'est le train qui part à huit heures. = That's the train that leaves at eight.

### SECTION 1   (has 5)
que: the next word is a subject
 Use que when the next verb already has its own subject ( je , tu , Léa …). It is where English can leave out "that", and it works for people as well as things: when English says "who" or "whom" with a new subject after it, French still says que . Before a vowel, que becomes qu' .
 EX: C'est le film que j'ai vu hier. | seh luh feelm kuh zhay vew yehr | That's the movie I saw yesterday.
 EX: Voilà le pain qu'il achète. | vwah-lah luh paN keel ah-sheht | Here's the bread he buys.
 A quick test: look at the word right after the gap. A verb means qui ; a subject means que .
CARDS NOW: je = I [card] || tu = you (one person, friendly) [card] || C'est le film que j'ai vu hier. = That's the movie I saw yesterday. || Hier, j'ai vu un film. = Yesterday I saw a movie. [card] || Voilà le pain qu'il achète. = Here's the bread he buys.

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
où: where, and when
 où joins a place, like "where". After a time word such as le jour or l'année , French also uses où where English says "when" or nothing at all. Keep the accent: ou without it means "or".
 EX: C'est la ville où j'habite. | seh lah veel oo zhah-beet | It's the town where I live.
 EX: Le jour où je suis arrivé, il pleuvait. | luh zhoor oo zhuh swee zah-ree-vay eel pluh-veh | The day I arrived, it was raining.
CARDS NOW: le jour = day [card] || ou = or [card] || C'est la ville où j'habite. = It's the town where I live. || Le jour où je suis arrivé, il pleuvait. = The day I arrived, it was raining.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
In the middle of a sentence
 The description can also sit between a subject and its verb, exactly as in English. Find the main verb at the end, and the sentence falls into place.
 EX: Le film que j'ai vu hier était super. | luh feelm kuh zhay vew yehr ay-teh sew-pehr | The movie I saw yesterday was great.
 Most common mistake: dropping the word, as English does. le film j'ai vu → le film que j'ai vu .
CARDS NOW: Le film que j'ai vu hier était super. = The movie I saw yesterday was great.
