## LESSON do-sounds — 2 · Dominican sounds: the breath s, ng and -ao — deck "Dominican sounds"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
You already know how Spanish vowels and letters sound, and Dominican Spanish keeps all of that: the vowels stay clear and never change. What changes is the end of a syllable. This lesson trains your ear for the changes you'll hear first in Santo Domingo. Spelling never changes: you always write standard Spanish.
 The breath s
 When an s ends a syllable, Dominicans often turn it into a breath, like a soft English h. Estoy sounds like "ehh-TOY", and ¿Para dónde vas? ends in a breath: "bahh". In relaxed speech the s can drop out completely. You still write every s.
 EX: Estoy en la casa. | ehs-TOY ehn lah KAH-sah | I'm at home.
 EX: ¿Para dónde vas? | PAH-rah DOHN-deh bahs | Where are you going?
CARDS NOW: ¿Para dónde vas? = Where are you going? [card] || Estoy en la casa. = I'm at home.

### SECTION 1   (has 6)
Plural without a heard s
 In English, you hear a plural in its s. In Dominican speech you may hear "lohh moo-CHAH-chohh", or even "lo muchacho" with no s at all. So listen to the small words around the noun: the article los or las , and the verb. Están is plural and está is singular, and that difference is always there.
 EX: Los muchachos están aquí. | lohs moo-CHAH-chohs ehs-TAHN ah-KEE | The guys are here.
 EX: Mis amigos están bien. | mees ah-MEE-gohs ehs-TAHN byehn | My friends are fine.
CARDS NOW: los = the (masculine plural) [card] || las = the (feminine plural) [card] || Está bien. = It's fine. [card] || Los muchachos están aquí. = The guys are here. || los muchachos = the guys; the kids [card] || Mis amigos están bien. = My friends are fine.

### SECTION 2   (has 3)
Final n: the ng of song
 At the end of a word, before a pause or a vowel, n often sounds like the ng in English song . El pan sounds like "ehl pahng", and bien like "byehng". It is still written n.
 EX: El pan está bien. | ehl pahn ehs-TAH byehn | The bread is fine.
CARDS NOW: el pan = bread [card] || bien = well; fine [card] || El pan está bien. = The bread is fine.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
cansado without its d
 When -ado carries the stress, the d usually disappears: cansado sounds like "cansao" (kahn-SAH-oh). Educated speakers say it this way too in everyday talk. You write cansado .
 EX: Estoy cansado. | ehs-TOY kahn-SAH-doh | I'm tired.
CARDS NOW: cansado = tired [card] || Estoy cansado. = I'm tired. [card]

### SECTION 4   (has 4)
Short forms and regional sounds
 Fast speech also clips whole words: para can shrink to "pa'" (¿Pa' dónde va'?), está to "'tá" ('tá bien) and todo to "to'". Dominicans write these short forms themselves in texts, but in this course you always write the full word. In the capital you may hear r as an l ("polque" for porque ). In rural parts of the Cibao, in the north, r can sound like an i ("poique"). Learn these to understand people, not to copy them: the audio is your model, and your spelling stays standard.
 EX: Está bien. | ehs-TAH byehn | It's fine.
 Write the word, not the sound you hear: Estoy cansao → Estoy cansado . And not hearing the s doesn't make a sentence singular: Los niños está aquí → Los niños están aquí .
CARDS NOW: para = for; in order to [card] || todo = all; the whole; everything [card] || porque = because [card] || Los niños están aquí. = The kids are here. [card]

## LESSON greetings — 3 · Greetings: tú and usted — deck "Greetings and courtesy"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
Hola works with anyone, at any hour. To sound warmer or more polite, add the greeting for the time of day.
 Morning, afternoon, evening
 English says "good morning". Spanish says "good days": buenos días . After about noon it is buenas tardes , and after dark buenas noches , which means both "good evening" and "good night".
 EX: Buenos días. | BWEH-nohs DEE-ahs | Good morning.
 EX: Buenas noches. | BWEH-nahs NOH-chehs | Good evening. Good night.
 Why buenos in one and buenas in the others? Días is a masculine word and tardes and noches are feminine, and the word for "good" changes to match. This is your first taste of agreement, which runs through all of Spanish.
CARDS NOW: Hola. = Hi. / Hello. [card] || Buenos días. = Good morning. [card] || Buenas tardes. = Good afternoon. [card] || Buenas noches. = Good evening. / Good night. [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 11)
Two ways to say "you"
 English has one "you". Spanish has two for one person. Use tú with friends, family, children and people your age in relaxed settings. Use usted with strangers, older people, customers and staff. The verb changes too: estás goes with tú , está with usted .

 What's your name? ; ¿Cómo te llamas? ; ¿Cómo se llama usted? ; 
 Excuse me. ; Disculpa. ; Disculpe. ; 
 EX: ¡Hola! ¿Cómo estás? | OH-lah KOH-moh ehs-TAHS | Hi! How are you?
 EX: Buenas tardes. ¿Cómo está usted? | BWEH-nahs TAHR-dehs KOH-moh ehs-TAH oos-TEHD | Good afternoon. How are you?
 EX: Bien, gracias. ¿Y tú? | byehn GRAH-syahs ee too | Fine, thanks. And you?
 When in doubt, start with usted . If the other person wants tú , they will switch, and you can follow.
 In the Dominican Republic, tú is the everyday form, and Dominicans are said to use it very widely, even with someone new of about their own age; watch what the people around you do. Keep usted for older people, for customers and staff, for the police, and for a stranger you stop to ask for help. You will also hear ¿Cómo tú estás? , with tú before the verb. It means the same as ¿Cómo estás? , and both are right.

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Names
 To give your name, say me llamo , literally "I call myself". It already contains "my name is", so nothing else goes in between. When you meet someone, say mucho gusto , "nice to meet you".
 EX: Me llamo Ana. | meh YAH-moh AH-nah | My name is Ana.
 EX: Mucho gusto. | MOO-choh GOOS-toh | Nice to meet you.
CARDS NOW: Mucho gusto. = Nice to meet you. [card] || Me llamo Ana. = My name is Ana.

### SECTION 3   (has 6)
Small polite words
 Por favor is "please", gracias is "thank you", and the answer is de nada , "you're welcome". To leave, say adiós or hasta luego , "see you later".
 EX: Gracias. De nada. | GRAH-syahs deh NAH-dah | Thank you. You're welcome.
 In Spain you will also hear vosotros for "you all". Latin America uses ustedes for any group, and so does this course.
 Most common mistake: one form of "good" for every greeting. Buenos tardes → buenas tardes . Only días takes buenos .

## LESSON do-greetings — 4 · ¿Qué lo que? Greetings and talk among friends — deck "Dominican greetings and slang"
### SECTION 0   (has 11)
In the greetings lesson you learned hola , buenos días and the difference between tú and usted . Among friends, Dominicans have their own openers and a handful of words you'll hear every day. This lesson is about what to say, and to whom.
 Saying hi to a friend
 English has "what's up?" and "how's it going?". Dominicans say ¿Qué lo que? to friends their age, or Dime a ver , literally "tell me, let's see". ¿Cómo tú estás? puts tú before the verb, and it is just as right as ¿Cómo estás? . A common answer is Tato , "all good", from está todo bien .
 EX: ¿Qué lo que? Tato. | keh loh keh TAH-toh | What's up? All good.
 EX: Dime a ver, ¿cómo tú estás? | DEE-meh ah behr KOH-moh too ehs-TAHS | Hey, how are you?
CARDS NOW: Hola. = Hi. / Hello. [card] || Buenos días. = Good morning. [card] || tú = you (one person, friendly) [card] || usted = you (one person, polite) [card] || ¿Qué lo que? = What's up? [card] || Dime a ver. = What's up? (tell me) [card] || ¿Cómo tú estás? = How are you? [card] || ¿Cómo estás? = How are you? [card] || ¡Tato! = All good! [card] || ¿Qué lo que? Tato. = What's up? All good. || Dime a ver, ¿cómo tú estás? = Hey, how are you?

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
Who gets what
 Slang is for friends your age. With your partner's grandmother, a neighbor you don't know or a clerk in a shop, use usted and a plain greeting. For a group, ustedes works with everyone, and its verb ends in -n.
 EX: Buenos días, ¿cómo está usted? | BWEH-nohs DEE-ahs KOH-moh ehs-TAH oos-TEHD | Good morning, how are you?
 EX: ¿Ustedes están bien? | oos-TEH-dehs ehs-TAHN byehn | Are you guys OK?
CARDS NOW: ustedes = you (more than one person) [card] || Buenos días, ¿cómo está usted? = Good morning, how are you? || ¿Ustedes están bien? = Are you guys OK?

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Friends: mi pana, mano, mana
 Your buddy is mi pana , and your buddies are mis panas . Close friends also call each other mano (bro) and mana (sis). All of these are informal.
 EX: Mis panas están aquí. | mees PAH-nahs ehs-TAHN ah-KEE | My buddies are here.
CARDS NOW: mi pana = my buddy [card] || Mis panas están aquí. = My buddies are here.

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
Cool, great, a pain
 ¡Qué jevi! means "how cool!", and nítido and chévere mean "great". Vaina is the all-purpose word for "thing" or "stuff": esa vaina is "that thing", and ¡Qué vaina! is "what a pain!". It is spelled with a v. You'll also hear "coño", a strong exclamation: understand it, but don't start with it.
 EX: ¿Qué vaina es esa? | keh BAI-nah ehs EH-sah | What's that thing?
CARDS NOW: ¡Qué jevi! = How cool! [card] || esa vaina = that thing [card] || ¿Qué vaina es esa? = What's that thing?

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Passing on what people say
 Dique means "supposedly" or "so they say", often with a raised eyebrow, the way English uses "supposedly" when you don't quite believe it.
 EX: Dique él viene hoy. | DEE-keh ehl BYEH-neh oy | Supposedly he's coming today.
 Slang with an elder is the classic slip. To your partner's grandmother: ¿Qué lo que? → Buenos días, ¿cómo está usted?
CARDS NOW: dique = supposedly; so they say [card] || Dique él viene hoy. = Supposedly he's coming today.

## LESSON gender — 5 · Gender and number of nouns — deck "People and family"
### SECTION 0   (has 9)
English has one word for "the" and treats every noun the same. In Spanish every noun has a gender: it is either masculine or feminine. That goes for things as well as people. A house is feminine, la casa ; a dog is masculine, el perro . The gender is part of the word, so always learn a noun with its el or la .
 -o and -a: the usual pattern
 Most nouns ending in -o are masculine and take el . Most nouns ending in -a are feminine and take la . For many people words, you just swap the ending.
 Masculine ; Feminine ; English ; 
 el hermano ; la hermana ; brother, sister ; 
 el hijo ; la hija ; son, daughter ; 
 el abuelo ; la abuela ; grandfather, grandmother ; 
 el amigo ; la amiga ; friend ; 
 EX: el hermano y la hermana | ehl ehr-MAH-noh ee lah ehr-MAH-nah | the brother and the sister
CARDS NOW: la casa = house; home [card] || la = the (feminine) [card] || el perro = the dog [card] || el = the (masculine) [card] || el hijo = the son [card] || la hija = daughter [card] || el amigo = friend (male, or a mixed group) [card] || la amiga = friend (female) [card] || el hermano y la hermana = the brother and the sister

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
Exceptions worth memorizing
 A few very common nouns break the pattern. Día , mapa and problema end in -a but are masculine. Mano (hand) ends in -o but is feminine.
 EX: el día | ehl DEE-ah | the day
 EX: el problema | ehl proh-BLEH-mah | the problem
 EX: la mano | lah MAH-noh | the hand
CARDS NOW: el día = the day [card] || el problema = the problem [card] || la mano = hand [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
Other endings
 Nouns ending in -e or a consonant can go either way: el padre , la madre , el hotel , la mujer . One helpful clue: nouns ending in -dad or -ción are feminine, like la ciudad (the city).
CARDS NOW: el padre = father; priest [card] || la madre = mother [card] || la mujer = woman; wife [card] || la ciudad = city [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
More than one
 To make a noun plural, add -s after a vowel and -es after a consonant. The article changes too: el becomes los , and la becomes las .
 One ; More than one ; 
 el amigo ; los amigos ; 
 la mano ; las manos ; 
 la ciudad ; las ciudades ; 
 EX: las ciudades | lahs syoo-DAH-dehs | the cities
CARDS NOW: los = the (masculine plural) [card] || las = the (feminine plural) [card] || las ciudades = the cities

### SECTION 4   (has 3)
Mixed groups
 For a group of men and women together, Spanish uses the masculine plural. So los hermanos can mean "the brothers" or "the brothers and sisters", and los padres means "the parents". Careful: los parientes means "the relatives", not "the parents".
 EX: los hermanos | lohs ehr-MAH-nohs | the brothers; the siblings
 EX: los padres | lohs PAH-drehs | the parents
 Most common mistake: trusting the ending every time. la problema → el problema , and el mano → la mano .
CARDS NOW: los hermanos = the brothers; the siblings || los padres = parents [card] || los parientes = relatives [card]

## LESSON ser — 7 · Subject pronouns and ser — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 12)
English "to be" has three forms: am, are, is . Spanish has two verbs for "to be". This lesson covers the first, ser , which you use to say who someone is, where they are from and what they do.
 Subject pronouns and ser
 The subject is who the sentence is about. Each subject has its own form of the verb.
 Subject ; ser ; English ; 
 yo ; soy ; I am ; 
 tú ; eres ; you are (a friend) ; 
 él, ella, usted ; es ; he is, she is, you are (a stranger) ; 
 nosotros, nosotras ; somos ; we are ; 
 ellos, ellas, ustedes ; son ; they are, you all are ; 
 Two things to notice. Usted takes the same form as él , and ustedes the same form as ellos . And nosotras and ellas are for groups of women only; any mixed group is nosotros or ellos .
 EX: Ella es Ana. | EH-yah ehs AH-nah | This is Ana. She's Ana.
 EX: Usted es de Santiago, ¿no? | oos-TEHD ehs deh sahn-TYAH-goh noh | You're from Santiago, right?
CARDS NOW: ser = to be (what something is) [card] || yo = I [card] || tú = you (one person, friendly) [card] || él = he; him (after a preposition) [card] || usted = you (one person, polite) [card] || nosotros = we (men or a mixed group) [card] || nosotras = we (only women) [card] || ellos = they (men or a mixed group) [card] || ellas = they (only women) [card] || ustedes = you (more than one person) [card] || Ella es Ana. = This is Ana. She's Ana. || Usted es de Santiago, ¿no? = You're from Santiago, right?

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
With or without the pronoun
 English always needs "I" or "you". In Spanish the verb ending already tells you who, so soy alone means "I am", and Soy de Chicago is a full sentence. Dominicans also say the pronoun a lot, much more than speakers in Mexico: yo soy , tú eres and nosotros somos are normal everyday Spanish, not heavy. Both ways are right. A pronoun is most useful for contrast or to make clear who is meant.
 EX: Soy de Chicago. | soy deh chee-KAH-goh | I'm from Chicago.
 EX: Nosotros somos de Santo Domingo. | noh-SOH-trohs SOH-mohs deh SAHN-toh doh-MEEN-goh | We're from Santo Domingo.
CARDS NOW: Soy de Chicago. = I'm from Chicago. [card] || Nosotros somos de Santo Domingo. = We're from Santo Domingo.

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Where you are from: ser de
 Origin always uses ser de , "to be from". To ask a friend, say ¿De dónde eres? ; to ask a stranger, ¿De dónde es usted?

## LESSON muy-mucho — 23 · muy and mucho: very cold is mucho frío — deck "Traps for English speakers"
### SECTION 0   (has 6)
English uses "very" before describing words, and "a lot (of)" or "much" with nouns and verbs. Spanish splits the same way, with muy and mucho . The trouble is that some things English says with "very" are nouns in Spanish.
 muy: very, before a describing word

 EX: La casa es muy grande. | lah KAH-sah ehs mwee GRAHN-deh | The house is very big.

## LESSON connectors — 24 · y, o, pero, porque — deck "Questions and connectors"
### SECTION 0   (has 9)
A few small words turn short sentences into real conversation. Four of them do most of the work: y (and), o (or), pero (but) and porque (because).
 y and o, and how they change
 y means "and" and o means "or", just as in English. When y joins two people, the verb takes the ellos form, because together they are "they". In a list, y goes only before the last item, as in English: arroz, habichuelas y carne .
 EX: Ana y Carlos son de Santiago. | AH-nah ee KAHR-lohs sohn deh sahn-TYAH-goh | Ana and Carlos are from Santiago.
 EX: ¿Quieres café o té? | KYEH-rehs kah-FEH oh teh | Do you want coffee or tea?
 One twist English doesn't have: before a word that starts with an "ee" sound (spelled i- or hi- ), y becomes e . Before a word that starts with an "oh" sound ( o- or ho- ), o becomes u . This keeps the two sounds from running together.

## LESSON preterite — 29 · The preterite: what you did — deck "Likes and free time"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
English has one simple past: "I worked, I ate, I went." Spanish has more than one past, and the preterite is the one for finished actions: what you did yesterday, last night or last weekend.
 Regular endings
 Take off -ar , -er or -ir and add these endings. The verb ending matches the subject, as in the present. -er and -ir verbs share one set.
 Who ; hablar ; comer ; vivir ;

 tú ; hablaste ; comiste ; viviste ; 
 él, ella, usted ; habló ; comió ; vivió ;

 ellos, ellas, ustedes ; hablaron ; comieron ; vivieron ; 
 The nosotros form of -ar and -ir verbs looks just like the present. A time word such as ayer shows you it's the past.
 EX: Anoche comí pica pollo con tostones. | ah-NOH-cheh koh-MEE PEE-kah POH-yoh kohn tohs-TOH-nehs | Last night I had fried chicken with tostones.

CARDS NOW: Hablo con mi jefa todos los días. = I talk to my boss every day. || Ayer mi jefa habló conmigo. = Yesterday my boss talked to me.
