## LESSON greetings — 8 · Greetings and goodbyes — deck "Greetings and courtesy"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
The phrases in this lesson work with anyone, anywhere in the Arab world: Modern Standard Arabic and the everyday dialects share them.
 Hello
 السَّلَامُ عَلَيْكُم as-salāmu ʿalaykum means "peace be upon you". Use it with anyone, at any time, when you arrive. The reply turns it around: وَعَلَيْكُم السَّلَام waʿalaykum as-salām , "and upon you peace". مَرْحَبًا marḥaban is a neutral "hi", and أَهْلًا وَسَهْلًا ahlan wasahlan is "welcome", said by a host or shopkeeper.
 EX: السَّلَامُ عَلَيْكُم | as-salāmu ʿalaykum | Hello. (peace be upon you)
 EX: وَعَلَيْكُم السَّلَام | waʿalaykum as-salām | Hello. (the reply)
 EX: أَهْلًا وَسَهْلًا | ahlan wasahlan | Welcome.
CARDS NOW: السَّلَامُ عَلَيْكُم = hello (peace be upon you) [card] || وَعَلَيْكُم السَّلَام = hello (the reply to as-salāmu ʿalaykum) [card] || مَرْحَبًا = hi [card] || أَهْلًا وَسَهْلًا = welcome [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 4)
Morning and evening
 English says "good morning". Arabic says "morning of the good": صَبَاح الْخَيْر ṣabāḥ al-khayr . The usual reply is صَبَاح النُّور ṣabāḥ an-nūr , "morning of light". From the afternoon on, use مَسَاء الْخَيْر and answer مَسَاء النُّور. Look at the article: the first word never takes ال, the second always does. This "X of the Y" shape is called iḍāfa, and you'll see it everywhere.
 EX: صَبَاح الْخَيْر | ṣabāḥ al-khayr | Good morning.
 EX: مَسَاء النُّور | masāʾ an-nūr | Good evening. (the reply)
CARDS NOW: صَبَاح الْخَيْر = good morning [card] || صَبَاح النُّور = good morning (the reply) [card] || مَسَاء الْخَيْر = good evening [card] || مَسَاء النُّور = good evening (the reply) [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
How are you?
 English has one "you"; Arabic has a form for a man and a form for a woman. To a man: كَيْفَ حَالُكَ؟ kayfa ḥāluka? To a woman: كَيْفَ حَالُكِ؟ kayfa ḥāluki? Only the last vowel changes. The answer is بِخَيْر bikhayr , "fine", often with الْحَمْدُ لِلّٰه al-ḥamdu lillāh , "thank God", which Muslims and Christians alike say every day.
 EX: كَيْفَ حَالُكِ؟ | kayfa ḥāluki? | How are you? (to a woman)
 EX: بِخَيْر، الْحَمْدُ لِلّٰه | bikhayr, al-ḥamdu lillāh | Fine, thank God.
CARDS NOW: كَيْفَ حَالُكَ؟ = How are you? (to a man) [card] || كَيْفَ حَالُكِ؟ = How are you? (to a woman) [card] || الْحَمْدُ لِلّٰه = fine, thank God [card] || بِخَيْر، الْحَمْدُ لِلّٰه = Fine, thank God.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Thanks, sorry, goodbye
 شُكْرًا shukran is "thanks"; the reply عَفْوًا ʿafwan is "you're welcome" (it also means "pardon?"). "Sorry" agrees with the speaker: a man says آسِف āsif , a woman says آسِفَة āsifa . To leave, say مَعَ السَّلَامَة maʿa as-salāma , "with safety".
 EX: أَنَا آسِفَة | anā āsifa | I'm sorry. (a woman speaking)
 EX: مَعَ السَّلَامَة | maʿa as-salāma | Goodbye.
CARDS NOW: مَعَ السَّلَامَة = goodbye [card] || أَنَا آسِفَة = I'm sorry (a woman speaking) [card]

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
What people really say
 You are learning the Arabic everyone reads and everyone understands. People chat in their own dialect; you'll hear different words in Cairo and in Amman. The Spoken Arabic deck shows you the most common ones. As always, read the Arabic first and check the romanisation after.
 Most common mistake: translating "I'm good" word for word. أَنَا جَيِّد for "I'm fine" → بِخَيْر: that is what people answer.
CARDS NOW: (none)

DECK CARDS IN NO SECTION (add by front, as a string, only where a section teaches them): شُكْرًا جَزِيلًا | shukran jazīlan | thank you very much || مِن فَضْلِكَ | min faḍlika | please (to a man) || مِن فَضْلِكِ | min faḍliki | please (to a woman) || عُذْرًا | ʿudhran | sorry; excuse me || تَشَرَّفْنَا | tasharrafnā | nice to meet you || إِلَى اللِّقَاء | ilā al-liqāʾ | see you later

## LESSON gender — 9 · Masculine and feminine: ة — deck "Family and people"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
In Arabic every noun is masculine or feminine: not only people, but a car, a school, a door. English does this only with "he" and "she". Arabic does it with "it", with "this", and with every describing word.
 The sign: ة
 Most feminine nouns end in ة, the taa marbuta: سَيَّارَة sayyāra "car", مَدْرَسَة madrasa "school". Nouns without it are usually masculine: بَيْت, كِتَاب, بَاب. When you meet a new noun, check its last letter first: it tells you which "this", which "it" and which form of every describing word to use. Write ة with its two dots; without them, ه is a different letter, a plain h.
 EX: هٰذِهِ سَيَّارَة | hādhihi sayyāra | This is a car.
 EX: هٰذَا بَيْت | hādhā bayt | This is a house.
CARDS NOW: هٰذِهِ سَيَّارَة = This is a car. || هٰذَا بَيْت = This is a house.

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
People
 Arabic always has to choose a gender for a person, so the course cues it in English: "student (a woman)". Many words for people add ة for a woman: طَالِب ṭālib is a man student, طَالِبَة ṭāliba a woman student; مُدَرِّس mudarris and مُدَرِّسَة mudarrisa work the same way.
 EX: هٰذَا طَالِب | hādhā ṭālib | This is a student. (a man)
 EX: هٰذِهِ مُدَرِّسَة | hādhihi mudarrisa | This is a teacher. (a woman)
CARDS NOW: هٰذَا طَالِب = This is a student. (a man) || هٰذِهِ مُدَرِّسَة = This is a teacher. (a woman)

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
Feminine without ة
 A few words are feminine because of what they mean, with no ة: أُمّ umm "mother", بِنْت bint "girl", أُخْت ukht "sister". Their partners are masculine: أَب ab "father", وَلَد walad "boy", أَخ akh "brother". For "man" and "woman" Arabic uses two different words: رَجُل rajul and اِمْرَأَة imraʾa .
 EX: هٰذَا رَجُل | hādhā rajul | This is a man.
 EX: هٰذِهِ اِمْرَأَة | hādhihi imraʾa | This is a woman.
CARDS NOW: رَجُل = man [card] || اِمْرَأَة = woman [card] || هٰذَا رَجُل = This is a man. || هٰذِهِ اِمْرَأَة = This is a woman.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
"It" is he or she
 Arabic has no separate "it". A masculine thing is هُوَ huwa , a feminine thing is هِيَ hiya . Talking about a book, "it" is هُوَ; talking about a car, "It's new" is literally "she is new":
 EX: هِيَ جَدِيدَة | hiya jadīda | It's new. (the car)
CARDS NOW: هِيَ جَدِيدَة = It's new. (the car)

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Describing words agree
 A describing word comes after its noun and agrees with it: it adds ة after a feminine noun. Lesson 17 covers this in detail.
 EX: بِنْت صَغِيرَة | bint ṣaghīra | a small girl
 Most common mistake: using the masculine for everything, as English has no gender. هٰذَا سَيَّارَة → هٰذِهِ سَيَّارَة: a feminine noun needs the feminine "this".
CARDS NOW: بِنْت صَغِيرَة = a small girl

DECK CARDS IN NO SECTION (add by front, as a string, only where a section teaches them): أُمِّي | ummī | my mother || أَبِي | abī | my father || أَخِي | akhī | my brother || أُخْتِي | ukhtī | my sister || اِبْنِي | ibnī | my son || اِبْنَتِي | ibnatī | my daughter || زَوْجِي | zawjī | my husband || زَوْجَتِي | zawjatī | my wife || جَدِّي | jaddī | my grandfather || جَدَّتِي | jaddatī | my grandmother || صَدِيقِي | ṣadīqī | my friend (a man) || صَدِيقَتِي | ṣadīqatī | my friend (a woman) || عَائِلَتِي كَبِيرَة | ʿāʾilatī kabīra | My family is big. || أُمِّي مُدَرِّسَة | ummī mudarrisa | My mother is a teacher. || عِنْدِي أَخ وَأُخْت | ʿindī akh waʾukht | I have a brother and a sister. || هٰذَا صَدِيقِي أَحْمَد | hādhā ṣadīqī aḥmad | This is my friend Ahmad.

## LESSON pronouns — 10 · I, you, he, she — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 7)
Arabic pronouns are short and you'll use them constantly. Two things are new for English speakers: "you" changes for a man or a woman, and there is no "it".
 English ; Arabic ; 
 I ; أَنَا ; 
 you (to a man) ; أَنْتَ ; 
 you (to a woman) ; أَنْتِ ; 
 he, it ; هُوَ ; 
 she, it ; هِيَ ; 
 we ; نَحْنُ ; 
 they ; هُم ; 
 You'll also see أَنْتُم antum "you" to a group and هُنَّ hunna "they" for women only; you only need to recognize them.
 No "am", "is" or "are"
 Put the pronoun next to the word that describes it, and you have a sentence. English needs "am", "is" or "are" in every sentence; Arabic doesn't, in the present. Lesson 11 says more about this.
CARDS NOW: أَنَا = I [card] || أَنْتَ = you (to a man) [card] || أَنْتِ = you (to a woman) [card] || هُوَ = he; it (masculine thing) [card] || هِيَ = she; it (feminine thing) [card] || نَحْنُ = we [card] || هُم = they (people) [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
"I" agrees with the speaker
 English "I'm a student" is the same for everyone. In Arabic the word after أَنَا anā agrees with whoever is speaking: a man says طَالِب, a woman says طَالِبَة.
 EX: أَنَا طَالِب | anā ṭālib | I'm a student. (a man speaking)
 EX: أَنَا طَالِبَة | anā ṭāliba | I'm a student. (a woman speaking)
CARDS NOW: أَنَا طَالِب = I'm a student (a man speaking). [card] || أَنَا طَالِبَة = I'm a student. (a woman speaking)

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Two "you"s
 To a man, "you" is أَنْتَ anta ; to a woman, أَنْتِ anti . In writing they differ by one vowel mark, and the describing word after them agrees too. This course always tells you who you're talking to: "(to a man)" or "(to a woman)".
 EX: أَنْتَ مُدَرِّس | anta mudarris | You're a teacher. (to a man)
 EX: أَنْتِ مُدَرِّسَة | anti mudarrisa | You're a teacher. (to a woman)
CARDS NOW: أَنْتَ مُدَرِّس = You're a teacher. (to a man) || أَنْتِ مُدَرِّسَة = You're a teacher. (to a woman)

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
He, she and it
 هُوَ huwa is "he", and also "it" for a masculine thing; هِيَ hiya is "she", and "it" for a feminine thing. The word for "engineer" works like "student": مُهَنْدِس for a man, مُهَنْدِسَة for a woman.
 EX: هُوَ مُهَنْدِس | huwa muhandis | He's an engineer.
 EX: هِيَ مُهَنْدِسَة | hiya muhandisa | She's an engineer.
CARDS NOW: هُوَ مُهَنْدِس = He's an engineer. || هِيَ مُهَنْدِسَة = She's an engineer.

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
We and they
 نَحْنُ naḥnu is "we", هُم hum is "they". With مِن min "from" you can already say where people come from.
 EX: نَحْنُ مِن شِيكَاغُو | naḥnu min shīkāghū | We're from Chicago.
 EX: هُم مِن تِكْسَاس | hum min tiksās | They're from Texas.
 Read the Arabic first and check the romanisation after.
 Most common mistake: using أَنْتَ for everyone. أَنْتَ طَالِبَة to a woman → أَنْتِ طَالِبَة: to a woman, "you" ends in kasra.
CARDS NOW: نَحْنُ مِن شِيكَاغُو = We're from Chicago. || هُم مِن تِكْسَاس = They're from Texas.

(to a woman) || اِسْمِي سَارَة | ismī sāra | My name is Sara. || مِن أَيْنَ أَنْتَ؟ | min ayna anta? | Where are you from? (to a man) || مِن أَيْنَ أَنْتِ؟ | min ayna anti? | Where are you from? ## LESSON nominal — 11 · Sentences with no "is" — deck "Describing things"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
English needs a verb in every sentence: "I am a student", "The house is big". Arabic does not, as long as you are talking about now. You put the two parts side by side, and the gap between them means "is", "am" or "are".
 Two parts, nothing between
 The first part is the subject : what the sentence is about. The second is the predicate : what you say about it. A pronoun such as أَنَا anā (I) or هِيَ hiya (she) can be the subject, and so can a noun with "the", such as الْبَيْت al-bayt (the house).
 EX: أَنَا طَالِب | anā ṭālib | I'm a student. (a man speaking)
 EX: أَنَا طَالِبَة | anā ṭāliba | I'm a student. (a woman speaking)
 EX: الْبَيْت كَبِير | al-bayt kabīr | The house is big.
CARDS NOW: أَنَا طَالِب = I'm a student (a man speaking). [card] || أَنَا طَالِبَة = I'm a student. (a woman speaking) || الْبَيْت كَبِير = The house is big. || كَبِير = big [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
The predicate agrees
 An adjective in the predicate takes the gender of the subject. After a feminine subject it adds ة: السَّيَّارَة as-sayyāra (the car) is feminine, so "new" becomes جَدِيدَة jadīda . "I" agrees with whoever is speaking, as you saw above.
 EX: السَّيَّارَة جَدِيدَة | as-sayyāra jadīda | The car is new.
 EX: هِيَ مَشْغُولَة | hiya mashghūla | She's busy.
CARDS NOW: السَّيَّارَة جَدِيدَة = The car is new. || هِيَ مَشْغُولَة = She's busy. [card]

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Places work the same way
 "Where" sentences have no verb either. فِي fī means "in" or "at", and it follows the subject directly.
 EX: هِيَ فِي الْبَيْت | hiya fī al-bayt | She's at home.
 EX: الْمُدَرِّس فِي الْمَدْرَسَة | al-mudarris fī al-madrasa | The teacher is at school.
CARDS NOW: هِيَ فِي الْبَيْت = She's at home. || الْمُدَرِّس فِي الْمَدْرَسَة = The teacher is at school.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Asking
 To ask, raise your voice at the end, or put هَل hal in front. Nothing else moves: no "is" to swap, as English does with "Is the house big?". Lesson 14 shows the other question words.
 EX: هَل أَنْتَ طَالِب؟ | hal anta ṭālib? | Are you a student? (to a man)
CARDS NOW: هَل أَنْتَ طَالِب؟ = Are you a student? (to a man)

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
Watch the article
 The predicate has no ال. الْبَيْت كَبِير al-bayt kabīr is a sentence: "The house is big." Give كَبِير the article too and it stops being a sentence: الْبَيْت الْكَبِير al-bayt al-kabīr is just "the big house", waiting for more. Lesson 12 is all about this difference.
 Arabic does have verbs for "was" and "will be"; you'll meet them later. For now, and for anything true at this moment, leave the gap empty.
 Most common mistake: putting هُوَ in as "is". الْبَيْت هُوَ كَبِير → الْبَيْت كَبِير. The same goes for "am": أَنَا أَكُون طَالِب → أَنَا طَالِب.
CARDS NOW: (none)

DECK CARDS IN NO SECTION (add by front, as a string, only where a section teaches them): صَغِيرَة | ṣaghīra | small (feminine) || جَمِيل جِدًّا | jamīl jiddan | very beautiful || قَدِيم | qadīm | old (things) || رَخِيص | rakhīṣ | cheap || جَدِيد | jadīd | new || طَوِيل | ṭawīl | long; tall || قَصِيرَة | qaṣīra | short (feminine) || سَهْل | sahl | easy || صَعْبَة | ṣaʿba | difficult (feminine) || حَارّ | ḥārr | hot || بَارِد | bārid | cold || الْجَوّ جَمِيل | al-jaww jamīl | The weather is nice. || مَدِينَة جَمِيلَة | madīna jamīla | a beautiful city || الْمَدِينَة الْجَمِيلَة | al-madīna al-jamīla | the beautiful city || الْعَرَبِيَّة لَيْسَت صَعْبَة | al-ʿarabiyya laysat ṣaʿba | Arabic isn't hard.

## LESSON definite — 12 · "The" and "a": ال and no ال — deck "Home and things"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
English has two little words, "a" and "the". Arabic has only one: the article ال, "the". For "a", Arabic uses nothing at all. This sounds easy, but it is the mistake English speakers make most often in their first two years, so this lesson takes it slowly.
 No word for "a"
 A noun on its own is indefinite : غُرْفَة ghurfa is "a room". Add ال and it becomes definite : الْغُرْفَة al-ghurfa , "the room". English speakers often reach for the word for "one", وَاحِد wāḥid , to say "a". Don't: وَاحِد سَيَّارَة is not Arabic. The bare noun already says "a".
 EX: غُرْفَة | ghurfa | a room
 EX: الْغُرْفَة | al-ghurfa | the room
CARDS NOW: غُرْفَة = room [card] || الْغُرْفَة = the room

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Three things make a noun definite
 The article ال: الْغُرْفَة, the room.
 A name: سَارَة sāra or شِيكَاغُو shīkāghū never takes ال. A few place names have ال built in, such as الْقَاهِرَة al-qāhira (Cairo); keep it.
 A possessive ending ("my", "your": lesson 19). The ending already means "the", so it never takes ال as well.
 EX: سَارَة فِي الْقَاهِرَة | sāra fī al-qāhira | Sara is in Cairo.
CARDS NOW: سَارَة فِي الْقَاهِرَة = Sara is in Cairo.

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Phrase or sentence: one ال decides

## LESSON nisba — 13 · Where you're from: -ī and -iyya — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
English makes nationality words in many ways: American, Lebanese, Moroccan, French. Arabic has one way. Take the country and add -ī for a man, -iyya for a woman, as in أَمْرِيكِيّ and أَمْرِيكِيَّة. This ending is called the nisba , and it works for countries, cities and many other words.
 Country → nisba

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)

## LESSON questions — 14 · Question words: ما, من, أين, هل — deck "Spoken Arabic"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
English questions move words around: "The school is near" becomes "Is the school near?" and "Where is the school?". Arabic moves nothing. Put the question word at the front, keep the rest of the sentence as it is, and raise your voice at the end.
 Yes or no: هَل
 Put هَل hal in front of any sentence. The sentence itself does not change: still no "is", still no ال on the predicate. هَل has no meaning of its own; it works like a spoken question mark. In speech you can also skip it and just let your voice rise. Answer with نَعَم naʿam (yes) or لَا lā (no).
 EX: هَل الْبَيْت كَبِير؟ | hal al-bayt kabīr? | Is the house big?

## LESSON idafa — 18 · Iḍāfa: the X of Y — deck "Travel and help"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
English joins two nouns in two ways: "the student's book" and "the door of the house". Arabic has one way, the iḍāfa : put the two nouns side by side, the owned thing first and the owner second, with an unspoken "of" between them. "The student's book" is "book the-student".
 The first word never takes ال
 In كِتَاب الطَّالِب kitāb aṭ-ṭālib , the article on the second word makes the whole phrase definite: " the student's book". So the first noun never adds its own ال. Compare an adjective, where both words take it: الْكِتَاب الْجَدِيد al-kitāb al-jadīd , "the new book".
 EX: كِتَاب الطَّالِب | kitāb aṭ-ṭālib | the student's book

## LESSON plurals — 21 · Plurals: sound and broken — deck "Food and drink"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
English makes almost every plural with -s. Arabic has three ways, and which one a noun uses belongs to the word, so learn each plural together with its singular.
 Groups of men: add -ūn
 Many words for people simply add -ūn : مُدَرِّس mudarris "teacher" becomes مُدَرِّسُون mudarrisūn "teachers". The word stays whole and only an ending is added, so this is called a sound plural. A group of men, or of men and women together, takes -ūn. In everyday speech you will often hear -īn on the same words; this course writes -ūn.
 EX: الْمُدَرِّسُون فِي الْمَدْرَسَة | al-mudarrisūn fī al-madrasa | The teachers are at the school.
 EX: الْمُهَنْدِسُون هُنَا | al-muhandisūn hunā | The engineers are here.
CARDS NOW: الْمُدَرِّسُون فِي الْمَدْرَسَة = The teachers are at the school. || الْمُهَنْدِسُون هُنَا = The engineers are here.

 EX: الْمُدَرِّسَات فِي الْمَكْتَب | al-mudarrisāt fī al-maktab | The teachers (women) are in the office.

## LESSON roots — 22 · Roots and patterns — deck "Daily routine"
### SECTION 0   (has 6)
English builds word families with prefixes and suffixes: write, writer, rewrite. Arabic builds them from a root: usually three consonants that carry a basic idea. Vowels and a few extra letters are put around those consonants in fixed shapes called patterns, and each pattern adds its own meaning.
 One root, many words
 The root ك ت ب (k-t-b) is about writing. Look for those three letters, in that order, in every word below.

Patterns are shapes with a meaning
 Grammar books write patterns with the letters ف ع ل standing for any root. The shape مَفْعَل mafʿal , ma- in front, often names a place: مَكْتَب is "the place of writing", and مَطْعَم maṭʿam , from طَعَام ṭaʿām "food", is a restaurant. مَطْبَخ maṭbakh , the kitchen, is the place of cooking. With ة at the end the shape works the same way: مَدْرَسَة, مَكْتَبَة.
 The shape فَاعِل fāʿil , with a long ā after the first letter, often names the person who does something: كَاتِب "writer", طَالِب ṭālib "student", literally "one who seeks".
 EX: الْمَطْعَم قَرِيب | al-maṭʿam qarīb | The restaurant is close.

CARDS NOW: الْمَطْعَم قَرِيب = The restaurant is close. || الطَّعَام فِي الْمَطْبَخ = The food is in the kitchen.

### SECTION 0   (has 12)
English verbs barely change: I study, she studies. An Arabic present-tense verb always shows who is doing it, with a prefix at the front and, for two persons, an ending. Here is one verb, "study", for every person you know.

## LESSON verb-sentences — 24 · Verb sentences and word order — deck "Daily routine"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
English sentences start with the subject: "Sara studies Arabic." Arabic can do that too, but it can also start with the verb: تَدْرُس سَارَة الْعَرَبِيَّة tadrus sāra al-ʿarabiyya , word for word "studies Sara Arabic". A sentence that starts with its verb is called a verb sentence. Both orders are normal statements; news and stories start with the verb very often, and conversation often starts with the person.
 EX: تَدْرُس سَارَة الْعَرَبِيَّة | tadrus sāra al-ʿarabiyya | Sara studies Arabic.
 EX: سَارَة تَدْرُس الْعَرَبِيَّة | sāra tadrus al-ʿarabiyya | Sara studies Arabic.
 Verb first is not a question
 In English, putting the verb first turns a sentence into a question: "Does Sara…?", "Is she…?". In Arabic it doesn't. A yes or no question starts with هَل, whatever order follows.
 EX: يَشْرَب أَحْمَد الشَّاي | yashrab aḥmad ash-shāy | Ahmad drinks tea.
 EX: هَل تَدْرُس سَارَة الْعَرَبِيَّة؟ | hal tadrus sāra al-ʿarabiyya? | Does Sara study Arabic?

The verb still agrees
 The prefix agrees with the subject wherever the subject stands. A woman's name or a feminine noun takes the same verb form as هِيَ; a man's name takes the form of هُوَ. So Maryam gets تَسْكُن taskun , and Omar gets يُحِبّ yuḥibb .
 EX: تَسْكُن مَرْيَم فِي عَمَّان | taskun maryam fī ʿammān | Maryam lives in Amman.
 EX: يُحِبّ عُمَر الْقَهْوَة الْعَرَبِيَّة | yuḥibb ʿumar al-qahwa al-ʿarabiyya | Omar likes Arabic coffee.
CARDS NOW: تَسْكُن مَرْيَم فِي عَمَّان = Maryam lives in Amman. || يُحِبّ عُمَر الْقَهْوَة الْعَرَبِيَّة = Omar likes Arabic coffee.

## LESSON negation — 25 · Saying no: لا and ليس — deck "Traps for English speakers"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
English has one "not" and puts it after a helper verb: "I do not know", "she is not busy". Arabic has two words for "not", and which one you use depends on what comes next: a verb, or a sentence with no verb at all. Ask yourself one question: is there a verb to put it in front of?
 لَا before a present verb
 Put لَا lā straight in front of a present-tense verb. Nothing else changes: there is no "do" to add and no new verb form, just لَا plus the verb you already know. The same word also means "no" as an answer, and نَعَم naʿam means "yes".
 EX: لَا أَعْرِف | lā aʿrif | I don't know.
 EX: لَا أَتَكَلَّم الْفَرَنْسِيَّة | lā atakallam al-faransiyya | I don't speak French.
 EX: هِيَ لَا تَشْرَب الْقَهْوَة | hiya lā tashrab al-qahwa | She doesn't drink coffee.
 EX: لَا شُكْرًا | lā shukran | No, thank you.
CARDS NOW: لَا أَعْرِف = I don't know [card] || لَا أَتَكَلَّم الْفَرَنْسِيَّة = I don't speak French [card] || هِيَ لَا تَشْرَب الْقَهْوَة = She doesn't drink coffee. || لَا شُكْرًا = no, thank you [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
لَيْسَ in a sentence with no verb
 A sentence with no "is", like هُوَ هُنَا "he is here", has no verb for لَا to stand in front of. Use لَيْسَ laysa , "is not", instead. It agrees with its subject: لَيْسَت laysat for "she" or a feminine noun, لَيْسَ for "he" or a masculine noun.
 EX: هُوَ لَيْسَ هُنَا | huwa laysa hunā | He's not here.
 EX: الْغُرْفَة لَيْسَت نَظِيفَة | al-ghurfa laysat naẓīfa | The room isn't clean.
 You already know one more use from the lesson on عِنْدَ: لَيْسَ عِنْدِي, "I don't have".
 EX: لَيْسَ عِنْدِي وَقْت | laysa ʿindī waqt | I don't have time.
CARDS NOW: هُوَ لَيْسَ هُنَا = He's not here. [card] || الْغُرْفَة لَيْسَت نَظِيفَة = The room isn't clean. [card] || لَيْسَ عِنْدِي وَقْت = I don't have time [card]

### SECTION 0   (has 11)
Time words work much as in English: they usually go at the start or at the end of the sentence, and putting one first gives it a little more weight. Most of them you learn as fixed words.
 Arabic has no special form for "I'm doing it right now". The present-tense verb covers both "I drink" and "I'm drinking", and the time word tells you which: أَشْرَب الشَّاي الْآن is "I'm drinking tea now", while أَشْرَب الشَّاي كُلّ يَوْم is "I drink tea every day".

## LESSON past — 27 · Past-tense verbs — deck "Time and days"
### SECTION 0   (has 10)
English marks the past with -ed or a new form: studied, went, ate. Arabic changes the verb too, but in its own way. The present-tense verb shows who with a prefix at the front; the past-tense verb shows who with an ending at the back, and it has no prefix at all.
 One verb, every person
 The basic past form is the "he" form, دَرَسَ darasa "he studied". Dictionaries and teachers use it as the name of the verb. The other persons add an ending to it.

## LESSON directions — 28 · Asking for help and directions — deck "Around town and directions"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
Asking the way puts together almost everything you know: "excuse me" with the right "you", a question word, places with the article, and adjectives that agree. You don't need long sentences. A polite opener, the question word and the place are enough, and most answers are a few short words plus a pointing hand.
 Stopping someone
 English "excuse me" fits everyone. Arabic changes with the person you speak to: لَوْ سَمَحْتَ law samaḥta to a man, لَوْ سَمَحْتِ law samaḥti to a woman. Only the last vowel differs, so look at the person and choose before you speak. The phrase also works as "please".
 EX: لَوْ سَمَحْتَ، أَيْنَ الْمَتْحَف؟ | law samaḥta, ayna al-matḥaf? | Excuse me, where's the museum? (to a man)

Where is it?
 Start with أَيْنَ ayna "where", then the place, with no "is". A place you mean, like "the bank" or "the station", takes the article. As in every iḍāfa, the ة of the first word is said -at .

 عَلَى طُول ; straight ahead ; 
 هُنَا ; here ; 
 هُنَاكَ ; over there ; 
 قَرِيب مِن هُنَا ; near here ; 
 بَعِيد عَن هُنَا ; far from here ; 
 Right and left take the article: عَلَى الْيَمِين ʿalā al-yamīn . Near and far are adjectives, so they agree with the place: a masculine place is قَرِيب, a feminine place ending in ة is قَرِيبَة qarība .
 EX: الْبَنْك عَلَى الْيَسَار | al-bank ʿalā al-yasār | The bank is on the left.
 EX: الْفُنْدُق قَرِيب مِن هُنَا | al-funduq qarīb min hunā | The hotel is near here.
 EX: الْمَحَطَّة بَعِيدَة | al-maḥaṭṭa baʿīda | The station is far.
 EX: الصَّيْدَلِيَّة هُنَاكَ، عَلَى الْيَمِين | aṣ-ṣaydaliyya hunāka, ʿalā al-yamīn | The pharmacy is over there, on the right.

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
When you don't follow
 Ask whether the person speaks English, with the right "you", or say that you don't understand. You may also hear people ask you to repeat with مِن فَضْلِكَ أَعِد, "please repeat"; you only need to recognize it. Asking people to speak slowly or to repeat is perfectly polite, and a smile and شُكْرًا at the end go a long way.
 EX: هَل تَتَكَلَّمِين الْإِنْجِلِيزِيَّة؟ | hal tatakallamīn al-injilīziyya? | Do you speak English? (to a woman)
 EX: لَا أَفْهَم | lā afham | I don't understand.
 EX: مِن فَضْلِكَ أَعِد | min faḍlika aʿid | Please repeat. (to a man)
 Most common mistake: putting the article on both nouns of a place name, as English "the station" suggests. أَيْنَ الْمَحَطَّة الْقِطَار؟ → أَيْنَ مَحَطَّةْ الْقِطَار؟ .
