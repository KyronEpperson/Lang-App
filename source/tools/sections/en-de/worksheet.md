# Worksheet: en-de

## LESSON sounds — 1 · Sounds, spelling and the alphabet — deck "Sounds and spelling"
### SECTION 0   (has 7)
German uses the same 26 letters as English, plus ä, ö, ü and ß . Its spelling is far more regular than English: once you know a handful of rules, you can read almost any word. Under every German phrase in this course there is a pronunciation line in English letters. The syllable in CAPITALS is the stressed one.
 Letters that sound different
 The letters are the ones you know, but several stand for a different sound. These are the ones English speakers misread most.
 Letter ; Sounds like ; Example ; 
 w ; English v ; das Wasser ; 
 v ; English f ; der Vater ; 
 z ; ts, as in cats ; die Zeit ; 
 j ; y, as in yes ; ja ; 
 s before a vowel ; z ; die Sonne ; 
 sp, st at the start ; shp, sht ; die Straße ; 
 ß ; s ; die Straße ; 
 EX: das Wasser | dahs VAHS-uh | the water
 EX: die Straße | dee SHTRAH-suh | the street
 ß is used after a long vowel or after ei, au, eu , as in Straße and heißen . Swiss German writes ss instead.
CARDS NOW: das Wasser = water [card] || der Vater = father [card] || die Zeit = time [card] || ja = yes [card] || die Sonne = sun [card] || die Straße = street [card] || heißen = to be called [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
ei and ie
 ei sounds like English eye (the line writes ai ), and ie sounds like ee . Read the second letter and say its English name: Wein (wine), Wien (Vienna). eu and äu sound like oy , and au like ow . A long e, as in Tee (tea), is written ay : say day without the glide at the end.
 EX: der Wein | dayr vain | the wine
 EX: in Wien | in veen | in Vienna
CARDS NOW: der Wein = wine [card] || in Wien = in Vienna

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Umlauts: ä, ö, ü
 The two dots change the sound. ä is close to the e in bet . For ü , say ee with your lips rounded; the pronunciation line writes it ew . For ö , say ay with rounded lips; the line writes ur , but there is no r sound in it.
 EX: fünf | fewnf | five
 EX: schön | shurn | beautiful
CARDS NOW: fünf = five [card] || schön = beautiful, nice [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Two ch sounds
 After a, o, u and au , ch is throaty, like Scottish loch : the line writes kh . Everywhere else it is soft, like a strong h in huge : the line writes hh . Neither one is a k or a sh.
 EX: das Buch | dahs bookh | the book
 EX: ich | ihh | I
CARDS NOW: das Buch = book [card] || ich = I [card]

### SECTION 4   (has 4)
Word endings
 At the end of a word, b, d, g sound like p, t, k : Hund sounds like hoont , and Tag (day) like tahk . A final e is never silent, so Bitte has two syllables. The line writes this weak e, and the weak endings -en and -er , with uh : BIT-uh .
 EX: der Hund | dayr hoont | the dog
 EX: Bitte! | BIT-uh | Please!
CARDS NOW: der Tag = day [card] || Bitte! = Please! / You're welcome! [card] || er = he; it (masculine noun) [card] || der Hund = dog [card]

### SECTION 5   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Stress, r and capital letters
 Most German words stress their first syllable, but be-, ge-, ver-, er- and ent- are never stressed, and many words borrowed from other languages stress the last syllable: Hotel , Büro . The German r is made in the throat, and after a vowel it often softens to an uh ; listen to the audio. Every noun starts with a capital letter, and the small words der, die, das in front of nouns all mean "the" (lesson 3 explains them).
 EX: Entschuldigung! | ehnt-SHOOL-dih-goong | Excuse me!
 Most common mistake: reading w the English way. Wasser with an English w → Wasser starting with a v sound.
CARDS NOW: Entschuldigung! = Excuse me! / Sorry! [card]

## LESSON greetings — 2 · Greetings: Sie and du — deck "Greetings and courtesy"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
Greetings change with the time of day, and German has two ways to say "you". Start with the polite one: it is what you need with strangers.
 Hello and goodbye
 Guten Morgen works until about ten or eleven, Guten Tag through the day, and Guten Abend from about six. Gute Nacht is only for leaving late or going to bed. Hallo is casual and fine almost anywhere. To leave, say Auf Wiedersehen (polite) or Tschüss (casual).
 EX: Guten Morgen! | GOO-tuhn MAWR-guhn | Good morning!
 EX: Guten Tag, Frau Becker. | GOO-tuhn tahk frow BEHK-uh | Hello, Ms. Becker.
CARDS NOW: Guten Morgen! = Good morning! [card] || Guten Tag! = Hello! (daytime) [card] || Guten Abend! = Good evening! [card] || Gute Nacht! = Good night! [card] || Hallo! = Hi! [card] || Auf Wiedersehen! = Goodbye! (formal) [card] || Tschüss! = Bye! [card] || Guten Tag, Frau Becker. = Hello, Ms. Becker.

### SECTION 1   (has 7)
Two ways to say "you"
 English has one "you". German uses Sie for people you would call Frau or Herr plus their last name: staff, officials, older strangers, customers. It uses du for friends, family, children and people your age at a party. When in doubt, use Sie : too polite is quickly fixed, too casual can sound rude.

(to a friend) [card] || Woher kommen Sie? = Where are you from? (formal) [card] || Wie geht es Ihnen? = How are you? (formal) [card] || Hallo! Wie heißt du? = Hi! What's your name? (to a friend)

### SECTION 2   (has 5)
The verb changes too
 The form of a verb you find in a word list is the infinitive: heißen (to be called), kommen (to come). With Sie , the verb looks exactly like the infinitive: Sie heißen, Sie kommen . With du , it ends in -st : kommst . heißen already ends in an s sound, so it adds only -t : du heißt . When you talk about yourself, with ich , the verb ends in -e .
 EX: Woher kommen Sie? | voh-HAYR KAWM-uhn zee | Where are you from? (formal)
 EX: Ich komme aus Chicago. | ihh KAWM-uh ows shih-KAH-goh | I'm from Chicago.
 EX: Ich heiße Emily. | ihh HAI-suh EH-mih-lee | My name is Emily.
CARDS NOW: heißen = to be called [card] || kommen = to come [card] || ich = I [card] || Ich komme aus Chicago. = I'm from Chicago. || Ich heiße Emily. = My name is Emily. [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
Sie or sie?
 Sie with a capital S means "you". sie with a small s means "she" or "they". In speech they sound the same; in writing the capital letter matters.
CARDS NOW: (none)

## LESSON gender — 3 · der, die, das and plurals — deck "People and family"
### SECTION 0   (has 6)
English has one word for "the". German has three for one person or thing, because every noun has a gender: masculine, feminine or neuter.
 Three genders
 Masculine nouns take der , feminine nouns take die , and neuter nouns take das . For people the gender usually follows the meaning: der Vater (father), die Mutter (mother), das Kind (child).
 EX: der Vater | dayr FAH-tuh | the father
 EX: die Mutter | dee MOOT-uh | the mother
 EX: das Kind | dahs kint | the child
CARDS NOW: der = the (masculine) [card] || die = the (feminine; all plurals) [card] || das = the (neuter); that [card] || der Vater = father [card] || die Mutter = mother [card] || das Kind = child [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Things have a gender too
 A table is masculine, a lamp is feminine and a bed is neuter. There is no logic you can see from the meaning, so learn every noun with its article, as one unit: not Tisch , but der Tisch . That is why every noun in this course comes with der , die or das . Notice the capital letter too: every German noun has one.
 EX: Das ist der Tisch. | dahs ist dayr tish | This is the table.
CARDS NOW: der Tisch = table [card] || Das ist der Tisch. = This is the table.

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Endings that help
 A few endings tell you the gender for certain. Nouns ending in -ung , -heit and -keit are always feminine. Nouns ending in -chen are always neuter, even when they mean a person: das Mädchen (the girl) is neuter, because grammar follows the ending, not the meaning.
 EX: die Zeitung | dee TSAI-toong | the newspaper
 EX: das Mädchen | dahs MEHT-hhuhn | the girl
CARDS NOW: das Mädchen = girl [card] || die Zeitung = newspaper [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Compound nouns
 German glues nouns together. The last part decides the gender: der Schlaf (sleep) + das Zimmer (room) = das Schlafzimmer (bedroom).
 EX: das Schlafzimmer | dahs SHLAHF-tsim-uh | the bedroom
CARDS NOW: das Zimmer = room [card] || das Schlafzimmer = bedroom [card]

### SECTION 4   (has 6)
Plurals
 Here is the good news: in the plural, every noun takes die , whatever its gender. The bad news: the noun itself changes in several ways, so learn the plural with the noun too.

 So die has two jobs: a feminine noun ( die Frau ) or any plural ( die Kinder , die Tage ).
 Most common mistake: using die for everything. die Tisch → der Tisch . Say the article every time you say the noun, until it sounds wrong without it.
CARDS NOW: der Tag = day [card] || die Frau = woman; wife [card] || das Buch = book [card] || die Bücher = the books || das Auto = car [card] || die Kinder = the children

## LESSON sein-haben — 4 · sein and haben: I am, I have — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 12)
English changes "be" a little: I am, you are, he is . German does the same with sein (to be) and haben (to have), but every person has its own form. Learn these two by heart: almost every sentence needs one, and they are the two verbs that break the regular pattern most.
 sein and haben

### SECTION 1   (has 4)
Jobs: no article
 English says "I'm a teacher". German leaves out "a" before a job: Ich bin Lehrerin . A woman adds -in to most job words; a man says Ich bin Lehrer . Many job words work this way: Student, Studentin ; Ingenieur, Ingenieurin .

Age: the same as English
 Good news: age uses sein , just as in English. You can also leave out Jahre alt and just say the number, as English speakers do.

### SECTION 3   (has 4)
Having things and people
 After haben , "a" is eine before a feminine noun and ein before a neuter one. (Masculine nouns change after haben ; that comes in a later lesson.)

 EX: Wir haben ein Auto. | veer HAH-buhn ain OW-toh | We have a car.
CARDS NOW: eine = a; an (feminine) [card] || ein = a; an (masculine, neuter) [card] || Er hat eine Schwester. = He has a sister. || Wir haben ein Auto. = We have a car.

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Hungry and thirsty
 Some feelings use haben where English uses "be". English is hungry; German has hunger. Ich habe Hunger and Ich habe Durst are the everyday ways to say "I'm hungry" and "I'm thirsty".

CARDS NOW: Ich habe Hunger. = I'm hungry. [card] || Ich habe Durst. = I'm thirsty. [card]

## LESSON present — 5 · Present tense: regular verbs — deck "Work and study"
### SECTION 0   (has 10)
English verbs barely change: I live, you live, she lives . German verbs take a different ending for almost every person. The good news: most verbs follow one pattern.
 Stem plus ending
 Take the infinitive, the form in a word list ( wohnen , to live), and drop -en . What is left is the stem: wohn- . Then add the ending that matches the subject.

 ich ; -e ; wohne ;

 EX: Ich wohne in Berlin. | ihh VOH-nuh in behr-LEEN | I live in Berlin.

 For Sie (formal you) and sie (they), the verb is simply the infinitive: Sie wohnen . A name works like er or sie : Lena wohnt . Two or more people work like sie (they): Lena und Max wohnen .
 EX: Sie wohnen in Köln. | zee VOH-nuhn in kurln | They live in Cologne.

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)

 If the stem ends in -t or -d , an e slips in so you can say the ending: du arbeitest , er arbeitet , ihr arbeitet . Without it, arbeitst would be almost impossible to say, and er arbeitt would sound the same as the stem. If it ends in an s sound ( s, ß, z ), the du form adds only -t : du heißt , du tanzt .

## LESSON word-order — 6 · Verb second: Heute arbeite ich — deck "Days, dates and weather"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
English keeps the subject before the verb, whatever comes first: Today I work . German has a different rule, and it is the most important rule of German word order.
 The verb is the second element
 In a German statement, the verb with the ending always stands in second place. The first place can hold the subject, a time, a place or something else. Whatever stands first, the verb comes next.
 EX: Ich arbeite heute. | ihh AHR-bai-tuh HOY-tuh | I'm working today.
 EX: Heute arbeite ich. | HOY-tuh AHR-bai-tuh ihh | Today I'm working.
 Both sentences mean the same; German simply lets you choose what to put first, often the thing you want to stress or the thing that links to what came before. In the second one, heute takes the first place, so the subject ich moves behind the verb. It cannot stay in front: Heute ich arbeite is the classic English speaker's mistake.
CARDS NOW: Ich arbeite heute. = I'm working today. || ich = I [card] || heute = today [card] || Heute arbeite ich. = Today I'm working.

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
Element, not word
 "Second" means the second building block, not the second word. Am Montag (on Monday) is one block, and so is die Kinder .
 EX: Am Montag habe ich Zeit. | ahm MOHN-tahk HAH-buh ihh tsait | On Monday I have time.
 EX: Die Kinder spielen im Park. | dee KIN-duh SHPEE-luhn im pahrk | The children are playing in the park.
CARDS NOW: am Montag = on Monday [card] || Am Montag habe ich Zeit. = On Monday I have time. || Die Kinder spielen im Park. = The children are playing in the park.

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
Time words that often come first
 heute (today), morgen (tomorrow), am Wochenende (on the weekend), and the days with am : am Montag, am Dienstag, am Samstag, am Sonntag . Starting with one of them is very natural, and the subject then follows the verb.
 EX: Morgen fahre ich nach Hamburg. | MAWR-guhn FAH-ruh ihh nahkh HAHM-boork | Tomorrow I'm going to Hamburg.
CARDS NOW: morgen = tomorrow [card] || am = on the; at the [card] || am Samstag = on Saturday [card] || Morgen fahre ich nach Hamburg. = Tomorrow I'm going to Hamburg.

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
The weather
 Weather sentences need es (it), just like English: Es regnet (it's raining). Put a time word first and es moves behind the verb.
 EX: Es regnet. | ehs RAYG-nuht | It's raining.
 EX: Heute ist es kalt. | HOY-tuh ist ehs kahlt | It's cold today.
CARDS NOW: es = it [card] || Es regnet. = It's raining. [card] || Heute ist es kalt. = It's cold today. [card]

### SECTION 4   (has 4)
und and aber do not count
