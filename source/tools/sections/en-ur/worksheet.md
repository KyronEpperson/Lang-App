## LESSON greetings — 6 · Greetings: السلام علیکم, شکریہ, and آپ — deck "Greetings and courtesy"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
From this lesson on, Urdu appears as it really is written, without vowel marks. The one exception is a handful of words that share their spelling with another word: they keep one small mark so you can tell them apart, like تُو below. Read the Urdu first; glance at the romanisation only to check.
 Hello and goodbye
 English changes its greeting with the time of day. In Pakistan one greeting works morning, noon and night, with anyone: السلام علیکم assalāmu alaikum , "peace be upon you". The reply is وعلیکم السلام va-alaikum assalām , "and upon you peace".
 EX: السلام علیکم۔ | assalāmu alaikum. | Hello.
 EX: وعلیکم السلام۔ | va-alaikum assalām. | Hello. (the reply)
 To say goodbye, say اللہ حافظ allāh hāfiz , the usual form today. خدا حافظ ḵhudā hāfiz means the same and is also heard, especially from older people. Both are correct.
 EX: اللہ حافظ۔ | allāh hāfiz. | Goodbye.
CARDS NOW: السلام علیکم۔ = Hello. (peace be upon you) [card] || وعلیکم السلام۔ = Hello. (the reply) [card] || خدا حافظ۔ = Goodbye. (the older form) [card] || اللہ حافظ۔ = Goodbye. [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
Thank you, and the answer
 شکریہ shukriyā is "thank you"; بہت شکریہ bahut shukriyā is "thank you very much". The answer is کوئی بات نہیں koī bāt nahī̃ , literally "it's nothing". معاف کیجیے māf kījie is "excuse me" or "sorry".
 EX: بہت شکریہ۔ | bahut shukriyā. | Thank you very much.
 EX: کوئی بات نہیں۔ | koī bāt nahī̃. | You're welcome. No problem.
CARDS NOW: بہت شکریہ۔ = Thank you very much. [card] || شکریہ۔ = Thank you. [card] || کوئی بات نہیں۔ = No problem. / You're welcome. [card]

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
The little word جی
 جی jī adds respect. Put it after someone else's name or title: علی صاحب alī sāhab , امی جی ammī jī . On its own it is a polite "yes?". جی ہاں jī hā̃ is a polite yes, جی نہیں jī nahī̃ a polite no. Never put جی after your own name.
CARDS NOW: جی = yes (polite); pardon? [card] || علی صاحب = Mr. Ali [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 9)
Three words for "you"
 English has one "you". Urdu has three. آپ āp is polite: strangers, older people, shopkeepers, teachers. تم tum is for friends, children and younger relatives. تُو tū is for very close friends and small children, and is rude otherwise; just recognize it. When in doubt, use آپ.
 آپ always takes the plural verb ہیں haĩ , even for one person. And "how" changes with the person you ask: to a man it is کیسے, to a woman کیسی.

 EX: آپ کیسے ہیں؟ | āp kaise haĩ? | How are you? (to a man)
 EX: آپ کیسی ہیں؟ | āp kaisī haĩ? | How are you? (to a woman)
 EX: میں ٹھیک ہوں، شکریہ۔ | maĩ ṭhīk hū̃, shukriyā. | I'm fine, thanks.
 Many people answer الحمد للہ alhamdu lillāh , "thank God", before or instead of "fine".
 Most common mistake: answering thanks with "welcome". خوش آمدید means "welcome" to a place, as on a sign: خوش آمدید → کوئی بات نہیں .

## LESSON hona — 7 · Pronouns and ہونا: am, is, are — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
English has three forms of "be": am, is, are. Urdu has four, all from the verb ہونا honā , "to be". Each pronoun takes its own form, and the form must agree with the pronoun.
 The verb goes last
 English says "I am a doctor". Urdu says "I doctor am": the verb comes at the end of the sentence. Build every sentence in this lesson the same way: first the person, then what they are, then the form of ہونا.
 EX: میں ڈاکٹر ہوں۔ | maĩ ḍākṭar hū̃. | I'm a doctor.
CARDS NOW: ہونا = to be; to become; to happen [card] || میں ڈاکٹر ہوں۔ = I'm a doctor. [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 7)
Who takes which form

 EX: تم خوش ہو۔ | tum ḵhush ho. | You're happy. (to a friend)
 EX: آپ مصروف ہیں۔ | āp masrūf haĩ. | You're busy. (polite)
 EX: ہم پاکستانی ہیں۔ | ham pākistānī haĩ. | We're Pakistani.
 آپ is plural in grammar even when you speak to one person, so it always takes ہیں haĩ .
CARDS NOW: تم = you (friend, child) [card] || آپ = you (polite) [card] || ہیں = are [card] || ہم = we [card] || تم خوش ہو۔ = You're happy. (to a friend) || آپ مصروف ہیں۔ = You're busy. (polite) || ہم پاکستانی ہیں۔ = We're Pakistani.

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
He, she, it: one word
 English chooses between he, she and it. Urdu does not: یہ yeh is someone or something near you, and وہ voh is someone or something farther away. So وہ ڈاکٹر ہے can mean "he is a doctor" or "she is a doctor"; the situation tells you which.
 EX: یہ کتاب ہے۔ | yeh kitāb hai. | This is a book.
 EX: وہ ڈاکٹر ہے۔ | voh ḍākṭar hai. | He's a doctor. She's a doctor.
CARDS NOW: یہ = this; he / she / it (near); these [card] || وہ = that; he / she / it (far); they [card] || یہ کتاب ہے۔ = This is a book. || وہ ڈاکٹر ہے۔ = He's a doctor. She's a doctor.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
More than one, and respect
 With ہیں, یہ and وہ mean "these" and "they". Urdu also uses this plural for one person you respect, such as a teacher or an older relative: وہ استاد ہیں is "he is a teacher", said with respect.
 EX: وہ یہاں ہیں۔ | voh yahā̃ haĩ. | They're here.
 EX: وہ استاد ہیں۔ | voh ustād haĩ. | He's a teacher. (said with respect)
CARDS NOW: وہ یہاں ہیں۔ = They're here. || وہ استاد ہیں۔ = He's a teacher. (said with respect)

### SECTION 4   (has 7)
Words you can use now
 Words that never change their shape fit straight into these sentences: ٹھیک ṭhīk fine, خوش ḵhush happy, بیمار bīmār sick, مصروف masrūf busy, پاکستانی pākistānī Pakistani, امریکی amrīkī American, ڈاکٹر ḍākṭar doctor. Words like بڑا change their ending to match the person or thing; that comes in the lesson on adjectives.
CARDS NOW: ٹھیک = fine; OK [card] || خوش = happy; glad [card] || بیمار = sick; ill [card] || مصروف = busy [card] || پاکستانی = Pakistani [card] || امریکی = American [card] || ڈاکٹر = doctor [card]

## LESSON intro — 8 · Introducing yourself, and yes-no questions with کیا — deck "Traps for English speakers"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Your name
 English says "my name is". Urdu says the same, with the verb last: میرا نام merā nām … ہے hai . "Name" is a masculine word in Urdu, so "my" is میرا whoever is speaking, man or woman. Never add جی after your own name; جی is respect you give to others.
 EX: میرا نام ڈیوڈ ہے۔ | merā nām ḍeviḍ hai. | My name is David.
CARDS NOW: میرا نام ڈیوڈ ہے۔ = My name is David.

### SECTION 1   (has 5)
Where you are from, and where you live
 English puts "from" and "in" before the place. Urdu puts them after it: امریکہ سے amrīkā se , "America from". These little words are called postpositions, and a whole lesson on them comes later.
 EX: میں امریکہ سے ہوں۔ | maĩ amrīkā se hū̃. | I'm from America.
 To say where you live, use میں after the city. It is spelled just like میں, "I", but after a place it means "in" and is said مِیں mẽ ; the small mark is only here to show the reading. The word for "live" changes with the speaker: a man says رہتا ہوں rahtā hū̃ , a woman says رہتی ہوں rahtī hū̃ . Learn these two as set phrases for now; the lesson on daily habits explains how they are built.
 EX: میں ہیوسٹن میں رہتا ہوں۔ | maĩ hyūsṭan mẽ rahtā hū̃. | I live in Houston. (a man speaking)
 EX: میں شکاگو میں رہتی ہوں۔ | maĩ shikāgo mẽ rahtī hū̃. | I live in Chicago. (a woman speaking)
 And when someone asks آپ کیسے ہیں؟, the everyday answer is میں ٹھیک ہوں maĩ ṭhīk hū̃ , "I am fine", with the same ہوں.
CARDS NOW: میں امریکہ سے ہوں۔ = I'm from America. [card] || میں ٹھیک ہوں۔ = I'm fine. [card] || مِیں = in; inside; into [card] || میں ہیوسٹن میں رہتا ہوں۔ = I live in Houston. (a man speaking) || میں شکاگو میں رہتی ہوں۔ = I live in Chicago. (a woman speaking) [card]

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Your age
 English says "I am 25". In Urdu that sounds like "I am 25 years", a strange thing to be. Say "my age is 25 years" instead. Age, عمر umr , is a feminine word, so "my" becomes میری.
 EX: میری عمر پچیس سال ہے۔ | merī umr pacchīs sāl hai. | I'm twenty-five.
 You will also hear میں پچیس سال کا ہوں from a man and میں پچیس سال کی ہوں from a woman. Both are correct.
CARDS NOW: عمر = age [card] || میری عمر پچیس سال ہے۔ = I'm twenty-five. [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 4)
Yes-no questions with کیا
 English turns "you are" around: "are you?". Urdu keeps the order of the sentence and puts کیا kyā at the start. In speech, a rising voice alone can also make the question.
 EX: کیا آپ پاکستانی ہیں؟ | kyā āp pākistānī haĩ? | Are you Pakistani?
 EX: جی ہاں، میں پاکستانی ہوں۔ | jī hā̃, maĩ pākistānī hū̃. | Yes, I'm Pakistani.
 EX: جی نہیں، میں امریکی ہوں۔ | jī nahī̃, maĩ amrīkī hū̃. | No, I'm American.
 Here کیا has no meaning of its own: it just tells the listener a question is coming. To answer, start with جی ہاں or جی نہیں from the greetings lesson, then say the sentence again with میں if you like.
 Most common mistake: giving your age the English way. میں پچیس سال ہوں → میری عمر پچیس سال ہے .

## LESSON gender — 9 · Gender and plurals: لڑکا, لڑکے, لڑکی, لڑکیاں — deck "Home and daily things"
### SECTION 0   (has 10)
English calls every thing "it". In Urdu every noun is either masculine or feminine, things as well as people: a room is masculine, a book is feminine. You cannot hear it in the noun itself, but you see it in the words around it. You have already met one: میرا merā for a masculine noun, میری merī for a feminine one.
 EX: یہ میرا گھر ہے۔ | yeh merā ghar hai. | This is my house.
 EX: یہ میری کتاب ہے۔ | yeh merī kitāb hai. | This is my book.
 For people, gender simply follows the person: a boy or a man is masculine, a girl or a woman, عورت aurat , is feminine. For things there is no logic to find; a table is not more "feminine" than a room.
 Endings that help
 Most nouns ending in -ā, written ا or ہ, are masculine: لڑکا laṛkā boy, کمرہ kamrā room, کپڑا kapṛā cloth. Most nouns ending in -ī, written ی, are feminine: لڑکی laṛkī girl, گاڑی gāṛī car, کرسی kursī chair.
CARDS NOW: میرا = my; mine [card] || یہ میرا گھر ہے۔ = This is my house. || یہ میری کتاب ہے۔ = This is my book. || کتاب = book [card] || عورت = woman [card] || لڑکا = boy; young man [card] || کمرہ = room [card] || لڑکی = girl; young woman [card] || گاڑی = car [card] || کرسی = chair [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 4)
Exceptions worth learning now
 Some very common words break the pattern. پانی pānī water and آدمی ādmī man end in ی but are masculine. کتاب kitāb book, بات bāt matter and میز mez table are feminine with no ی at all. For words with other endings, learn the gender with the word, the way the cards in this course give it.
 EX: یہ میرا پانی ہے۔ | yeh merā pānī hai. | This is my water.
CARDS NOW: پانی = water [card] || آدمی = man [card] || بات = matter; thing said; talk [card] || یہ میرا پانی ہے۔ = This is my water.

### SECTION 2   (has 5)
Making plurals
 English adds -s. Urdu has four patterns, decided by gender and ending.

 other masculine (no change) ; گھر، آدمی ; گھر، آدمی ;

 EX: یہ لڑکے ہیں۔ | yeh laṛke haĩ. | These are boys.
 EX: یہ لڑکیاں ہیں۔ | yeh laṛkiyā̃ haĩ. | These are girls.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Learn it with a partner word
 The quickest way to remember a gender is to learn each new noun with میرا or میری: میرا کمرہ, میری گاڑی. The pair sticks better than a rule.

### SECTION 0   (has 7)
English puts little words like "in", "on" and "from" before the noun: "in the house". Urdu puts them after it: گھر میں ghar mẽ , literally "house in". These words are called postpositions, because they come after the noun.

### SECTION 0   (has 7)
English uses one verb, "have", for a car, a brother and a fever. Urdu has no verb "to have" at all. Instead it says where the thing is, or whose it is, and uses ہے or ہیں . Which pattern you use depends on what you have.

## LESSON time — 19 · Clock time: کتنے بجے ہیں؟ — deck "Time, days and routine"
### SECTION 0   (has 5)
English says "It's three o'clock". Urdu says "three have struck": تین بجے ہیں . The word بجے baje comes from the verb for a clock striking, and like any -ā word it agrees.
 EX: کتنے بجے ہیں؟ | kitne baje haĩ? | What time is it?
 In casual speech many people ask with the English loan word instead: ٹائم کیا ہوا ہے؟ Both are everyday Urdu.
 One o'clock and the other hours
 One o'clock is singular: ایک بجا ہے . From two on, the hour is plural: بجے ہیں .
 EX: ایک بجا ہے۔ | ek bajā hai. | It's one o'clock.
 EX: چار بجے ہیں۔ | chār baje haĩ. | It's four o'clock.
 "At" a time needs no extra word: تین بجے is "at three", and even "at one" is ایک بجے .
 EX: کلاس کتنے بجے ہے؟ | klās kitne baje hai? | What time is the class?

## LESSON past — 25 · The simple past and نے — deck "Getting around"
### SECTION 0   (has 11)
English adds -ed (or uses an irregular form) for something that happened and is finished: "came", "ate", "went". Urdu does it with the stem plus an ending, and adds one twist that English does not have.
 Stem + ending
 The ending agrees with the subject, like اچھا : -ا for one man, -ے for men and آپ , -ی for a woman, -یں for women. A stem ending in a vowel adds ی or ئ first.

### SECTION 1   (has 11)
The twist: نے
 With a verb that takes an object (eat, drink, read, buy, take, see), the completed past works differently. The subject takes نے ne , and the verb stops agreeing with the subject. It agrees with the object instead. Think of it as "by me, tea was drunk": the tea decides the ending.
 EX: میں نے چائے پی۔ | maĩ ne chāe pī. | I drank tea.
 EX: سارہ نے کھانا کھایا۔ | sārā ne khānā khāyā. | Sarah ate.
 Sarah is a woman, but کھانا (food) is masculine, so the verb is کھایا . With کتاب , which is feminine, it is پڑھی , whoever read it.
 EX: اُس نے کتاب پڑھی۔ | us ne kitāb paṛhī. | He read the book. / She read the book.
 EX: میں نے دو سموسے کھائے۔ | maĩ ne do samose khāe. | I ate two samosas.

 EX: آپ نے کیا کھایا؟ | āp ne kyā khāyā? | What did you eat?

## LESSON can-must — 27 · Can and must: سکنا, مجھے جانا ہے — deck "Health and help"
### SECTION 0   (has 6)
English has a set of little helper words: can, may, have to, should. Urdu builds the same ideas from patterns you already know.
 can: stem + سکنا

 EX: میں اردو بول سکتا ہوں۔ | maĩ urdū bol saktā hū̃. | I can speak Urdu. (a man speaking)

### SECTION 2   (has 7)
have to: مجھے + infinitive + ہے
 "I have to go" is built like مجھے چاہیے : the person takes کو ( مجھے , آپ کو , ہمیں ), then the full verb with نا , then ہے . Literally, "to me, going is".
 EX: مجھے جانا ہے۔ | mujhe jānā hai. | I have to go.
 EX: ہمیں کل جلدی اٹھنا ہے۔ | hamẽ kal jaldī uṭhnā hai. | We have to get up early tomorrow.
 It is also the polite way to leave: مجھے جانا ہے , then اللہ حافظ . For a friend the person is تمہیں ; for anyone else, the name plus کو : علی کو کام کرنا ہے (Ali has to work). The verb never changes for the person, because the person is not the subject.
CARDS NOW: مجھے = me; to me [card] || کو = to; at (time); object marker [card] || آپ کو = you; to you (polite) [card] || ہمیں = us; to us [card] || مجھے جانا ہے۔ = I have to go. [card] || ہمیں کل جلدی اٹھنا ہے۔ = We have to get up early tomorrow. || تمہیں = you; to you (friend) [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
should: مجھے + infinitive + چاہیے
 Swap ہے for چاہیے and "have to" becomes "should". This is the pattern for advice, from friends and from doctors alike. It is softer than ہے : a suggestion, not an obligation.
 EX: آپ کو آرام کرنا چاہیے۔ | āp ko ārām karnā chāhie. | You should rest.
 EX: آپ کو ڈاکٹر کے پاس جانا چاہیے۔ | āp ko ḍākṭar ke pās jānā chāhie. | You should see a doctor.
 English ; Urdu pattern ; 
 I can go ; میں جا سکتا ہوں ; 
 I have to go ; مجھے جانا ہے ; 
 I should go ; مجھے جانا چاہیے ; 
 Notice the split: "can" starts with میں and uses the stem; "have to" and "should" start with مجھے and use the full verb.
 Most common mistake: keeping the English "I" in "I have to". میں جانا ہے → مجھے جانا ہے (I have to go). And with "can", drop the نا : میں بولنا سکتا ہوں → میں بول سکتا ہوں .
CARDS NOW: چاہیے = need; should [card] || آپ کو آرام کرنا چاہیے۔ = You should rest. [card] || آپ کو ڈاکٹر کے پاس جانا چاہیے۔ = You should see a doctor.

## LESSON connectors — 28 · اور, لیکن, کیونکہ: joining ideas — deck "Questions and connectors"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
You can already say a lot in short sentences. This lesson gives you the small words that join them, so you can explain, give reasons and talk about plans with conditions. Each half keeps its own word order, with its verb at the end.
 and, or, but
 These work as in English, between the two things they join: اور aur (and), یا yā (or), لیکن lekin (but). مگر magar also means "but" and is a little more formal. Two people joined by اور are plural, so the verb agrees: ہیں . A question with یا offers a choice, as at a dhaba.
 EX: علی اور سارہ دوست ہیں۔ | alī aur sārā dost haĩ. | Ali and Sarah are friends.
 EX: چائے یا کافی؟ | chāe yā kāfī? | Tea or coffee?
 EX: مجھے اردو پسند ہے، لیکن یہ مشکل ہے۔ | mujhe urdū pasand hai, lekin yeh mushkil hai. | I like Urdu, but it's hard.
CARDS NOW: اور = and [card] || یا = or [card] || لیکن = but [card] || مگر = but [card] || ہیں = are [card] || علی اور سارہ دوست ہیں۔ = Ali and Sarah are friends. || چائے یا کافی؟ = Tea or coffee? [card] || مجھے اردو پسند ہے، لیکن یہ مشکل ہے۔ = I like Urdu, but it's hard.

### SECTION 1   (has 4)
because, and so
 English learners often swap these two. کیونکہ kyõki (because) comes before the reason. اس لیے is lie (so, that's why) comes before the result. Same facts, two orders:
 EX: میں نہیں آیا، کیونکہ میں بیمار تھا۔ | maĩ nahī̃ āyā, kyõki maĩ bīmār thā. | I didn't come because I was sick. (a man speaking)
 EX: میں بیمار تھا، اس لیے نہیں آیا۔ | maĩ bīmār thā, is lie nahī̃ āyā. | I was sick, so I didn't come. (a man speaking)
CARDS NOW: کیونکہ = because [card] || اس لیے = so; that's why [card] || میں نہیں آیا، کیونکہ میں بیمار تھا۔ = I didn't come because I was sick. (a man speaking) || میں بیمار تھا، اس لیے نہیں آیا۔ = I was sick, so I didn't come. (a man speaking)

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
that: کہ
 After "say", "think" and "know", English can drop "that". Urdu keeps it: کہ ki joins the two halves. Do not confuse it with کہہ , the stem of "say". A very common way to say "I think" uses it too: مجھے لگتا ہے کہ , literally "it seems to me that".
 EX: سارہ کہتی ہے کہ وہ کل آئے گی۔ | sārā kahtī hai ki voh kal āe gī. | Sarah says (that) she'll come tomorrow.
CARDS NOW: کہ = that [card] || سارہ کہتی ہے کہ وہ کل آئے گی۔ = Sarah says (that) she'll come tomorrow.

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
if … then: اگر … تو
 English often says "if" alone. Urdu likes a pair: اگر agar starts the condition and تو to starts the result, like "if … then".
 EX: اگر بارش ہوئی تو ہم گھر پر رہیں گے۔ | agar bārish huī to ham ghar par rahẽ ge. | If it rains, we'll stay at home.
CARDS NOW: اگر = if [card] || تو = then; so [card] || اگر بارش ہوئی تو ہم گھر پر رہیں گے۔ = If it rains, we'll stay home. [card]

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
when: جب
 جب jab starts a time clause, which usually comes first. As with اگر , the second half may start with تو , but here it is optional.
 EX: جب میں چھوٹا تھا، میں لاہور میں رہتا تھا۔ | jab maĩ chhoṭā thā, maĩ lāhaur mẽ rahtā thā. | When I was little, I lived in Lahore. (a man speaking)
CARDS NOW: جب = when (not a question) [card] || جب میں چھوٹا تھا، میں لاہور میں رہتا تھا۔ = When I was little, I lived in Lahore. (a man speaking)

### SECTION 5   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
and then: پھر
 To tell events in order, Urdu likes پھر phir (then, next) between the steps, where English might just say "and".
 EX: ہم نے کھانا کھایا، پھر بازار گئے۔ | ham ne khānā khāyā, phir bāzār gae. | We ate, and then we went to the bazaar.
 Most common mistake: using کیونکہ before the result, as if it meant "so". میں بیمار تھا، کیونکہ نہیں آیا → میں بیمار تھا، اس لیے نہیں آیا (I was sick, so I didn't come).
CARDS NOW: پھر = then; after that [card] || ہم نے کھانا کھایا، پھر بازار گئے۔ = We ate, and then we went to the bazaar.
