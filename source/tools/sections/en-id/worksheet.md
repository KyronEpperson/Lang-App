# Worksheet: en-id

## LESSON sounds — 1 · Sounds and spelling — deck "Sounds and spelling"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
Indonesian uses the same letters as English, and its spelling is very regular: a handful of rules let you read almost any word. Under every Indonesian phrase in this course there is a pronunciation line in English letters.
 Six short, pure vowels
 English vowels slide: say "no" slowly and your lips end on a w. Indonesian vowels stay put. The line writes them ah, ee, oo, oh , plus two sounds for one letter. The letter e is either the strong e, eh as in "bed", or the weak e, uh as in "about". The spelling never shows which, so learn it with the word; the pronunciation line always tells you. An e at the very end of a word is usually strong.
 EX: enak | EH-nahq | delicious
 EX: besar | buh-SAHR | big
 EX: sore | SOH-reh | late afternoon
CARDS NOW: enak = delicious [card] || besar = big [card] || sore = late afternoon [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
Capitals mean a slight lift
 One syllable per word is in CAPITALS. It gets a slight lift, nothing like an English stress. Say every syllable evenly and keep every vowel full: never swallow the others the way English swallows the first a in "banana".

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
How words end
 A final k is a catch in the throat, like the break in "uh-oh" (a glottal stop). The line writes it as q . Don't pop the k. A final h is breathed out, never dropped: the line adds an h after the vowel, ahh , eeh .
 EX: tidak | TEE-dahq | not; no
 EX: rumah | ROO-mahh | house
 ai and au at the end of a word are one sound, as in "Thai" and "how". Before a final consonant they split into two syllables.
 EX: pantai | PAHN-tai | beach
 EX: baik | BAH-eeq | good; fine
CARDS NOW: tidak = not; no [card] || pantai = beach [card] || baik = good; fine [card]

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
The noun comes first
 Many cards come as two-word phrases, and they show the language's biggest rule. English says "hot coffee"; Indonesian says kopi panas , literally "coffee hot". The noun comes first and the describing word follows.
 EX: kopi panas | KOH-pee PAH-nahs | hot coffee
 EX: nasi goreng | NAH-see GOH-rehng | fried rice
 Listen first, then read. The line is a crutch: hide it once the spelling feels natural.
 Most common mistake: reading c as k. cantik said "kantik" → cantik said with ch, as in "church".
CARDS NOW: kopi panas = hot coffee [card] || nasi goreng = fried rice [card]

## LESSON greetings — 2 · Greetings by time of day — deck "Greetings and courtesy"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
Indonesians greet by the clock. Selamat means roughly "safe, blessed", and you add the part of the day.
 Four parts of the day
 Greeting ; When ; 
 Selamat pagi ; until about 11 a.m. ; 
 Selamat siang ; about 11 a.m. to 3 p.m. ; 
 Selamat sore ; about 3 p.m. until dark ; 
 Selamat malam ; after dark ; 
 English "good afternoon" covers 1 p.m. and 5 p.m. alike; Indonesian splits it into siang and sore . And malam only starts when it gets dark.
 EX: Selamat pagi. | suh-LAH-maht PAH-ghee | Good morning.
 EX: Selamat sore. | suh-LAH-maht SOH-reh | Good afternoon. (after about 3 p.m.)

### SECTION 1   (has 9)
Add a title
 A bare greeting to an older stranger sounds abrupt. Add a title: Pak (short for Bapak ) for a man, Bu (short for Ibu ) for a woman. For a young waiter or shop assistant, say Mas to a man and Mbak to a woman. The next lesson explains these titles in full.
 EX: Selamat pagi, Bu. | suh-LAH-maht PAH-ghee boo | Good morning, ma'am.
 EX: Selamat malam, Pak. | suh-LAH-maht MAH-lahm pahq | Good evening, sir.
CARDS NOW: Selamat pagi, Pak. = Good morning, sir. [card] || Pak = you (to an older man) [card] || Bapak = sir; Mr.; father [card] || Bu = you (to an older woman) [card] || Ibu = ma'am; Mrs.; mother [card] || Mas = you (to a young man) [card] || Mbak = you (to a young woman) [card] || Selamat pagi, Bu. = Good morning, ma'am. || Selamat malam, Pak. = Good evening, sir.

### SECTION 2   (has 9)
How are you? Thank you.
 Apa kabar? is literally "What news?" The usual answer is Baik , "fine". Terima kasih is "thank you", and the reply is Sama-sama , "you're welcome". For "thanks a lot", add banyak : Terima kasih banyak .
 EX: Apa kabar? | AH-pah KAH-bahr | How are you?
 EX: Baik, terima kasih. | BAH-eeq tuh-REE-mah KAH-seeh | Fine, thank you.
 Careful: Sama-sama never means "same to you". To return a wish, repeat it and add juga , "too".
 EX: Selamat tahun baru juga! | suh-LAH-maht TAH-hoon BAH-roo JOO-gah | Happy New Year to you too!
CARDS NOW: Apa kabar? = How are you? [card] || baik = good; fine [card] || Terima kasih. = Thank you. [card] || Sama-sama. = You're welcome. [card] || banyak = many; a lot [card] || Terima kasih banyak. = Thank you very much. [card] || Baik, terima kasih. = Fine, thank you. [card] || juga = also; too [card] || Selamat tahun baru juga! = Happy New Year to you too!

### SECTION 3   (has 4)
Excuse me, sorry, goodbye
 Permisi gets attention or lets you pass someone. Maaf is "sorry". Goodbye depends on who leaves: the person leaving says Selamat tinggal ("stay well"), and the person staying says Selamat jalan ("go well").
 EX: Permisi. | puhr-MEE-see | Excuse me.
 EX: Selamat jalan. | suh-LAH-maht JAH-lahn | Goodbye. (to someone leaving)
CARDS NOW: Permisi. = Excuse me. [card] || Maaf. = Sorry. [card] || Selamat tinggal. = Goodbye. (said by the one leaving) [card] || Selamat jalan. = Goodbye. (to the one leaving) [card]

Your name
 To say your name, use Nama saya , literally "name my". The owner comes after the noun, and there is no word for "is". The same goes for teman saya , "my friend".
 EX: Nama saya Emma. | NAH-mah SAH-yah EHM-mah | My name is Emma.
 EX: Ini teman saya. | EE-nee tuh-MAHN SAH-yah | This is my friend.

## LESSON pronouns — 3 · Pronouns and titles: saya, kamu, Anda, Bapak, Ibu — deck "People and family"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
Indonesian pronouns never change their form: the same word means "I", "me" and "my". The hard part is "you", which depends on who you are talking to.
 I, he, she, we, they
 English ; Indonesian ; 
 I, me ; saya ; 
 he, she ; dia ; 
 we (not you) ; kami ; 
 we (including you) ; kita ; 
 they ; mereka ; 
 Saya is right everywhere. Friends and family also use aku , which you will hear but need not say yet. Dia is both "he" and "she".
 EX: Dia teman saya. | DEE-ah tuh-MAHN SAH-yah | He's my friend. She's my friend.
 EX: Mereka teman saya. | muh-REH-kah tuh-MAHN SAH-yah | They're my friends.
CARDS NOW: saya = I; me; my [card] || dia = he; she [card] || kami = we (not you) [card] || kita = we (you and me) [card] || mereka = they; them; their [card] || aku = I; me (casual) [card] || Dia teman saya. = He's my friend. She's my friend. || Mereka teman saya. = They're my friends.

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Two words for "we"
 English "we" can include the listener or leave them out. Indonesian always says which. Kami means "we, but not you" (the exclusive "we"); kita means "we, you included" (the inclusive "we"). Telling an Indonesian where you and your family come from, use kami .
 EX: Kami dari Amerika. | KAH-mee DAH-ree ah-meh-REE-kah | We're from the US. (not you)
 EX: teman kita | tuh-MAHN KEE-tah | our friend (yours and mine)
CARDS NOW: Kami dari Amerika. = We're from the US. (not you) || teman kita = our friend (yours and mine)

### SECTION 2   (has 10)
"You": a title first
 English has one "you" for everyone. Indonesians usually say "you" with a title or a name instead of a pronoun:
 Bapak ( Pak when you speak to him): a man older than you, or in a formal role.
 Ibu ( Bu ): a woman older than you, or in a formal role.
 Mas and Mbak : a young man or young woman, such as a waiter or shop assistant. They come from Javanese and are everyday in Jakarta and other cities.
 The person's name: Pak Budi , Bu Sari , or just the name with a friend.
 EX: Terima kasih, Pak. | tuh-REE-mah KAH-seeh pahq | Thank you, sir.
 EX: Selamat siang, Mbak. | suh-LAH-maht SEE-ahng mbahq | Good day. (to a young woman, around midday)
 In shops and online you will also hear Kak for a young person.
CARDS NOW: Bapak = sir; Mr.; father [card] || Pak = you (to an older man) [card] || Ibu = ma'am; Mrs.; mother [card] || Bu = you (to an older woman) [card] || Mas = you (to a young man) [card] || Mbak = you (to a young woman) [card] || Pak Budi = Mr. Budi [card] || Terima kasih, Pak. = Thank you, sir. || Selamat siang, Mbak. = Good day. ### SECTION 3   (has 4)
kamu and Anda
 Kamu is for friends, people your age you know well, and children. Never use it with an older stranger. Anda is the neutral "you" of forms, signs, adverts and formal speech, and a fallback with a stranger when no title fits. In conversation it sounds distant.
 EX: Apa kabar, Budi? | AH-pah KAH-bahr BOO-dee | How are you, Budi?
 EX: Siapa nama Anda? | see-AH-pah NAH-mah AHN-dah | What's your name? (formal)
CARDS NOW: kamu = you (to a friend or a child) [card] || Anda = you (formal, neutral) [card] || Apa kabar, Budi? = How are you, Budi? || Siapa nama Anda? = What's your name? [card]

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
Pronouns as owners
 A pronoun after a noun names the owner: teman saya , "my friend"; teman mereka , "their friend". The noun comes first.
 Most common mistake: kamu to an older stranger. Terima kasih, kamu → Terima kasih, Pak (or Bu ).

## LESSON intro — 4 · Introducing yourself — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
Meeting people in Indonesia, you will answer the same few questions again and again. The good news: none of the answers needs a word for "am".
 Name and country
 English says "My name is Emma" and "I'm from the US". Indonesian says "name my Emma" and "I from America": no "is", no "am".
 EX: Nama saya Emma. | NAH-mah SAH-yah EHM-mah | My name is Emma.
 EX: Saya dari Amerika. | SAH-yah DAH-ree ah-meh-REE-kah | I'm from the US.
 Amerika usually means the United States. To give your nationality, put orang (person) before the country: orang Amerika , "an American", literally "person America".
 EX: Saya orang Amerika. | SAH-yah OH-rahng ah-meh-REE-kah | I'm American.
CARDS NOW: Nama saya Emma. = My name is Emma. [card] || Saya dari Amerika. = I'm from the US. [card] || orang = person; people [card] || Saya orang Amerika. = I'm American. [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
Where you live
 Tinggal is "live, stay", and di means "in" or "at" a place.
 EX: Saya tinggal di Jakarta. | SAH-yah TEENG-gahl dee jah-KAHR-tah | I live in Jakarta.
CARDS NOW: tinggal = to live (somewhere); to stay [card] || di = at; in; on [card] || Saya tinggal di Jakarta. = I live in Jakarta. [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
Your age
 English uses "be" for age: "I'm 25". Indonesian says "my age 25 years": umur saya plus the number plus tahun (year). You will learn the numbers in a later lesson; for now, dua puluh lima is 25. Never use punya (have) for age.
 EX: Umur saya dua puluh lima tahun. | OO-moor SAH-yah DOO-ah POO-looh LEE-mah TAH-hoon | I'm 25 years old.
CARDS NOW: tahun = year [card] || dua puluh lima = twenty-five [card] || punya = to have; to own [card] || Umur saya dua puluh lima tahun. = I'm 25 years old.

### SECTION 3   (has 4)
Job and languages
 For your job, say Saya bekerja sebagai (I work as) plus the job. For a language, bisa (can) plus the language means "speak it", and sedikit is "a little".
 EX: Saya bekerja sebagai guru. | SAH-yah buh-kuhr-JAH suh-BAH-gai GOO-roo | I work as a teacher.
 EX: Saya bisa bahasa Indonesia sedikit. | SAH-yah BEE-sah bah-HAH-sah een-doh-neh-SEE-ah suh-DEE-keet | I speak a little Indonesian.
CARDS NOW: bisa = can; to be able to [card] || sedikit = a little; a few [card] || Saya bekerja sebagai guru. = I work as a teacher. [card] || Saya bisa bahasa Indonesia sedikit. = I speak a little Indonesian. [card]

### SECTION 4   (has 9)
Asking back
 Names are asked with siapa , "who", not apa , "what": literally "Who is your name?". To ask where someone is from, Dari mana? , "From where?", is enough. Use Anda with a stranger when no title fits; with an older man say Bapak , with an older woman Ibu .
 EX: Siapa nama Anda? | see-AH-pah NAH-mah AHN-dah | What's your name? (formal)
 EX: Dari mana? | DAH-ree MAH-nah | Where are you from?
 EX: Kamu tinggal di mana? | KAH-moo TEENG-gahl dee MAH-nah | Where do you live? (to a friend)
 Short answers are normal: to Dari mana? you can simply say Dari Amerika . Once everyone knows who is talking, Indonesians often drop saya as well, so do not worry about repeating it in every sentence.
 Most common mistake: asking a name with "what". Apa nama Anda? → Siapa nama Anda?

[card] || Anda = you (formal, neutral) [card] || Bapak = sir; Mr.; father [card] || Ibu = ma'am; Mrs.; mother [card] || Siapa nama Anda? = What's your name? [card] || Kamu tinggal di mana? = Where do you live? (to a friend) [card] || saya = I; me; my [card]

## LESSON no-be — 5 · A is B, with no 'to be' — deck "Feelings and free time"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
English cannot make a sentence without a verb, so it uses "am", "is" and "are" as glue. Indonesian needs no glue: put the two parts side by side. It is one of the easiest parts of the language, and one of the hardest habits for English speakers to drop, because "am" and "is" slip in without your noticing.
 How you feel: no "am"
 English says "I am hungry". Indonesian says "I hungry": Saya lapar . A describing word follows the person directly.
 EX: Saya lapar. | SAH-yah LAH-pahr | I'm hungry.
 EX: Saya capek. | SAH-yah CHAH-pehq | I'm tired.
 EX: Teman saya senang. | tuh-MAHN SAH-yah suh-NAHNG | My friend is happy.
CARDS NOW: Saya lapar. = I'm hungry. [card] || Saya capek. = I'm tired. [card] || Teman saya senang. = My friend is happy. || Saya senang. = I'm happy. [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
Where someone is: no "is"
 The same goes for places. English "She is at the office" is Dia di kantor , "she at office". Remember that di means "at" or "in".
 EX: Dia di rumah. | DEE-ah dee ROO-mahh | He's at home. She's at home.
 EX: Mereka di Bali. | muh-REH-kah dee BAH-lee | They're in Bali.
CARDS NOW: di = at; in; on [card] || Dia di rumah. = He's at home. She's at home. || Mereka di Bali. = They're in Bali.

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
What someone is: no "is", and no "a"
 For a job or role, put the noun straight after the person. Indonesian needs no word for "a" either, so Saya guru is the whole sentence: "I'm a teacher".
 EX: Saya guru. | SAH-yah GOO-roo | I'm a teacher.
 EX: Budi dokter. | BOO-dee DOHK-tuhr | Budi is a doctor.
CARDS NOW: Saya guru. = I'm a teacher. || Budi dokter. = Budi is a doctor.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
adalah: the careful link between two nouns
 You will see adalah , often translated "is". It only links two noun phrases, mostly in writing and careful speech, such as definitions and facts. In everyday talk it is often left out even there. It is never used before a describing word or a place.
 EX: Ibu kota Indonesia adalah Jakarta. | EE-boo KOH-tah een-doh-neh-SEE-ah ah-DAH-lahh jah-KAHR-tah | The capital of Indonesia is Jakarta.

 She's at the office. ; Dia di kantor. ; Dia adalah di kantor. ;
CARDS NOW: adalah = is (linking two nouns) [card] || Ibu kota Indonesia adalah Jakarta. = The capital of Indonesia is Jakarta.

### SECTION 4   (has 5)
The verb never changes
 Indonesian verbs have no tenses: makan is "eat", "eats", "ate" and "eaten". When the time matters, a time expression such as kemarin (yesterday) or besok (tomorrow) does the job, and the verb stays as it is.
 EX: Saya makan nasi. | SAH-yah MAH-kahn NAH-see | I eat rice. I ate rice.
 EX: Kemarin saya makan nasi goreng. | kuh-MAH-reen SAH-yah MAH-kahn NAH-see GOH-rehng | Yesterday I ate fried rice.
 Most common mistake: translating "am" with adalah . Saya adalah lapar → Saya lapar .
CARDS NOW: makan = to eat [card] || kemarin = yesterday [card] || besok = tomorrow [card] || Saya makan nasi. = I eat rice. I ate rice. || Kemarin saya makan nasi goreng. = Yesterday I ate fried rice.

## LESSON ini-itu — 6 · ini, itu: this and that — deck "Shopping and bargaining"
### SECTION 0   (has 5)
Ini means "this" and itu means "that". They are the words you need most at a market, and where you put them changes the meaning.
 Before the noun: a sentence
 Put ini or itu first and you have a whole sentence: "This is…", "That is…". There is no word for "is", and you need no word for "a". You already met this in Ini teman saya , "This is my friend".
 EX: Ini buku. | EE-nee BOO-koo | This is a book.
 EX: Itu tas saya. | EE-too tahs SAH-yah | That's my bag.
CARDS NOW: tas ini = this bag [card] || ini = this [card] || itu = that; the [card] || Ini buku. = This is a book. || Itu tas saya. = That's my bag.

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
After the noun: "this book"
 English puts "this" and "that" in front: "this book". Indonesian puts them after the noun: buku ini , literally "book this". That is just a phrase, not a sentence, so you can go on and say something about it. Indonesian rarely marks plurals, so buku ini can also mean "these books".
 EX: buku ini | BOO-koo EE-nee | this book
 EX: Buku ini murah. | BOO-koo EE-nee MOO-rahh | This book is cheap.
 EX: Tas itu mahal. | tahs EE-too MAH-hahl | That bag is expensive.

## LESSON possession — 8 · Possession: buku saya, bukuku, bukunya — deck "Home and routine"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
Indonesian has no word for "my" or "your" and no apostrophe-s. The owner simply comes after the thing.
 The owner goes last

 To say "your" politely, use the title: rumah Ibu is "your house" to an older woman, and mobil Bapak is "your car" to an older man.

## LESSON negation — 10 · tidak and bukan — deck "Traps for English speakers"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)

## LESSON time — 12 · Telling the time: jam, pukul, setengah — deck "Time, days and dates"
### SECTION 0   (has 7)
Telling the time in Indonesian uses the numbers you know plus one key word, jam . Its position changes the meaning completely, so watch where it goes.
 jam before the number: o'clock
 English says "three o'clock", with the number first. Indonesian says jam tiga , literally "hour three". Put the number first and you get a length of time instead: tiga jam means "three hours".
 EX: Sekarang jam berapa? | suh-KAH-rahng jahm buh-RAH-pah | What time is it now?
 EX: Sekarang jam tiga. | suh-KAH-rahng jahm TEE-gah | It's three o'clock now.
 EX: Saya tidur delapan jam. | SAH-yah TEE-door duh-LAH-pahn jahm | I sleep eight hours.
 So Jam berapa? asks "what time?", while Berapa jam? asks "how many hours?". In news, timetables and invitations you will see pukul in place of jam : pukul tujuh is a formal "seven o'clock".
CARDS NOW: jam = hour; clock; watch [card] || jam tiga = three o'clock [card] || tiga jam = three hours [card] || Sekarang jam berapa? = What time is it now? || Jam berapa sekarang? = What time is it? [card] || Sekarang jam tiga. = It's three o'clock now. || Saya tidur delapan jam. = I sleep eight hours.

Half past: count toward the next hour
 This is the big trap. English "half past two" counts up from two. Indonesian setengah tiga means "half (way to) three", which is 2:30, not 3:30.
 EX: jam setengah tiga | jahm suh-tuh-NGAHH TEE-gah | 2:30
CARDS NOW: setengah tiga = half past two [card] || jam setengah tiga = 2:30

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Minutes and quarters
 lewat means "past" and kurang means "to" (literally "less"). A quarter is seperempat .

### SECTION 3   (has 7)
Morning, afternoon, night
 English says "at three"; in everyday Indonesian there is no word for "at" before a clock time: you just say the time.
 EX: Saya bangun jam enam. | SAH-yah BAH-ngoon jahm uh-NAHM | I get up at six.
 Instead of a.m. and p.m., add the part of the day after the hour: pagi (morning), siang (midday, about 11 to 3), sore (late afternoon), malam (evening and night).
 EX: jam tujuh pagi | jahm TOO-jooh PAH-ghee | seven in the morning
 EX: jam delapan malam | jahm duh-LAH-pahn MAH-lahm | eight in the evening
CARDS NOW: Saya bangun jam enam. = I get up at six. [card] || pagi = morning [card] || siang = midday; early afternoon [card] || sore = late afternoon [card] || malam = night; evening [card] || jam tujuh pagi = seven in the morning [card] || jam delapan malam = eight in the evening

### SECTION 4   (has 5)
Days and dates
 Days usually take hari (day) in front: hari Senin , Monday. The week runs Senin, Selasa, Rabu, Kamis, Jumat, Sabtu, Minggu . Dates take tanggal and put the day before the month.
 EX: hari Senin | HAH-ree suh-NEEN | Monday
 EX: tanggal tujuh belas Agustus | TAHNG-gahl TOO-jooh buh-LAHS ah-GOOS-toos | August 17
 Most common mistake: putting the number first for a clock time. tiga jam for three o'clock → jam tiga . And remember setengah tiga is 2:30.
CARDS NOW: hari Jumat = Friday [card] || hari Minggu = Sunday [card] || hari = day [card] || hari Senin = Monday [card] || tanggal tujuh belas Agustus = August 17 [card]

## LESSON ada — 13 · ada: there is, have — deck "Around town and directions"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
English says "there is" and "there are", and asks "Is there…?" or "Do you have…?". Indonesian covers all of these with one short word, ada .
 ada: there is, there are
 Put ada in front of the thing. It never changes for one or many, or for past and present. A number still goes before the noun, and a describing word after it.
 EX: Ada dua kamar kosong. | AH-dah DOO-ah KAH-mahr KOH-sohng | There are two free rooms.
 To ask, just raise your voice at the end. The answer is the same word: Ada. (Yes, there is.)
 EX: Ada apotek di dekat sini? | AH-dah ah-POH-tehq dee duh-KAHT SEE-nee | Is there a pharmacy near here?
 EX: Ada. | AH-dah | Yes, there is.
CARDS NOW: ada = there is; to be (somewhere); to have [card] || Ada dua kamar kosong. = There are two free rooms. || Ada apotek di dekat sini? = Is there a pharmacy near here? [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
tidak ada: there isn't any
 For "there isn't" or "there aren't any", put tidak before ada . On its own, Tidak ada. is the short answer "No, there isn't."
 EX: Tidak ada air panas. | TEE-dahq AH-dah AH-eer PAH-nahs | There's no hot water.
 EX: Tidak ada. | TEE-dahq AH-dah | No, there isn't.
CARDS NOW: Tidak ada. = There isn't one. [card] || tidak = not; no [card] || Tidak ada air panas. = There's no hot water.

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
ada for "do you have…?"
 In shops, cafes and hotels, Indonesians ask what is available with ada , not with a word for "have". The staff answer Ada or Tidak ada . To say you own something, use punya : Saya punya mobil .
 EX: Ada kamar kosong? | AH-dah KAH-mahr KOH-sohng | Do you have a free room?
CARDS NOW: punya = to have; to own [card] || Ada kamar kosong? = Do you have a room available? [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
ada before a place
 To say where someone is, you need no verb at all: Dia di rumah , "she's at home". You will also hear ada slipped in before the place, with the same meaning. Both are correct.
 EX: Dia ada di rumah. | DEE-ah AH-dah dee ROO-mahh | She's at home.
CARDS NOW: Dia ada di rumah. = She's at home.

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
ada is not adalah
 They look alike, but they are different words. ada says that something exists or is somewhere. adalah only links two noun phrases in careful speech ( Ibu kota Indonesia adalah Jakarta ). It never means "there is", and it never goes before a place.
 Most common mistake: reaching for adalah because it looks like "is". Adalah apotek di dekat sini? → Ada apotek di dekat sini?
CARDS NOW: adalah = is (linking two nouns) [card]

## LESSON prepositions — 14 · di, ke, dari — deck "Transport and travel"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
English has many small place words (in, at, on, to, from) and one "where". Indonesian sorts places by one question: are you there, going there, or coming from there?
 di: at, in (where something is)
 di covers English "at", "in" and often "on" when nothing is moving. There is still no word for "am" or "is".
 EX: Saya di hotel. | SAH-yah dee HOH-tehl | I'm at the hotel.
CARDS NOW: di = at; in; on [card] || Saya di hotel. = I'm at the hotel.

### SECTION 1   (has 4)
ke: to (where you are going)
 ke marks a destination. After pergi (go) or pulang (go home) you need it, just as English needs "to". In everyday speech ke can even do the job alone: Saya ke kantor , "I'm going to the office".
 EX: Saya pergi ke Bali. | SAH-yah puhr-GHEE kuh BAH-lee | I'm going to Bali.
CARDS NOW: ke = to (a place) [card] || pergi = to go [card] || pulang = to go home [card] || Saya pergi ke Bali. = I'm going to Bali.

### SECTION 2   (has 3)
dari: from
 dari marks where someone or something comes from, as in Saya dari Amerika .
 EX: Kami datang dari Jakarta. | KAH-mee DAH-tahng DAH-ree jah-KAHR-tah | We're coming from Jakarta.
CARDS NOW: dari = from [card] || Saya dari Amerika. = I'm from the US. [card] || Kami datang dari Jakarta. = We're coming from Jakarta.

### SECTION 3   (has 5)
Three ways to ask "where"
 Because the place words split three ways, so does "where": di mana (where, at), ke mana (where to), dari mana (where from). The question word can stay where the answer would go.
 EX: Kamu pergi ke mana? | KAH-moo puhr-GHEE kuh MAH-nah | Where are you going?
 EX: Kamu dari mana? | KAH-moo DAH-ree MAH-nah | Where are you from?
CARDS NOW: di mana = where (at) [card] || ke mana = where to [card] || Dari mana? = Where are you from? [card] || Kamu pergi ke mana? = Where are you going? || Kamu dari mana? = Where are you from?

### SECTION 4   (has 7)
On, under, in front of
 For more exact places, add a position word after di , then the place. The main place word comes first and the owner comes last, as always.

 EX: Toko itu di sebelah hotel. | TOH-koh EE-too dee suh-buh-LAHH HOH-tehl | That shop is next to the hotel.
CARDS NOW: di atas = on top of; above [card] || di bawah = under; below [card] || di depan = in front of [card] || di belakang = behind [card] || di sebelah = next to [card] || Tas saya di bawah meja. = My bag is under the table. || Toko itu di sebelah hotel. = That shop is next to the hotel.

### SECTION 5   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
Spelling: two words
 di and ke before a place are separate words: di rumah , ke Bali , di mana . Indonesians themselves sometimes run them together, but it is a spelling mistake. (Later you will meet a di- that is joined to a verb; that is a different thing.)
 Most common mistake: using di for movement because English "go to" and "be at" both feel like places. Saya pergi di Bali → Saya pergi ke Bali .

### SECTION 0   (has 8)
Family comes up early in any conversation in Indonesia: whether you are married, whether you have children, how many brothers and sisters you have. The words are easy; the surprise is how they sort people.

Older or younger, not brother or sister
 English sorts siblings by sex: brother, sister. Indonesian sorts them by age. kakak is an older brother or sister, adik a younger one. There is no single word that means only "brother". To say which, add laki-laki (male) or perempuan (female) after the word, before the owner.
 EX: kakak laki-laki saya | KAH-kahq LAH-kee LAH-kee SAH-yah | my older brother

CARDS NOW: kakak saya = my older brother or sister [card] || kakak = older brother or sister [card] || adik saya = my younger brother or sister [card] || adik = younger brother or sister [card] || laki-laki = man; male [card] || perempuan = woman; female [card] || kakak laki-laki saya = my older brother || adik perempuan saya = my younger sister

### SECTION 2   (has 3)
Family words as "you"
 Several family words double as polite ways to say "you". You already know Bapak and Ibu . Kak (from kakak ) is a friendly "you" for a young person, common in shops and online. Mas and Mbak also mean older brother and older sister.
CARDS NOW: Kak = you (to a young person) [card] || Mas = you (to a young man) [card] || Mbak = you (to a young woman) [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 6)
The questions you will be asked
 Indonesians often ask Sudah menikah? (Are you married?, literally "already married?"). If you are not, the expected answer is Belum , "not yet", even if you have no plans. A flat Tidak sounds blunt. For children, people often put the number last: Anak saya dua , "my children (are) two".

 EX: Suami saya orang Bali. | soo-AH-mee SAH-yah OH-rahng BAH-lee | My husband is Balinese.

## LESSON time-words — 16 · sudah, sedang, akan, belum — deck "Home and routine"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
English changes the verb to show time: eat, ate, eating, will eat, have eaten. Indonesian never touches the verb. makan is always makan . When the time matters, a small time word goes in front of the verb.
 sudah: done, already
 sudah says something is finished. It covers English "have eaten", "already ate" and often plain "ate".
 EX: Saya sudah makan. | SAH-yah SOO-dahh MAH-kahn | I've already eaten.
CARDS NOW: makan = to eat [card] || sudah = already [card] || Saya sudah makan. = I've already eaten.

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
sedang: in the middle of it
 sedang is English "-ing" for something happening right now. If you are on the phone and someone asks what you are doing, this is the word you need.
 EX: Dia sedang mandi. | DEE-ah suh-DAHNG MAHN-dee | She's taking a shower.
CARDS NOW: sedang = in the middle of (doing) [card] || Dia sedang mandi. = She's taking a shower.

### SECTION 2   (has 3)
akan: will
 akan points to the future. In conversation it is often left out when a word like besok (tomorrow) already shows the time.
 EX: Kami akan pulang besok. | KAH-mee AH-kahn POO-lahng BEH-sohq | We'll go home tomorrow.
CARDS NOW: akan = will; going to [card] || besok = tomorrow [card] || Kami akan pulang besok. = We'll go home tomorrow.

### SECTION 3   (has 4)
belum: not yet
 belum is the negative of sudah . Indonesians use it far more than English uses "not yet": the polite answer to Sudah makan? is Belum , not Tidak , because it leaves the door open.
 EX: Saya belum makan. | SAH-yah buh-LOOM MAH-kahn | I haven't eaten yet.
CARDS NOW: Belum. = Not yet. [card] || Sudah makan? = Have you eaten? [card] || tidak = not; no [card] || Saya belum makan. = I haven't eaten yet. [card]

### SECTION 4   (has 4)
pernah and masih
 pernah means "ever, at some time", for experiences. belum pernah is "never (yet)". masih means "still", and it works before a place too, with no word for "is".
 EX: Saya belum pernah ke Bali. | SAH-yah buh-LOOM puhr-NAHH kuh BAH-lee | I've never been to Bali.
 EX: Dia masih di kantor. | DEE-ah MAH-seeh dee KAHN-tohr | He's still at the office.

CARDS NOW: pernah = ever; have done before [card] || masih = still [card] || Saya belum pernah ke Bali. = I've never been to Bali. || Dia masih di kantor. = He's still at the office.

### SECTION 5   (has 5)
Words for when
 Words such as kemarin (yesterday), tadi (earlier today), sekarang (now), nanti (later) and besok (tomorrow) can carry the time on their own. You can add sudah as well, but you do not have to. Just do not mix a future word with a past one.
 EX: Saya makan tadi. | SAH-yah MAH-kahn TAH-dee | I ate earlier.
 Most common mistake: mixing future and past, as if akan were a tense ending. Saya akan makan kemarin → Saya makan kemarin .

## LESSON ber — 17 · The ber- prefix — deck "Feelings and free time"
### SECTION 0   (has 10)
Many Indonesian verbs are a root word with a prefix (a few letters stuck on the front). English does a little of this ("re-do", "un-pack"); Indonesian does it all the time. The first prefix to know is ber- .
 What ber- verbs do
 A ber- verb mostly says what someone does or is doing, and it is usually complete without an object (a thing that the action is done to): you just work, swim or walk. A few, like belajar and bermain , can be followed by what you study or play. The verb still never changes for time.

 EX: Saya bekerja di Jakarta. | SAH-yah buh-kuhr-JAH dee jah-KAHR-tah | I work in Jakarta.
 EX: Dia belajar bahasa Indonesia. | DEE-ah buh-LAH-jahr bah-HAH-sah een-doh-neh-SEE-ah | She's studying Indonesian.
 EX: Mereka bermain bola. | muh-REH-kah buhr-MAH-een BOH-lah | They're playing soccer.

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Learn the spelling with the word
 The prefix is not always spelled ber- . Three very common verbs lose letters: bekerja (from kerja ), belajar and berenang (from renang ). Learn these as whole words rather than building them.
 EX: Kami berenang di pantai. | KAH-mee buh-ruh-NAHNG dee PAHN-tai | We swim at the beach.
CARDS NOW: kerja = work [card] || Kami berenang di pantai. = We swim at the beach.

### SECTION 2   (has 3)

## LESSON me — 18 · The me- prefix — deck "Work and study"
### SECTION 0   (has 16)
The second big prefix is me- . Where a ber- verb usually stands alone, a me- verb usually does something TO something: you read a book, write a letter, cook rice. In careful speech these verbs wear their prefix.
 EX: Saya membaca buku. | SAH-yah muhm-BAH-chah BOO-koo | I'm reading a book.

Tone words: dong, sih, kok, deh
 These small words add feeling, the way English uses tone of voice. dong pushes ("come on"), sih softens or adds "anyway", kok shows surprise ("how come?"), and deh settles things ("OK then").

gue and lo
 In Jakarta, friends say gue for "I" and lo for "you". Use them only with Jakarta friends your own age, never with strangers or older people. Learn to recognize them; you do not need to say them. Outside Jakarta they can sound out of place, and other regions have their own casual words.

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
When to switch
 Use standard Indonesian with strangers, older people, at work and in writing to a teacher, landlord or office. Casual forms are for friends, people your age and children. If someone older or a stranger switches to casual with you, you can follow them, but let them go first. Even in casual speech the noun still comes first and there is still no word for "is": Rumahku jauh .
 Most common mistake: using friends' words with an older person. Pak, gue nggak bisa datang → Pak, saya tidak bisa datang .
