## LESSON sounds — 1 · Sounds and spelling — deck "Sounds and spelling"
### SECTION 0   (has 5)
Tagalog (Filipinos also call it Filipino, the national language) is written in English letters, and most words are spelled the way they sound. Under every phrase in this course there is a pronunciation line in English letters. Keep it on: Tagalog spelling never shows two things the line does show, stress and the catch.
 Five short, pure vowels
 English vowels slide; Tagalog vowels stay put. The line spells them ah, eh, ee, oh, oo . Two vowel pairs blend: ay sounds like the i in bite , and aw like the ow in cow .
 EX: bahay | BAH-hai | house
 EX: ikaw | ee-KOW | you
CARDS NOW: Tagalog = Tagalog (the language) [card] || oo = yes [card] || ay = topic-first marker (formal) [card] || bahay = house [card] || ikaw = you (one friend; first in the sentence) [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Stress: the CAPITAL syllable
 One syllable in each word is louder and longer, and the line writes it in CAPITALS. English speakers guess the stress; in Tagalog a wrong guess can make another word. aso stressed on the first syllable is "dog"; stressed at the end (ah-SOH) it means "smoke". The spelling is the same, so learn the stress with each word.
 EX: aso | AH-soh | dog
CARDS NOW: aso = dog [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
The catch: q
 A q at the end of a word in the line is a catch in the throat, the break in "uh-oh". bata with a catch is "child"; with no catch (BAH-tah) it means "robe". You hear the catch before a pause. Inside a sentence it usually drops and the vowel just stretches.
 EX: bata | BAH-tahq | child
 EX: Hindi po. | heen-DEE pohq | No. (polite)
 A hyphen after mag- also stands for a catch: mag-aral (to study) has a small break before aral .
CARDS NOW: bata = child [card] || Hindi po. = No. (polite) [card] || mag-aral = to study [card] || aral = study [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 6)
ng and mga
 ng is one sound, the ng in singer , and it can start a word. The little word ng on its own is said nang , and mga , which makes a noun plural, is said mah-NGAH . Inside a word, ngg is that sound plus a hard g: mangga .
 EX: ngayon | ngah-YOHN | now
 EX: mga bata | mah-NGAH BAH-tahq | children
 EX: Kumain ako ng mangga. | koo-MAH-een ah-KOH nahng mahng-GAH | I ate a mango.
CARDS NOW: ng = of; a (non-topic marker) [card] || mga = plural marker [card] || mangga = mango [card] || ngayon = now [card] || mga bata = children [card] || Kumain ako ng mangga. = I ate a mango. [card]

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
A few more letters

 r ; one quick tap, like the tt in American butter ; aral (lesson) ; 
 ts ; ch as in church ; tsinelas (flip-flops) ; 
 siya ; usually sha , one syllable ; siya (he, she) ; 
 EX: tsinelas | chee-NEH-lahs | flip-flops
CARDS NOW: tsinelas = flip-flops; house slippers [card] || siya = he; she [card]

### SECTION 5   (has 5)
Two small joiners you will see everywhere
 English puts "big" straight before "house". Tagalog joins them with a linker: -ng on a word that ends in a vowel, na after a consonant. And for "Ana's", Tagalog puts ni before the owner's name, after the thing. Later lessons explain both; for now, read them as one phrase.
 EX: malaking bahay | mah-lah-KEENG BAH-hai | a big house
 EX: maliit na bahay | mah-lee-EET nah BAH-hai | a small house
 EX: aso ni Ana | AH-soh nee AH-nah | Ana's dog
 The line is a map; the voice is the real sound. Listen first, then read along.
 Most common mistake: reading mga letter by letter. "em-ga bata" → mga bata , said mah-NGAH BAH-tahq , with the ng of singer .
CARDS NOW: na = already; now (also the linker) [card] || ni = of; by (before a name) [card] || malaking bahay = big house [card] || maliit na bahay = a small house || aso ni Ana = Ana's dog [card]

## LESSON greetings — 2 · Greetings and respect: po and opo — deck "Greetings and respect"
### SECTION 0   (has 7)
Filipinos greet by the time of day, and they show respect in almost every sentence they say to an older person. Both are easy once you see the pattern.
 Good morning, good afternoon, good evening
 English says "good morning". Tagalog says "beautiful morning": maganda (beautiful, fine) joined to the time of day with the linker -ng . That is why every greeting starts with Magandang .
 When ; Greeting ; 
 morning ; Magandang umaga ; 
 around noon ; Magandang tanghali ; 
 afternoon ; Magandang hapon ; 
 evening and night ; Magandang gabi ; 
 EX: Magandang umaga po. | mah-gahn-DAHNG oo-MAH-gah pohq | Good morning. (polite)
 EX: Magandang hapon. | mah-gahn-DAHNG HAH-pohn | Good afternoon.
CARDS NOW: maganda = beautiful [card] || ng = of; a (non-topic marker) [card] || Magandang tanghali po. = Good day. (polite, around noon) [card] || Magandang hapon po. = Good afternoon. (polite) [card] || Magandang gabi po. = Good evening. (polite) [card] || Magandang hapon. = Good afternoon. || Magandang umaga po. = Good morning. (polite) [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 6)
po and opo: respect in one small word
 English shows respect with "sir" or "ma'am" now and then. Tagalog adds po to the sentence, right after the first word or phrase. Use it with anyone older than you, adult strangers, customers and staff you do not know, your parents and grandparents. Leave it out with friends, children and people younger than you. To say yes politely, say opo ; oo is the plain yes for friends.
 EX: Salamat po. | sah-LAH-maht pohq | Thank you. (polite)
 EX: Opo, Lola. | OH-pohq LOH-lah | Yes, Grandma.
 You will also hear ho and oho ; they mean the same as po and opo .
CARDS NOW: po = respect particle [card] || Opo. = Yes. (polite) [card] || oo = yes [card] || Salamat po. = Thank you. (polite) [card] || Opo, Lola. = Yes, Grandma. || ho = respect particle (less formal) [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 5)
How are you?
 To a friend: Kumusta ka? To an older person, Tagalog uses the plural "you", kayo , even for one person, together with po . The usual answer is Mabuti naman (I'm fine).
 EX: Kumusta ka? | koo-moos-TAH kah | How are you? (to a friend)
 EX: Kumusta po kayo? | koo-moos-TAH poh kah-YOH | How are you? (polite)
 EX: Mabuti naman po. | mah-BOO-tee nah-MAHN pohq | I'm fine. (polite)
CARDS NOW: Kumusta ka? = How are you? (to a friend) [card] || kayo = you (plural or polite) [card] || Mabuti naman. = I'm good. [card] || Kumusta po kayo? = How are you? (polite) [card] || Mabuti naman po. = I'm fine. (polite)

### SECTION 3   (has 4)
Thanks and goodbye
 Salamat is thank you, and the reply is Walang anuman (you're welcome). When someone leaves, Filipinos say Ingat! (take care) more often than Paalam (goodbye), which sounds formal.
 EX: Walang anuman. | wah-LAHNG ah-noo-MAHN | You're welcome.
 EX: Ingat po. | EE-ngaht pohq | Take care. (polite)
CARDS NOW: Walang anuman. = You're welcome. [card] || Ingat! = Take care! [card] || Paalam. = Goodbye. [card] || Ingat po. = Take care. (polite)

### SECTION 4   (has 5)
Titles: kuya, ate, tito, tita
 A young man a bit older than you, including a driver or a guard, is Kuya (big brother); a young woman is Ate (big sister, said AH-teh). Your parents' friends are Tito and Tita . In shops you will also hear sir and ma'am .
 EX: Salamat po, Ate. | sah-LAH-maht pohq AH-teh | Thank you, miss.
 Most common mistake: answering an elder with the plain yes. Oo, Lola. → Opo, Lola. And to an elder, Kumusta ka po? → Kumusta po kayo?
CARDS NOW: kuya = older brother [card] || ate = older sister [card] || tito = uncle [card] || tita = aunt [card] || Salamat po, Ate. = Thank you, miss. (to a young woman a bit older) [card]

(polite) || Pasensya na po. | pah-SEHN-shah nah pohq | Sorry. (polite) || Tuloy po kayo. | too-LOY poh kah-YOH | Please come in. (polite) || Mano po. | MAH-noh pohq | Bless me, please. (to an elder) || Ingat po kayo. | EE-ngaht poh kah-YOH | Take care. (polite)

## LESSON pronouns — 3 · ang pronouns: ako, ikaw / ka, siya, kami, tayo, kayo, sila — deck "Family and people"
### SECTION 0   (has 12)
These are the ang pronouns, the forms for the person a sentence is about. Later you will meet two more sets ( ko , akin …); for now, these seven do the work of English "I, you, he, she, we, they".
 English ; Tagalog ; 
 I ; ako ; 
 you (one friend) ; ikaw / ka ; 
 he, she ; siya ; 
 we (not you) ; kami ; 
 we (with you) ; tayo ; 
 you (several people, or one person politely) ; kayo ; 
 they ; sila ; 
 si before a name
 English says "I am Ana". Tagalog has no word for "am" here; it puts the little word si before the name: Ako si Ana . Every person's name gets si in this spot, never ang .
 EX: Ako si Ana. | ah-KOH see AH-nah | I'm Ana.
 EX: Siya si Juan. | shah see hwahn | He's Juan.

[card] || Siya si Juan. = This is Juan. [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
One word for he and she
 siya means both "he" and "she" (it is not used for things). The name or the situation tells you which.
CARDS NOW: (none)

### SECTION 2   (has 5)
ikaw or ka
 Both mean "you" for one friend. ikaw starts a sentence; ka never does, it comes right after the first word. The meaning is the same.
 EX: Ikaw si Mark. | ee-KOW see mahrk | You're Mark.
 EX: Si Mark ka. | see mahrk kah | You're Mark.
 Tagalog often puts the new information first and the pronoun last, the reverse of English: Guro ako is "I'm a teacher", word for word "teacher I".
 EX: Guro ako. | GOO-roh ah-KOH | I'm a teacher.
CARDS NOW: ikaw = you (one friend; first in the sentence) [card] || ka = you (one friend; after the first word) [card] || Ikaw si Mark. = You're Mark. || Si Mark ka. = You're Mark. || Guro ako. = I'm a teacher.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Two words for we
 English "we" can mean "me and others, not you" or "you and me". Tagalog keeps them apart. kami leaves the listener out; tayo includes them. Talking about your own family to a Filipino friend, say kami . Inviting everyone at the table to eat, say tayo .
 EX: Guro kami. | GOO-roh kah-MEE | We're teachers. (not you)
 EX: Kain tayo! | KAH-een TAH-yoh | Let's eat!
CARDS NOW: Guro kami. = We're teachers. (not you) || Kain tayo! = Let's eat! [card]

### SECTION 4   (has 3)
kayo: more than one, or polite
 kayo is "you" for a group, and also the respectful "you" for one older person, with po , as in Kumusta po kayo? 
 EX: Mga guro sila. | mah-NGAH GOO-roh see-LAH | They're teachers.
 Most common mistake: putting ang before a name. Ako ang Ana. → Ako si Ana. And ka never starts a sentence: Ka si Mark. → Ikaw si Mark.
CARDS NOW: po = respect particle [card] || Kumusta po kayo? = How are you? (polite) [card] || Mga guro sila. = They're teachers.

## LESSON intro — 4 · Introducing yourself: Ako si…, Taga-… ako — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 7)
When you meet Filipinos, the first questions are almost always the same: your name, where you are from, and often your age. Here are the questions and the answers.
 Your name
 You already know Ako si… with si before the name. To ask a friend's name, say Ano ang pangalan mo? (word for word "What the name your?"). In speech you will usually hear Anong pangalan mo? , with ano joined to the noun by the linker -ng (some people write ano'ng , short for ano ang ). To an older person: Ano po ang pangalan ninyo? 
 EX: Ano ang pangalan mo? | ah-NOH ahng pah-NGAH-lahn moh | What's your name? (to a friend)
 EX: Anong pangalan mo? | ah-NOHNG pah-NGAH-lahn moh | What's your name? (to a friend)
 EX: Ako si Carlo. | ah-KOH see KAHR-loh | I'm Carlo.
CARDS NOW: si = marker before a name (topic) [card] || Ano ang pangalan mo? = What's your name? (to a friend) [card] || Ano? = What? [card] || Anong pangalan mo? = What's your name? (to a friend) || ng = of; a (non-topic marker) [card] || Ano po ang pangalan ninyo? = What's your name? (polite) [card] || Ako si Carlo. = I'm Carlo.

### SECTION 1   (has 4)
Where you are from: taga-
 English says "I'm from Manila". Tagalog puts taga- (from) in front of the place, with a hyphen, and the pronoun at the end: Taga-Maynila ako . To ask, put taga- on saan (where). Answer with your city, your state or your country; a Filipino relative may also ask which province your family comes from.
 EX: Taga-saan ka? | tah-gah-sah-AHN kah | Where are you from? (to a friend)
 EX: Taga-saan po kayo? | tah-gah-sah-AHN poh kah-YOH | Where are you from? (polite)
 EX: Taga-Amerika ako. | tah-gah-ah-MEH-ree-kah ah-KOH | I'm from the US.
CARDS NOW: Saan? = Where? [card] || Taga-saan ka? = Where are you from? (to a friend) [card] || Taga-saan po kayo? = Where are you from? (polite) [card] || Taga-Amerika ako. = I'm from the US. [card]

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Nationality
 For nationality, the word itself comes first: Amerikano ako (I'm American), Pilipino ako (I'm Filipino). Many heritage learners say Pilipino-Amerikano ako .
 EX: Amerikano ako. | ah-meh-ree-KAH-noh ah-KOH | I'm American.
CARDS NOW: Amerikano ako. = I'm American. [card] || Pilipino-Amerikano ako. = I'm Filipino American. [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Learning Tagalog
 Learn this sentence as one block for now; the verb will make sense in the verb lessons. People will be delighted to hear it, and many will switch to slow, careful Tagalog for you.
 EX: Nag-aaral ako ng Tagalog. | nahg-ah-AH-rahl ah-KOH nahng tah-GAH-lohg | I'm studying Tagalog.
CARDS NOW: Nag-aaral ako ng Tagalog. = I'm studying Tagalog. [card]

### SECTION 4   (has 8)
Your age
 Filipinos ask age openly; it decides who gets po , kuya or ate . English asks "How old?"; Tagalog asks "How many years already?": ilan (how many) + the linker -g + taon (year). After a word ending in n , the linker is just -g .
 EX: Ilang taon ka na? | ee-LAHNG tah-OHN kah nah | How old are you? (to a friend)
 EX: Dalawampu't limang taon na ako. | dah-lah-wahm-POOT lee-MAHNG tah-OHN nah ah-KOH | I'm twenty-five.
 The numbers come in a later lesson; until then, you can answer with the English number, which everyone understands.
 Most common mistake: translating "How are you?" word for word. Paano ka? → Kumusta ka? And do not drop taga- : Ako ay Amerika. → Taga-Amerika ako.

(to a friend) [card] || Dalawampu't limang taon na ako. = I'm twenty-five. [card] || Kumusta ka? = How are you? (to a friend) [card]

## LESSON this-that — 5 · ito, iyan, iyon: this and that — deck "Shopping and the market"
### SECTION 0   (has 9)
At a market stall you will point and ask all day: what is this, how much is that. Tagalog has three words where English has two.
 Three distances
 English has "this" and "that". Tagalog splits "that" in two, by who is closer.
 Word ; Means ; Where the thing is ; 
 ito ; this ; near me, the speaker ; 
 iyan ; that ; near you, the listener ; 
 iyon ; that (over there) ; far from both of us ; 
 So if the vendor is holding the mango, it is iyan ; if it is on the shelf behind her, it is iyon . Ano asks "what", and Magkano asks "how much"; with a vendor, add po right after it. Pointing with your lips, a Filipino habit, works too, but the right word helps.
 EX: Ano ito? | ah-NOH ee-TOH | What's this?
 EX: Magkano po iyan? | mahg-KAH-noh poh ee-YAHN | How much is that? (polite)
 EX: Ano iyon? | ah-NOH ee-YOHN | What's that over there?
CARDS NOW: ito = this [card] || iyan = that (near you) [card] || iyon = that (over there) [card] || Ano? = What? [card] || magkano = how much (price) [card] || po = respect particle [card] || Ano ito? = What's this? [card] || Magkano po iyan? = How much is that? (polite) [card] || Ano iyon? = What's that over there?

### SECTION 1   (has 5)
This is…
 To name a thing, say Ito ang… (this is the…). For a person, use si before the name, as with the pronouns: Ito si Ana (this is Ana). You can also turn it around and put the pointing word last: Bahay ni Ana iyon .
 EX: Ito ang bahay ni Ana. | ee-TOH ahng BAH-hai nee AH-nah | This is Ana's house.
 EX: Iyan ang aso ni Juan. | ee-YAHN ahng AH-soh nee hwahn | That's Juan's dog.
 EX: Ito si Ana. | ee-TOH see AH-nah | This is Ana.
 EX: Bahay ni Ana iyon. | BAH-hai nee AH-nah ee-YOHN | That's Ana's house over there.
CARDS NOW: si = marker before a name (topic) [card] || Ito si Ana. = This is Ana. || Bahay ni Ana iyon. = That's Ana's house over there. || Ito ang bahay ni Ana. = This is Ana's house. || Iyan ang aso ni Juan. = That's Juan's dog.

### SECTION 2   (has 3)
For things, not siya
 English says "it". Tagalog does not use siya for things; it uses the pointing words. Mahal ito means "It's expensive" (word for word "expensive this").
 EX: Mahal ito. | mah-HAHL ee-TOH | This is expensive.
CARDS NOW: siya = he; she [card] || Mahal ito. = This is expensive. || mahal = expensive [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
Short forms you will hear
 In fast speech the words shrink: 'to , 'yan , 'yon . You will hear Ano 'yan? everywhere. Understand them, but write the full forms in this course. There are also other forms, nito , niyan and niyon (of this, of that), which you will meet later; for now just recognize them.
 Most common mistake: using siya for a thing. Mahal siya. (about a bag) → Mahal ito. And pick "that" by distance: something far away is iyon , not iyan .

## LESSON markers — 6 · Markers: ang / si, ng / ni, sa / kay — deck "Home and daily routine"
### SECTION 0   (has 9)
English shows who does what mostly by word order. Tagalog puts a small word, a marker, in front of almost every noun. The marker tells you the noun's job, so learn each noun together with its marker.
 Three jobs, two sets
 Ordinary nouns (house, teacher) take ang , ng or sa . A person's name takes si , ni or kay instead.
 Job ; Ordinary noun ; Name ; 
 the topic: what the sentence is about ; ang ; si ; 
 the owner (later also the doer or the thing) ; ng ; ni ; 
 the place, or the person something goes to ; sa ; kay ; 
 For two or more names there are plural forms: sina , nina , kina ( sina Ana at Juan , Ana and Juan).
CARDS NOW: ang = the (topic marker) [card] || ng = of; a (non-topic marker) [card] || sa = to; at; in; from [card] || si = marker before a name (topic) [card] || ni = of; by (before a name) [card] || kay = to; at (before a name) [card] || sina = marker before two or more names (topic) [card] || nina = of; by (two or more names) [card] || kina = to; at the place of (names) [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
ang and si: the topic
 English says "The house is big". Tagalog puts the description first and marks the topic with ang : "Big the house". A simple sentence has only one topic, so only one ang or si .
 EX: Malaki ang bahay. | mah-lah-KEE ahng BAH-hai | The house is big.
 EX: Guro si Ana. | GOO-roh see AH-nah | Ana is a teacher.
CARDS NOW: Malaki ang bahay. = The house is big. || bahay = house [card] || Guro si Ana. = Ana is a teacher.

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
ng and ni: the owner
 English puts the owner first with 's: "the teacher's house". Tagalog puts the thing first and the owner after it, with ng (said nang ) or, for a name, ni .
 EX: Ito ang bahay ng guro. | ee-TOH ahng BAH-hai nahng GOO-rohq | This is the teacher's house.
 EX: Malinis ang kusina ni Lola. | mah-LEE-nees ahng koo-SEE-nah nee LOH-lah | Lola's kitchen is clean.
CARDS NOW: Ito ang bahay ng guro. = This is the teacher's house. || bahay ni Lola = Lola's house [card] || Malinis ang kusina ni Lola. = Lola's kitchen is clean. || kusina = kitchen [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 3)

### SECTION 0   (has 8)
English uses two different verbs for two ideas: "there is" (There's rice) and "have" (I have a dog). Tagalog uses one small word for both: may . Its opposite is wala , which you met in the last lesson.
 may + noun

### SECTION 2   (has 5)

### SECTION 3   (has 4)

## LESSON word-order — 15 · Word order: predicate first, ay, and the little words — deck "Questions and little words"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
English almost always starts with the person or thing the sentence is about: "I am a teacher", "Ana is tired". Everyday Tagalog starts with the new information, the part that says something (the predicate), and puts the topic after it.
 Predicate first

## LESSON aspect — 18 · Aspect: completed, ongoing, future — deck "Work and school"
### SECTION 0   (has 5)
English verbs show time: past, present, future. Tagalog verbs show something slightly different, called aspect: has the action started, and is it finished? There are three forms, and every -um- and mag- verb has all three.
 Completed : started and finished ( ate ).
 Ongoing : started, not finished, or done regularly ( is eating , eats ).
 Future : not started yet ( will eat ). Grammar books call it "contemplated".
 Repeat the first syllable
 The key move is repeating the first syllable of the root: kain gives ka-kain , luto gives lu-luto . If the root starts with a vowel, repeat just that vowel: inom gives i-inom , aral gives a-aral .

 For -um- verbs: ongoing keeps -um- and repeats the syllable; future repeats the syllable and drops -um- . For mag- verbs: ongoing is nag- plus the repeat; future is mag- plus the repeat.

## LESSON requests — 24 · Requests and commands: paki-, huwag — deck "Health and help"
### SECTION 0   (has 6)
English softens a request with "please" and "could you". Tagalog has a "please" that goes inside the verb, and the polite po does the rest. These are the words you need when you ask for help, at home, in a shop or at the pharmacy.
