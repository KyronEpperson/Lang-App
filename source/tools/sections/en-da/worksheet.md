# Worksheet: en-da

## LESSON alphabet — 1 · The alphabet: æ, ø, å — deck "Sounds and spelling"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
Danish uses the Latin alphabet, plus three letters English does not have. There are 29 letters. The extras come last, after z: æ , ø and å . Each is its own letter. Swap in a or o, and the word changes.
 How to read the pronunciation line
 Under every Danish phrase in this course there is a pronunciation line in English letters. The syllable in CAPITALS is the stressed one. A one-syllable word stays in small letters. A hyphen breaks syllables. dh is soft d: not English th, and not a hard d. The tongue stays down. uh is a swallowed e, the weak vowel at the end of æble. The line is a careful Copenhagen reading. It does not mark the stød, a catch in the voice. A later lesson trains that by ear.
 EX: æble | AEB-luh | apple
 EX: mælk | maelk | milk
CARDS NOW: uh = ooh (cold, scary) [card] || æble = apple [card] || mælk = milk [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 4)
æ, ø and å
 English has no letter æ, so the eye reads it as a plus e. It is one letter. On the line it is ae , the vowel of bed, so it is not read as å. ø is not the English "er". The line writes oe : say "ay" with rounded lips. å is not a doubled a, and not a plain a. The line writes aw , the vowel of caught.
 EX: øl | oel | beer
 EX: år | aw | year
 EX: nej | nai | no
 EX: Hej. | hai | Hi.
CARDS NOW: øl = beer [card] || år = year [card] || nej = no [card] || hej = hi [card]

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
The accent on e
 You will sometimes see é , an e with an accent. It is not a new sound. It is stressed e, and the line writes eh . The mark tells the number one apart from the article: én with a common-gender noun, ét with a neuter noun. The spellings idé and café use it too. The line is unreliable for those two, so learn the sound from én and ét, and trust the voice for idé and café.
 EX: én | ehn | one (the number, not the article en)
 EX: ét | eht | one (the number, not the article et)
CARDS NOW: én = one [card] || ét = one (the number, not the article et)

### SECTION 3   (has 5)
Two little words in front of a noun
 Nouns take a small word in front. en is common gender: an en-word. et is neuter: an et-word. There is no safe rule yet. Learn the chunk: et æble , en øl , et år . A later lesson explains the split. Until then, the article is part of the word.
 EX: Det er et æble. | deh eh eht AEB-luh | It is an apple.
 Most common mistake: reading the new letters as English combinations. ar for a year → år , and the same for æ and ø: they are letters, not a plus e or an "er".
CARDS NOW: en = a; an (common gender) [card] || et = a; an (neuter) [card] || et æble = an apple [card] || en øl = a beer [card] || Det er et æble. = It is an apple.

## LESSON vowels — 2 · Vowels: many sounds, few letters — deck "Sounds and spelling"
### SECTION 0   (has 7)
English spells about fifteen vowel sounds with five letters, and the letters slide: say no slowly and the end is a w. Danish writes each vowel with one symbol on the pronunciation line. Long and short often share that symbol. The two that split are i and o . Learn the set as a key, then trust your ear where the line stays coarse.
 a, e, i, o, u
 a is the a of cat or bed, not the ah of father. tak uses that a. A stressed e is eh , the vowel of bed. An unstressed e is uh . A long i is ee , as in bil . A short i is eh, not ee. ikke is the word to hear: EH-guh. The doubled k has no puff, so the line writes g. A long o is oh , the vowel of go, without a strong glide. A short o is aw , the same symbol as å. ost is short. u is oo , the vowel of food, long or short. hus is hoos.
 EX: tak | tak | thanks
 EX: bil | beel | car
 EX: ikke | EH-guh | not
 EX: stor | stoh | big
 EX: ost | awst | cheese
 EX: hus | hoos | house
CARDS NOW: tak = thanks [card] || uh = ooh (cold, scary) [card] || bil = car || ikke = not [card] || ost = cheese || hus = house || stor = big; large [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
y, and the w that is not a glide
 y is ee said with rounded lips. The line writes ew . The w is the mark of that rounding. It is not the glide at the end of English few . by , a city, comes out bew. Round your lips for the ee, and do not add an English w after it.
 EX: by | bew | city
CARDS NOW: by = city

### SECTION 2   (has 5)
æ, ø, å, and the pairs that glide
 You already met æ, ø and å as letters. As vowels: æ is ae, the vowel of bed. ø is oe, ay with rounded lips, never "er". å is aw, the same symbol as short o. ej is ai , the vowel of eye, so hej and nej rhyme with eye. øj is oy , the vowel of boy.
 EX: mælk | maelk | milk
 EX: øl | oel | beer
 EX: høj | hoy | high
 EX: hej | hai | hi
CARDS NOW: hej = hi [card] || nej = no [card] || mælk = milk [card] || øl = beer [card] || høj = tall; high; loud [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 5)
far and får
 far , father, is fa . får , a sheep, is faw . å is a different vowel, not a catch in the voice. The line drops the final r of both, and the words stay different.
 EX: far | fa | father
 EX: får | faw | sheep
 en bil is an en-word, common gender. et hus and et får are et-words, neuter. Store each noun with en or et.
 EX: et får | eht faw | a sheep
 Most common mistake: giving every letter one English value. A sheep read as far → får , and ikke read with the ee of see → ikke with the e of bed.
CARDS NOW: far = father [card] || får = sheep [card] || en bil = a car [card] || et hus = a house [card] || et får = a sheep

## LESSON soft-d — 3 · Soft d, silent letters, and the stød — deck "Sounds and spelling"
### SECTION 0   (has 7)
English d is a hard stop. Danish d after a vowel is soft d, and the line writes dh . The tongue stays down. People often hear something close to an l. Do not aim at English th. mad is madh, gade is GA-dhuh, and rød is roedh. Doubled dd is soft d too.
 EX: mad | madh | food
 EX: gade | GA-dhuh | street
 EX: rød | roedh | red
 Silent d
 d after n, l or r is silent. land is lan. kold is kawl. rd drops the d and the r together, so mor and mord come out the same: moh. You still write the d.
 EX: land | lan | country
 EX: kold | kawl | cold
 EX: mor | moh | mother
 EX: mord | moh | murder
CARDS NOW: mad = food [card] || gade = street || rød = red [card] || land = country || kold = cold [card] || mor = mother || mord = murder

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
The stød, heard and not written
 The stød is a catch in the voice, a creak on some stressed syllables. It is the difference between hun (she) and hund (dog), and between mor and mord. The line gives each pair one string, hoon and moh, because a mark it cannot predict would be worse than no mark. Do not hunt for the catch in the spelling. Listen for it. Typing cannot hear it either, so this course will not ask you to choose hun or hund by spelling alone.
 EX: hun | hoon | she
 EX: hund | hoon | dog
CARDS NOW: hun = she [card] || hund = dog

### SECTION 2   (has 6)
Swallowed endings
 An unstressed e is uh, not an English "er". -er is uh too, and the r is not written: taler is TA-luh. -en is uhn: bilen is BEE-luhn. -et is uhdh, and the t of that ending is soft d: huset is HOO-suhdh. Those endings are how Danish says "the". The lesson on the definite ending will teach when to use them. For now, learn the sound. An r inside a cluster stays: bedre is BEH-dhruh, soft d and then r.
 EX: huset | HOO-suhdh | the house
 EX: bilen | BEE-luhn | the car
 EX: taler | TA-luh | speaks
 EX: bedre | BEH-dhruh | better
CARDS NOW: taler = speaks || en = a; an (common gender) [card] || bilen = the car [card] || et = a; an (neuter) [card] || huset = the house [card] || bedre = better

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
The puff, and the letters with no puff
 p, t and k at the start of a syllable have a puff of air. In Copenhagen that t can sound a little like ts. The line does not spell the puff, and it does not write ts. A doubled kk , pp or tt has no puff. The line writes g, b or d for those. ikke is EH-guh. drikker is DREH-guh. The short i is the e of bed, which you already met.
 EX: ikke | EH-guh | not
 EX: drikker | DREH-guh | drinks
 The nouns still carry their articles. gade is an en-word. land is an et-word. brød , a loaf, is an et-word, and the d is soft.
 EX: et brød | eht broedh | a loaf of bread
 Most common mistake: a hard English d in mad. mad with that d → mad with soft d, the dh on the line. The second trap is ignoring the catch that separates hund from hun.
CARDS NOW: ikke = not [card] || drikker = drinks || et brød = bread; loaf [card]

## LESSON greetings — 4 · Greetings: hej, tak, farvel — deck "Greetings and courtesy"
### SECTION 0   (has 7)
Hej works with anyone, at any hour. It is the greeting this course wants you to use. The clock greetings are real, and you will hear them, but hej is the one that never picks the wrong time of day.
 Morning, afternoon, evening, night
 English says "good morning". Danish has a word for each part of the day. Godmorgen is good morning. Goddag is the afternoon greeting, and it is a little stiff. Godaften is good evening. Godnat is good night. If you are unsure of the hour, say hej and you are done. To leave, hej hej is the casual goodbye. Farvel is a fuller goodbye.
 EX: Hej. | hai | Hi.
 EX: Godmorgen. | goh-MAW-uhn | Good morning.
 EX: Goddag. | goh-DA | Good afternoon.
 EX: Godaften. | goh-AF-uhn | Good evening.
 EX: Godnat. | goh-NAT | Good night.
 EX: Hej hej. | hai hai | Bye.
 EX: Farvel. | fa-VEHL | Goodbye.
CARDS NOW: hej = hi [card] || godmorgen = good morning [card] || goddag = good afternoon [card] || godaften = good evening [card] || godnat = good night [card] || hej hej = bye [card] || farvel = goodbye [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 8)
Thanks, and the words around it
 There is no everyday word for "please". Politeness is tak and gerne . Ja, gerne is yes, please. Nej tak is no, thanks. When the thanks goes both ways, say selv tak . That is thanks, you too, to one person or to several. When you did the favor, you're welcome is det var så lidt , and those words do not change either. Værsgo is what you say as you hand something over: here you go, to one person or to several. It also invites a person to go ahead. Undskyld is excuse me, and it is also sorry. The line is rough on undskyld. Trust the voice for that word.
 EX: Tak. | tak | Thanks.
 EX: Selv tak. | sehlv tak | Thanks, you too. (to one person)
 EX: Det var så lidt. | deh va saw leht | You're welcome. (to one person)
 EX: Værsgo. | VEHS-gaw | Here you go. (to one person)
 EX: Ja, gerne. | ya GEH-nuh | Yes, please.
 EX: Undskyld. | OON-skewl | Excuse me. Sorry.
 Venligst is a real word, and it is correct on a sign or in a letter. At a counter it sounds stiff. Do not build an order out of it. The café lesson will give you the full polite order. For now, tak and gerne do the work.
CARDS NOW: tak = thanks [card] || gerne = gladly [card] || ja, gerne = yes, I'd love to [card] || nej tak = no, thanks [card] || selv tak = thanks, you too (to one person) [card] || det var så lidt = you're welcome (to one person) [card] || værsgo = here you go (to one person) [card] || undskyld = sorry; excuse me [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 6)
du, and the you you only recognize
 English has one "you". Danish uses du with a stranger, a shop clerk, a doctor, a teacher and a friend. To one person, du is the word. Please is not a different pronoun. To several people the word is I , always a capital, and the pronouns lesson teaches it. De , and with it Dem and Deres , is the stiff, old-fashioned you. You may meet it in an old letter. Recognize it. Do not produce it, unless a prompt says it is a formal letter. In model answers those three words keep their capitals.
 A few nouns from the sound lessons are still worth saying with their articles. en øl is an en-word. et æble is an et-word. The articles stay chunks until the gender lesson.
 Most common mistake: venligst at the counter, or De to a stranger. venligst → Ja, gerne , and De → du .
CARDS NOW: du = you (to one person) [card] || de = they [card] || dem = them [card] || deres = their; theirs [card] || en øl = a beer [card] || et æble = an apple [card]

DECK CARDS IN NO SECTION (add by front, as a string, only where a section teaches them): ja tak | ya tak | yes, please || hvordan har du det | vaw-DAN ha doo deh | how are you? (to one person) || det går godt | deh gaw gawt | I'm doing well

## LESSON intro — 5 · Introducing yourself — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
Four sentences cover a first meeting. Each one starts with jeg , I. The pronunciation line writes jeg as yai, a diphthong, not "yeg" with a hard g.
 Your name
 English says "my name is". Danish says jeg hedder , literally "I am called". hedder already contains the meaning, so no extra "am" goes in the middle. Jeg hedder Anna. You can also say Jeg er Anna , "I am Anna". er is "am". When a prompt says to use hedder, stay with hedder. To ask one person their name, the chunk is Hvad hedder du? du is you, to one person. De does not go here.
 EX: Jeg hedder Anna. | yai HEH-dhuh A-na | My name is Anna.
 EX: Jeg er Anna. | yai eh A-na | I am Anna.
 EX: Hvad hedder du? | vadh HEH-dhuh doo | What's your name? (to one person)
CARDS NOW: jeg = I [card] || I = you (to several people) [card] || jeg hedder Anna = my name is Anna [card] || Jeg er Anna. = I am Anna. || jeg er = I am [card] || hvad hedder du = what's your name? (to one person) [card] || du = you (to one person) [card] || de = they [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 8)
Where you are from, and where you live
 Jeg kommer fra… is "I come from". The word is fra, not a copy of English "of". Jeg bor i… is "I live in". A city takes i , and that i is the preposition "in", said ee. It is not the capital I, which means you, to several people.
 EX: Jeg kommer fra Amerika. | yai KAW-muh fra A-muh-ree-ka | I come from America.
 EX: Jeg kommer fra Aarhus. | yai KAW-muh fra AW-hoos | I come from Aarhus.
 EX: Jeg bor i København. | yai boh ee koe-buhn-HAWN | I live in Copenhagen.
 EX: Jeg bor i Aalborg. | yai boh ee AWL-baw | I live in Aalborg.
 EX: Jeg bor i Odense. | yai boh ee OH-dhuhn-suh | I live in Odense.
 The cities in this course are København, Aarhus, Aalborg and Odense. Ordinary words use å, not aa. Aarhus, Aalborg and Aabenraa keep a local Aa at the start. å would not be wrong there. This course uses the local spelling. The country is Danmark .
CARDS NOW: jeg kommer fra = I come from [card] || jeg kommer fra Odense = I come from Odense [card] || jeg bor i = I live in [card] || jeg kommer fra Amerika = I come from America [card] || Jeg kommer fra Aarhus. = I come from Aarhus. || jeg bor i København = I live in Copenhagen [card] || Jeg bor i Aalborg. = I live in Aalborg. || Jeg bor i Odense. = I live in Odense.

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
The articles are still chunks
 You can already point at something: Det er et hus , it is a house. Det er en bil , it is a car. et and en are still chunks. hus is neuter, an et-word. bil is common gender, an en-word. The next lesson says why. Do not drop the article, and do not swap them.
 EX: Det er et hus. | deh eh eht hoos | It is a house.
 EX: Det er en bil. | deh eh ehn beel | It is a car.
 Most common mistake: an article in front of a nationality. Jeg er en amerikaner → Jeg er amerikaner . The nationality lesson finishes the pattern. A name needs no article either: Jeg hedder Anna.
CARDS NOW: Det er et hus. = It is a house. || det = it; that [card] || Det er en bil. = It is a car. || jeg er amerikaner = I'm American [card]

## LESSON en-et — 6 · en and et: the two genders — deck "en and et"
### SECTION 0   (has 13)
English has one word for "a". Danish has two, and every noun takes one of them. The two groups are common gender and neuter . Common gender takes the indefinite article en . This course calls that noun an en-word. Neuter takes et . That noun is an et-word. About 75 percent of nouns are common gender. The rest are neuter.
 No rule you can trust
 There is no sure rule from the ending, the meaning, or the English word. A few tendencies exist, and they stay tendencies. An ending in e proves nothing: æble ends in e and is neuter, et æble . Learn each noun with en or et the day you meet it. From this lesson on, every new noun in the course is stored that way. The articles you already met, on æble, øl, år, gade and land, were chunks. Now you know what the chunk was doing.

 en bog ; et æble ; 
 en øl ; et brød ; 
 en gade ; et land ; 
 en by ; et år ; 
 en kop ; et navn ; 
 EX: en bil | ehn beel | a car
 EX: et hus | eht hoos | a house
 EX: et æble | eht AEB-luh | an apple
 EX: en bog | ehn baw | a book
 EX: et brød | eht broedh | a loaf of bread
 EX: en by | ehn bew | a city

CARDS NOW: en = a; an (common gender) [card] || et = a; an (neuter) [card] || æble = apple [card] || et æble = an apple [card] || en bil = a car [card] || et hus = a house [card] || en bog = book [card] || en øl = a beer [card] || et brød = bread; loaf [card] || en gade = a street [card] || et land = a country [card] || en by = a city [card] || et navn = a name [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
The article you can see
 When you name something, the article is still there. Det er en bil. Det er et hus. The det does not change with the gender. The en or the et does. A cup of coffee follows the cup, not the coffee: kop is an en-word, so it is en kop kaffe . The o in kop is the short o, aw, the same symbol as å.
 EX: en kop kaffe | ehn kawp KA-fuh | a cup of coffee
 EX: Det er en by. | deh eh ehn bew | It is a city.
 EX: Det er et navn. | deh eh eht nawn | It is a name.
 The number "one" is én or ét, with the accent, when you truly mean the number and someone could confuse it with the article. Everywhere else, write en and et with no accent. The accent does not change the sound on the line: en and én are both ehn, et and ét are both eht.
 Most common mistake: one article for every noun. en hus → et hus . hus is neuter.
CARDS NOW: en kop kaffe = a cup of coffee [card] || Det er en by. = It is a city. || Det er et navn. = It is a name.

## LESSON pronouns — 7 · Pronouns: jeg, du, mig, dig — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 13)
English has I, you, he, she, we and they, and one "you" for a single person or a crowd. Danish splits "you", and it splits "it". The subject is who the sentence is about.

 jeg ; I ; 
 du ; you (to one person) ;

 I and i

me, you, him, her

 EX: sig | sai | himself, herself

### SECTION 2   (has 5)
det, den, de and der
 When you name something for the first time, the sentence is det er , whatever the gender. The en or the et carries the gender. Det er en bil. Det er et hus. bil is an en-word, common gender. hus is an et-word, neuter. de means they, and the line writes dee. det means it or that, and the line writes deh. der , there, is also deh. The line cannot tell det from der. You can, from the spelling and the sentence. A person you know is han or hun , not den. Anna is hun. den is for a common-gender thing you already know, and that pattern waits until the definite ending.
 EX: Det er en bil. | deh eh ehn beel | It is a car.
 EX: Det er et hus. | deh eh eht hoos | It is a house.
 EX: de arbejder | dee A-bai-dhuh | they work
 De , Dem and Deres are the stiff you again, always capital in a model answer. Recognize them in an old letter. Do not produce them.
 Most common mistake: de where English says "it", or a hard g in mig. de for "it" → det , and mig is mai, not a hard g.
CARDS NOW: der = there [card] || Det er en bil. = It is a car. || Det er et hus. = It is a house. || de arbejder = they work || deres = their; theirs [card]

## LESSON present — 8 · Present tense in -r — deck "Days and routines"
### SECTION 0   (has 7)
English changes the verb with the person, and it adds a second form for what is happening now: I work, she works, I am working. Danish has one present, and it covers both "I work" and "I am working". The form ends in -r for every person. There is no table of six endings to memorize.
 English ; Danish ; 
 I work ; jeg arbejder ; 
 you work (to one person) ; du arbejder ; 
 she works ; hun arbejder ; 
 we work ; vi arbejder ; 
 you work (to several people) ; I arbejder ; 
 they work ; de arbejder ; 
 EX: Jeg arbejder. | yai A-bai-dhuh | I work. I am working.
 EX: Hun arbejder. | hoon A-bai-dhuh | She works. She is working.
 EX: Vi arbejder. | vee A-bai-dhuh | We work.
 EX: de arbejder | dee A-bai-dhuh | they work
 The Danish column does not change. du, hun, vi, I and de take the same word jeg takes. er , the "am, is, are" you already use in Jeg er Anna , is the same for every person too. It does not grow an extra ending on top.
 No am … -ing
 Do not build "am" plus a verb. Jeg spiser is both "I eat" and "I am eating". If you want the picture of it going on, Danish often adds a posture: Jeg sidder og spiser , "I sit and eat". The little word og is "and", and the line writes it aw, not "ohg". The -r is in the spelling even when the line swallows it. spiser is SPEE-suh. sidder is SEH-dhuh, the same line as the plain form sidde, because -er is a swallowed ending and not an English "er".
 EX: Jeg spiser. | yai SPEE-suh | I eat. I am eating.
 EX: Jeg sidder og spiser. | yai SEH-dhuh aw SPEE-suh | I am eating. (I sit and eat)
CARDS NOW: jeg arbejder = I work [card] || Hun arbejder. = She works. She is working. || Vi arbejder. = We work. || de arbejder = they work || jeg spiser = I eat [card] || Jeg sidder og spiser. = I am eating. (I sit and eat) || og = and [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 5)
A few verbs you can use today
 drikker is drink. The doubled k has no puff, so the line writes g, and the i is the short one: DREH-guh. taler is speak. The language name takes a small letter: dansk , not a capital. læser is read. The noun after the verb still shows its gender. øl is an en-word. æble is an et-word. bog, a book, is an en-word.
 EX: Jeg drikker en øl. | yai DREH-guh ehn oel | I drink a beer. I am drinking a beer.
 EX: Jeg spiser et æble. | yai SPEE-suh eht AEB-luh | I eat an apple.
 EX: Jeg taler dansk. | yai TA-luh dansk | I speak Danish.
 EX: Jeg læser en bog. | yai LAE-suh ehn baw | I read a book. I am reading a book.
 Most common mistake: an English -ing, or a missing -r. Jeg er spiser → Jeg spiser , and Jeg arbejde → Jeg arbejder .

## LESSON negation — 9 · ikke: where it goes — deck "Sentences and ikke"
### SECTION 0   (has 11)
English builds a negative with do: "I do not eat", "she does not work". Danish has no do there. Do not insert gør . The word for not is ikke , and the whole problem is where it sits.
 After the verb in a main clause
 A main clause can stand alone. In a main clause the verb is the second element, and ikke goes after the verb . Jeg spiser ikke. Jeg arbejder ikke. The person stays first in this lesson. Something else in front, which the next lesson teaches, still leaves the verb as the second element. ikke still follows the verb.
 EX: Jeg spiser ikke. | yai SPEE-suh EH-guh | I don't eat.
 EX: Jeg arbejder ikke. | yai A-bai-dhuh EH-guh | I don't work.
 altid (always) and aldrig (never) sit in that same slot, after the verb. Jeg arbejder altid. Jeg spiser aldrig. The gender of a noun in the sentence does not move ikke. øl is an en-word, so "a beer" is en øl, and ikke still follows drikker. hus is neuter: Det er ikke et hus. 
 EX: Jeg arbejder altid. | yai A-bai-dhuh AL-teedh | I always work.
 EX: Jeg spiser aldrig. | yai SPEE-suh AL-dree | I never eat.
 EX: Jeg drikker ikke en øl. | yai DREH-guh EH-guh ehn oel | I don't drink a beer.
 EX: Det er ikke et hus. | deh eh EH-guh eht hoos | It is not a house.
CARDS NOW: ikke = not [card] || Jeg spiser ikke. = I don't eat. || jeg arbejder aldrig = I never work [card] || jeg arbejder ikke = I don't work [card] || altid = always [card] || aldrig = never [card] || Jeg arbejder altid. = I always work. || Jeg spiser aldrig. = I never eat. || øl = beer [card] || Det er ikke et hus. = It is not a house. || Jeg drikker ikke en øl. = I don't drink a beer.

### SECTION 1   (has 11)
Before the verb after at, fordi, hvis, når
 A subordinate clause hangs off another clause. It starts with at (that), fordi (because), hvis (if) or når (when). In a subordinate clause, ikke comes before the verb . altid and aldrig move with it. Anna siger, at hun ikke kommer. Not "at hun kommer ikke". This at means "that". Before hun, du or jeg the line writes it a .
 EX: Anna siger, at hun ikke kommer. | A-na SEE-yuh a hoon EH-guh KAW-muh | Anna says that she isn't coming.
 EX: Anna kommer, hvis hun ikke arbejder. | A-na KAW-muh vehs hoon EH-guh A-bai-dhuh | Anna comes if she isn't working.
 EX: Jeg spiser, når jeg ikke arbejder. | yai SPEE-suh naw yai EH-guh A-bai-dhuh | I eat when I'm not working.
 EX: Anna bor i Aarhus, fordi hun ikke bor i København. | A-na boh ee AW-hoos foh-DEE hoon EH-guh boh ee koe-buhn-HAWN | Anna lives in Aarhus, because she doesn't live in Copenhagen.
 This course writes the comma before at, fordi, hvis and når. You will see Danish without that comma. Leaving it out is still legal, if a text is consistent. The comma is where the order changes, which is why the course keeps it. A typed answer does not have to include it.
 Most common mistake: ikke in front of the verb, or a do-word copied from English. Jeg ikke spiser → Jeg spiser ikke , and at hun kommer ikke → at hun ikke kommer .
CARDS NOW: at = to (before a verb); that [card] || fordi = because [card] || hvis = if [card] || når = when (every time, or in the future) [card] || vi bor ikke i Aarhus = we don't live in Aarhus [card] || Anna siger, at hun ikke kommer. = Anna says that she isn't coming. || nu kommer jeg = I'm coming now [card] || i København bor jeg = in Copenhagen, I live [card] || Anna kommer, hvis hun ikke arbejder. = Anna comes if she isn't working. || Jeg spiser, når jeg ikke arbejder. = I eat when I'm not working. || Anna bor i Aarhus, fordi hun ikke bor i København. = Anna lives in Aarhus, because she doesn't live in Copenhagen.

## LESSON inversion — 10 · Verb second: I dag arbejder jeg — deck "Sentences and ikke"
### SECTION 0   (has 11)
English likes the person first: today I work, tomorrow I'm coming, in Copenhagen I live. Danish keeps the verb in one slot and lets the person move around it. You already put ikke after the verb when the person is first. This lesson is what happens when something else stands in front.
 The verb is the second element
 Call it verb second. A plain sentence still starts with the person: Jeg arbejder i dag. If you start with today, tomorrow, here, or a place, that opening is the first element and the verb is still next. The person follows the verb.
 Count elements, not words. i dag is two words and one element. i morgen is one element, and it means tomorrow. i København is one element. The verb is the second element, not the second word.
 EX: Jeg arbejder i dag. | yai A-bai-dhuh ee da | I work today.
 EX: I dag arbejder jeg. | ee da A-bai-dhuh yai | Today I'm working.
 EX: I morgen kommer jeg. | ee MOH-guhn KAW-muh yai | I'm coming tomorrow.
 EX: Her bor jeg. | heh boh yai | I live here.
 EX: I København bor jeg. | ee koe-buhn-HAWN boh yai | In Copenhagen, I live.
 The stress on København in the line is a guess. Trust the voice. ikke still follows the verb when the person has moved: I dag arbejder jeg ikke. Same slot as in Jeg arbejder ikke. 
 EX: I dag arbejder jeg ikke. | ee da A-bai-dhuh yai EH-guh | Today I don't work.
CARDS NOW: ikke = not [card] || Jeg arbejder i dag. = I work today. || nu kommer jeg = I'm coming now [card] || i dag = today [card] || i morgen = tomorrow [card] || i dag arbejder jeg = today I'm working [card] || i morgen kommer jeg = I'm coming tomorrow [card] || her bor jeg = I live here [card] || i København bor jeg = in Copenhagen, I live [card] || I dag arbejder jeg ikke. = Today I don't work. || jeg arbejder ikke = I don't work [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
A whole clause can be the first element
 A clause with hvis or når can stand in front. The whole clause is the first element, so the verb of the main clause comes right after the comma, and the person follows that verb. Inside the hvis-clause, ikke comes before the verb , which you already practiced.
 EX: Hvis du ikke kommer, går jeg. | vehs doo EH-guh KAW-muh gaw yai | If you don't come, I'll leave. (to one person)
 Both orders are ordinary Danish. Start with the person, or start with the time or the place. Either way the verb stays the second element. Pick the order the sentence is asking for, and do not park the person between the opener and the verb.
 Most common mistake: leaving the person in the English slot. I dag jeg arbejder → I dag arbejder jeg .
CARDS NOW: hvis = if [card] || når = when (every time, or in the future) [card] || hvis du ikke kommer, går jeg = if you don't come, I'll leave (to one person) [card]

## LESSON questions — 11 · Questions: hvad, hvor, and yes/no — deck "Questions"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
English builds most questions with do: Do you work? Where do you live? Danish has no do there. Do not insert gør . A question is another kind of verb second: something stands in front of the verb, or the verb itself moves to the front.
 Yes or no: start with the verb
 A yes/no question starts with the verb. The person follows it. Arbejder du? is "do you work?" to one person. Taler du dansk? is "do you speak Danish?" To several people the word is I , always a capital: Arbejder I? A small i is the preposition "in". On the line they are the same sound. Write the capital anyway.
 EX: Arbejder du? | A-bai-dhuh doo | Do you work? (to one person)
 EX: Taler du dansk? | TA-luh doo dansk | Do you speak Danish? (to one person)
 EX: Arbejder I? | A-bai-dhuh ee | Do you work? (to several people)
 EX: Bor du i København? | boh doo ee koe-buhn-HAWN | Do you live in Copenhagen? (to one person)
CARDS NOW: Arbejder du? = Do you work? (to one person) || taler du dansk = do you speak Danish? (to one person) [card] || Arbejder I? = Do you work? (to several people) || Bor du i København? = Do you live in Copenhagen? (to one person)

The question word, then the verb
 A question word is the first element. The verb is the second element, and the person follows the verb. That is the same order as I dag arbejder jeg , with the question word in the opening slot.

## LESSON nationality — 12 · Nationalities and languages — deck "Nationalities and languages"
### SECTION 0   (has 11)
English says "I am an American" and "I speak English", with a capital on the language. Danish drops the article after er , and it writes the language, the nationality, and the adjective with a small letter. The country keeps its capital.
 No article after er
 A man says Jeg er amerikaner and Jeg er dansker and Jeg er englænder . There is no en in that sentence. Anyone can use the adjective instead, and the article stays off: Jeg er amerikansk , Jeg er dansk . The adjective works for any person. The noun with the article is a different sentence: en dansker is "a Dane", en amerikaner is "an American", en englænder is "an Englishman". Those are common gender. You use them when you mean the person as a noun, not right after er.
 EX: Jeg er amerikaner. | yai eh A-muh-ree-ka-nuh | I'm American.
 EX: Jeg er amerikansk. | yai eh A-muh-ree-kansk | I'm American.
 EX: Jeg er dansker. | yai eh DAN-skuh | I'm a Dane.
 EX: en dansker | ehn DAN-skuh | a Dane
 EX: Jeg er englænder. | yai eh EHNG-lae-nuh | I'm English.
 EX: Er du dansker? | eh doo DAN-skuh | Are you a Dane? (to one person)
 The stress on amerikaner, amerikansk, and Amerika in the line is a guess. Trust the voice. A question you already know still has no en: Er du dansker? The verb is first, and the article stays out.

### SECTION 1   (has 4)
A trade after er
 A trade after er takes no indefinite article, the same way a nationality does. Jeg er tømrer means I am a carpenter. The article comes back with an adjective: Han er en dygtig tømrer. The line's reading of dygtig is rough. Follow the voice.
 EX: Jeg er tømrer. | yai eh TOEM-uh | I am a carpenter.
 EX: Han er en dygtig tømrer. | han eh ehn DEW-teey TOEM-uh | He is a skilled carpenter.

### SECTION 2   (has 12)
Prepositions English moves
 venter takes på , not for. ringer takes til . The person after til is the object form. To one person that is dig . To several people that is jer . du and I are the subject forms, so ringer til du is the wrong shape. glade takes for , not med. One person is glad . Several people are glade , the adjective ending -e .
 EX: Jeg venter på bussen. | yai VEHN-tuh paw BOO-suhn | I am waiting for the bus.
 EX: Jeg ringer til dig. | yai REHNG-uh tehl dai | I am calling you (to one person).
 EX: Jeg ringer til jer. | yai REHNG-uh tehl yeh | I am calling you (to several people).
 EX: Jeg er glad for gaven. | yai eh gladh foh GA-wuhn | I am glad about the present.
 EX: Vi er glade for gaven. | vee eh GLA-dhuh foh GA-wuhn | We are glad about the present.

### SECTION 3   (has 6)
The two rules in these sentences
 A fronted phrase is still verb second. til sidst or i dag is the first element, and the verb is the second element. hus stays an et-word: et hus . bus is an en-word, and the bus is bussen . In a main clause, ikke goes after the verb. In a subordinate clause, ikke comes before the verb.
 EX: Til sidst fik jeg et hus. | tehl sehst fehk yai eht hoos | In the end I got a house.
 EX: I dag venter jeg på bussen. | ee da VEHN-tuh yai paw BOO-suhn | Today I am waiting for the bus.
 EX: Han siger, at han ikke venter. | han SEE-yuh a han EH-guh VEHN-tuh | He says that he is not waiting.
 Most common mistake: the English meaning of a look-alike, or for after venter. Tak for din gift → Tak for gaven. Jeg venter for bussen → Jeg venter på bussen.
