# Worksheet: en-cs

## LESSON alphabet — 1 · Alphabet and the háček — deck "Háček letters"
### SECTION 0   (has 6)
Czech uses the same alphabet as English, plus a few marks. The good news: each letter almost always has one sound, so once you know the letters you can read any word aloud. Under every Czech phrase in this course there is a line in English letters that tells you how to say it.
 The háček: a little hook that changes the sound
 The small hook on č š ž ř ě ď ť ň is called a háček . It is not decoration. Without it you get a different sound, and often a different word.
 Letter ; Sounds like ; In the line ; Example ; 
 č ; ch in church ; ch ; čaj ; 
 š ; sh in shop ; sh ; šest ; 
 ž ; s in measure ; zh ; žena ; 
 ř ; a buzzing r (lesson 3) ; rzh or rsh ; řeka ; 
 ď ť ň ; d, t, n with a y glued on ; dy, ty, ny ; děkuji ; 
 EX: čaj | chahy | tea
 EX: šest | shehst | six
 EX: žena | ZHEH-nah | a woman
 ě is e with a y before it. After d , t and n it softens the consonant instead: dě sounds like dye . After m it sounds like mnye: město (a town).
 EX: děkuji | DYEH-koo-yih | thank you
CARDS NOW: čaj = tea [card] || šest = six [card] || žena = a woman [card] || řeka = a river [card] || děkuji = thank you [card] || město = a town [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
c and ch: the two big surprises
 English reads c as k or s. Czech c is always ts , as in cats . And Czech ch is not the English ch at all: it is a rough kh , like the end of Scottish loch . The English ch sound is written č .
 EX: cena | TSEH-nah | a price
 EX: chléb | khlehhp | bread
CARDS NOW: cena = a price [card] || chléb = bread [card]

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
How to read the pronunciation line
 y in the line is the Czech letter j , the y of yes : já (I) is yaah.
 j in the line is the rare Czech dž , as in jam .
 CAPITALS show the beat. In Czech the beat is on the first syllable of a word. It is not about a long vowel, and not about shouting.
 rr and ll mean the r or l itself is the vowel: vlk (a wolf) is vllk, with no vowel in it.
CARDS NOW: já = I [card] || vlk = a wolf [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
ten, ta, to
 Czech nouns come with a little partner word, which works like "the" or "this": ten čaj , ta žena , to město (the town). Which one a noun takes is part of the word. The gender lesson explains why. For now, learn these with their partner: ten čaj , ten chléb , ta žena , ta cena , ta řeka (the river), to město .
 EX: ta žena | tah ZHEH-nah | the woman
 EX: to město | toh MNYEHS-toh | the town
 Most common mistake: reading Czech ch like English church . chléb with the ch of church → chléb with a rough kh, as in loch . The church sound is č .
CARDS NOW: ten čaj = the tea [card] || ta žena = the woman; that woman [card] || to město = the town [card]

## LESSON vowels — 2 · Long and short vowels — deck "Long and short vowels"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
Czech has five vowels, and each comes in a short and a long version. The long one carries a mark: á é í ó ú ů ý . English speakers tend to skip the mark. In Czech that gives you a different word.
 Long means held longer
 A long vowel is the same sound, held about twice as long. In the pronunciation line a short a is ah and a long á is aah; a short i or y is ih, as in sit , and a long í or ý is ee, as in seat .
 Short ; Long ; In the line ; 
 a ; á ; ah, aah ; 
 e ; é ; eh, ehh ; 
 i, y ; í, ý ; ih, ee ; 
 o ; ó ; oh, ohh ; 
 u ; ú, ů ; oo, ooh ; 
 EX: byt | biht | a flat; an apartment
 EX: být | beet | to be
 EX: pas | pahs | a passport
 EX: pás | paahs | a belt
CARDS NOW: byt = a flat; an apartment [card] || být = to be [card] || pas = a passport [card] || pás = a belt; a strip [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
The beat stays on the first syllable
 English moves the beat around: PHOtograph , phoTOgraphy . Czech puts it on the first syllable, every time. A long vowel later in the word does not pull the beat to itself: dobrý (good) is short then long, with the beat on the short do . Don't shout the CAPITALS, either. The Czech beat is light, and the syllable after it often sounds just as strong.
 EX: dobrý | DOH-bree | good
 EX: Dobrý den. | DOH-bree dehn | Hello. (Good day.)
CARDS NOW: dobrý = good (masculine) [card] || do = to; into [card] || Dobrý den. = Good day. [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 3)
ů and ú: one sound, two spellings
 ů , with a ring, and ú sound the same: a long ooh. ú is written at the start of a word, ů in the middle or at the end. In other forms of the same word the ring often turns into a short o : dům (a house), but do domu (into the house).
 EX: dům | doohm | a house
 EX: do domu | DOH doh-moo | into the house
CARDS NOW: dům = a house [card] || do domu = into the house || domu = to the house; of the house [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
i and y: one sound, two letters
 i and y sound the same, and so do í and ý : být (to be) and bít (to hit) sound identical. The spelling still counts. You write a word the way it is spelled, and only i softens a d , t or n in front of it.
CARDS NOW: bít = to hit [card]

### SECTION 4   (has 3)
Long endings that match
 Words like dobrý and dlouhý (long) change their last vowel to match the noun's partner word: ten dobrý čaj , ta dobrá káva (the good coffee), to dobré pivo (the good beer), ten dlouhý den (the long day), ta dlouhá řeka . The gender lesson explains it. For now, notice that all three endings are long.
 EX: ta dlouhá řeka | tah DLOHW-haah RZHEH-kah | the long river
 Most common mistake: dropping the length mark. byt for "to be" → být . Without the mark, byt means an apartment.
CARDS NOW: dlouhý = long (masculine) [card] || káva = coffee [card] || ta dlouhá řeka = the long river

## LESSON r-ch — 3 · ř, ch, and the end of a word — deck "ř and ch"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
This lesson covers the sounds that make Czech sound Czech: the famous ř , the letter ch , what happens at the end of a word, and words with no vowel at all.
 ř: an r and a zh at once
 ř is a quick trilled r with a buzzing zh, as in measure , on top. The line writes it rzh. Next to a voiceless sound like t or p , and at the end of a word, the buzz drops out and it sounds like rsh. A plain English r is wrong: r is a different letter.
 EX: řeka | RZHEH-kah | a river
 EX: tři | trshih | three
 EX: dobře | DOH-brzheh | well
CARDS NOW: řeka = a river [card] || tři = three [card] || dobře = well [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
ch is kh
 Czech ch is one sound: a rough kh from the back of the mouth, as in Scottish loch or German Bach . It is never the ch of church (that is č ) and never a plain k.
 EX: chléb | khlehhp | bread
CARDS NOW: chléb = bread [card]

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
The end of a word goes quiet
 English keeps the buzz of d, b, z and v at the end of a word: bad , cab . Czech swaps them for their quiet partners: d becomes t, b becomes p, z becomes s, v becomes f, ž becomes sh and h becomes kh. So led (ice) and let (a flight) sound exactly the same, and chléb ends in a p. The spelling stays the same; only the sound changes.
 EX: led | leht | ice
 EX: let | leht | a flight
CARDS NOW: led = ice [card] || let = a flight [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 3)
Words with no vowel
 In Czech, r and l can carry a syllable on their own. vlk (a wolf) is one syllable, with the l doing the vowel's job. krk (a neck) and čtvrtek (Thursday) work the same way. Don't slip in an "uh": the line writes rr and ll to remind you.
 EX: vlk | vllk | a wolf
 EX: čtvrtek | CHTVRR-tehk | Thursday
CARDS NOW: vlk = a wolf [card] || krk = a neck [card] || čtvrtek = Thursday [card]

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
p, t, k with no puff
 English p, t and k at the start of a word come with a small puff of air. Czech p , t and k have none. Hold a hand in front of your mouth: for pivo you should feel almost nothing. The line can't show this, so listen to the audio.
CARDS NOW: pivo = beer [card]

### SECTION 5   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Partner words again
 Keep learning nouns with ten, ta or to: ten chléb , ten led , ten vlk , ten čtvrtek , ta řeka . A word like dlouhý (long) matches too: ten dlouhý čtvrtek , ta dlouhá řeka .
 EX: ten dlouhý čtvrtek | tehn DLOHW-hee CHTVRR-tehk | that long Thursday
 Most common mistake: adding a vowel to a cluster. vlk said as "vuh-lk" → vlk , one syllable, with the l as the vowel.
CARDS NOW: dlouhý = long (masculine) [card] || ten dlouhý čtvrtek = that long Thursday

## LESSON greetings — 4 · Greetings: vy and ty — deck "Greetings and courtesy"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
Czech greetings are easy to learn and easy to get wrong, because the one you hear most among friends is the one you should not use with strangers.
 Dobrý den: your all-day hello
 English says "hello" to everyone. Czech says Dobrý den , "good day", to anyone you don't know well: in a shop, an office, an elevator, to a neighbor. It works from morning to evening. When you leave, say Na shledanou . Notice the beat: it falls on na .
 EX: Dobrý den. | DOH-bree dehn | Hello. (Good day.)
 EX: Na shledanou. | NAH skhleh-dah-nohw | Goodbye.
CARDS NOW: Dobrý den. = Good day. [card] || Na shledanou. = Goodbye. [card] || na = on; at; to [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 10)
The time of day
 Early in the morning you can say Dobré ráno , and in the evening Dobrý večer . Dobré odpoledne (good afternoon) exists, but most people just say Dobrý den . Dobrou noc is for bedtime, or for leaving late at night. Notice that "good" changes its ending: den and večer take dobrý , ráno and odpoledne take dobré . The gender lesson explains why.
 EX: Dobré ráno. | DOH-brehh RAAH-noh | Good morning.
 EX: Dobrý večer. | DOH-bree VEH-chehr | Good evening.
 EX: Dobrou noc. | DOH-brohw nohts | Good night.
CARDS NOW: Dobré ráno. = Good morning. [card] || dobré = good (neuter) [card] || ráno = the morning [card] || Dobrý večer. = Good evening. [card] || dobrý = good (masculine) [card] || večer = the evening [card] || Dobré odpoledne. = Good afternoon. [card] || odpoledne = the afternoon [card] || den = a day [card] || Dobrou noc. = Good night. [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 8)
Two kinds of "you"
 English has one "you". Czech has two. vy is for anyone you don't know well, for older people, and for more than one person. ty is for friends, family and children. The verb changes with it: Jak se máte? is the vy question, Jak se máš? the ty one. In this course you say the vy forms, and you learn to recognize ty.
 EX: Jak se máte? | yahk seh MAAH-teh | How are you?
 EX: Dobře, děkuji. A vy? | DOH-brzheh DYEH-koo-yih ah vih | Fine, thanks. And you?
 Between friends, hello and goodbye are both Ahoj . A Czech who asks Můžeme si tykat? is offering to switch to ty: after that, ahoj is fine.
CARDS NOW: vy = you (polite, or more than one) [card] || ty = you (one friend) [card] || Jak se máte? = How are you? [card] || Dobře, děkuji. A vy? = Fine, thanks. And you? || Dobře, děkuji. = Fine, thank you. [card] || Děkuji. = Thank you. [card] || Ahoj. = Hi. [card] || Můžeme si tykat? = May we use the familiar you? [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Small polite words
 Děkuji is "thank you"; you will also hear Děkuju , which is just as correct. Prosím does three jobs: "please", "you're welcome" and, as you hand something over, "here you are".
 EX: Děkuji. | DYEH-koo-yih | Thank you.
 EX: Prosím. | PROH-seem | Please. You're welcome.
 In Prague you will hear everyday spoken Czech, with forms such as dobrej for dobrý . Understand it, but this course teaches and accepts the standard forms.
 Most common mistake: ahoj to a stranger. Ahoj! to a shop assistant → Dobrý den. Keep ahoj for friends.
CARDS NOW: Prosím. = Please. [card]

## LESSON byt — 5 · být: jsem, jste, je — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
English says "I am, you are, she is". Czech být (to be) also changes with the person, with one big difference: the word for "I" is usually left out, because the verb already says who it is.
 The forms
 English ; Czech ; 
 I am ; (já) jsem ; 
 you are (a friend) ; (ty) jsi ; 
 he, she, it is ; (on, ona, ono) je ; 
 we are ; (my) jsme ; 
 you are (vy) ; (vy) jste ; 
 they are ; (oni) jsou ; 
 Jsem already means "I am". Já jsem is for stress: "I am (and he isn't)". Most of the time, just say Jsem .
 EX: Jsem student. | ysehm STOO-dehnt | I'm a student. (a man)
 EX: Jsem studentka. | ysehm STOO-dehnt-kah | I'm a student. (a woman)
 Notice the two words: a man is a student , a woman a studentka . Many words for people work this way. Notice too what is missing: English needs "a" in "a student", but Czech has no such word, so Jsem student is the whole sentence.
CARDS NOW: být = to be [card] || Jsem student. = I am a student. [card] || Jsem studentka. = I am a student (a woman). [card] || studentka = a student (a woman) [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
jste, even for one person
 When you say vy to one stranger, the verb is jste , the same as for a group. It feels odd at first, like saying "you all are" to one person, but it is simply the polite form. jsi is the ty form: you will hear it among friends, but you say jste . A question can be the same words as a statement, said with a rising voice.
 EX: Jste student? | ysteh STOO-dehnt | Are you a student? (to a man)
 EX: Jste unavená? | ysteh OO-nah-veh-naah | Are you tired? (to a woman)
CARDS NOW: Jsi unavená? = Are you tired? (a friend, to a woman) [card] || Jste student? = Are you a student? (to a man) || Jste unavená? = Are you tired? (to a woman) [card]

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
He, she, they
 For one other person, the form is je , whether it is a man, a woman or a thing. For two or more, it is jsou .
 EX: Petr je student. | PEH-trr yeh STOO-dehnt | Petr is a student.
 EX: Eva a Petr jsou studenti. | EH-vah ah PEH-trr ysohw STOO-dehn-tyih | Eva and Petr are students.
CARDS NOW: Petr je student. = Petr is a student. || Eva a Petr jsou studenti. = Eva and Petr are students.

### SECTION 3   (has 6)
To je: this is, that is
 To je means "this is" or "that is". Czech has no word for "a" or "the", so nothing else goes in: To je Petr , To je čaj . To ask, put kdo (who) or co (what) in front: Kdo je to? for a person, Co je to? for a thing. You will also hear Kdo to je? and Co to je? , which mean the same.
 EX: To je Petr. | toh yeh PEH-trr | This is Petr.
 EX: Kdo je to? | gdoh yeh toh | Who is it?
 EX: Co je to? | tsoh yeh toh | What is it?
 Most common mistake: leaving out the verb. Já student. → Jsem student. Czech needs jsem , and usually drops já .
CARDS NOW: To je Petr. = This is Petr. || kdo = who [card] || co = what [card] || Kdo je to? = Who is that? [card] || Co je to? = What is that? [card] || já = I [card]

## LESSON intro — 6 · Introducing yourself — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
This lesson puts together what you need to introduce yourself: your name, where you are from, what you do and the languages you speak.
 Your name
 English says "My name is Eva". Czech says Jmenuji se Eva , roughly "I call myself Eva". Jmenuju se is the same word in its everyday spelling, and both are correct. To ask a stranger, say Jak se jmenujete? Among friends you will hear Jak se jmenuješ? , but you say the vy form.
 EX: Jmenuji se Eva. | YMEH-noo-yih seh EH-vah | My name is Eva.
 EX: Jak se jmenujete? | yahk seh YMEH-noo-yeh-teh | What is your name?
 EX: Těší mě. | TYEH-shee mnyeh | Nice to meet you.
CARDS NOW: Jmenuji se Eva. = My name is Eva. [card] || Jak se jmenujete? = What is your name? [card] || Těší mě. = Nice to meet you. [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 5)
Where you are from
 To ask where someone is from, say Odkud jste? The answer is Jsem z… plus the place. After z (from), the place name changes its ending: Praha becomes z Prahy , Brno becomes z Brna , Amerika becomes z Ameriky . For now, learn each one as a whole chunk.
 EX: Odkud jste? | OHT-koot ysteh | Where are you from?
 EX: Jsem z Prahy. | ysehm SPRAH-hih | I'm from Prague.
 EX: Jsem z Ameriky. | ysehm SAH-meh-rih-kih | I'm from America.
CARDS NOW: Odkud jste? = Where are you from? || Jsem z Brna. = I am from Brno. [card] || Brno = Brno [card] || Jsem z Prahy. = I am from Prague. [card] || Jsem z Ameriky. = I'm from America.

Languages

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)

## LESSON gender — 7 · Gender: ten, ta, to — deck "People and family"
### SECTION 0   (has 9)
English has one "the" for everything. Czech has no word for "a" or "the" at all, but every noun has a gender: masculine, feminine or neuter. You have already met the partner words ten , ta and to . Here is what they are.
 ten, ta, to
 ten goes with masculine nouns, ta with feminine nouns and to with neuter nouns. They mean "the", "this" or "that", and you use them when you point at something or have already mentioned it. Gender is grammar, not meaning: tea is masculine, coffee is feminine and beer is neuter.
 Masculine ; Feminine ; Neuter ; 
 ten muž ; ta žena ; to dítě ; 
 ten čaj ; ta káva ; to pivo ; 
 EX: ten muž | tehn moosh | the man
 EX: ta káva | tah KAAH-vah | the coffee
 EX: to pivo | toh PIH-voh | the beer
CARDS NOW: ten = that; the (masculine) [card] || ta = that; the (feminine) [card] || to = it; this; that [card] || ten muž = the man [card] || ta žena = the woman; that woman [card] || to dítě = the child [card] || ten čaj = the tea [card] || ta káva = the coffee [card] || to pivo = the beer [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Guess from the ending
 Ends in a consonant: usually masculine. ten čaj , ten chléb , ten student .
 Ends in -a : usually feminine. ta žena , ta káva , ta studentka .
 Ends in -o : usually neuter. to pivo , to město .
CARDS NOW: to město = the town [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 3)
People follow the person
 For people, the gender follows the person. ten muž (the man) is masculine, and so is ten táta (the dad), even though it ends in -a . A woman's word, like studentka or učitelka , is feminine.
 EX: ten táta | tehn TAAH-tah | the dad
CARDS NOW: ten táta = the dad || studentka = a student (a woman) [card] || učitelka = a teacher (a woman) [card]

### SECTION 0   (has 7)
This last lesson adds no new grammar. It collects the words and sentence shapes where English quietly leads you into a mistake.
 False friends
 Some Czech words look English but mean something else. Each one is a real Czech word, so people will understand you, just not the way you meant.

### SECTION 1   (has 5)
No "a", no "the", but always jsem
 English needs "a" and "the". Czech has no word for either, so leave them out. The verb is the opposite: it is never left out. "I am a student" is Jsem student , with jsem and no word for "a". Já student is not a sentence. To je káva means "This is coffee" or "This is a coffee": the situation does the work of "a" and "the". Gender still shows, though, through ten, ta and to: ten čaj , ta káva , to pivo .
 EX: Jsem student. | ysehm STOO-dehnt | I am a student.
CARDS NOW: Jsem student. = I am a student. [card] || To je káva. = This is coffee. [card] || ten čaj = the tea [card] || ta káva = the coffee [card] || to pivo = the beer [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 7)
"I like": the person is mi
 In English, "I" is the subject of "like". In líbí se mi and chutná mi , the thing you like is the subject, and you are mi . With mám rád , the word rád or ráda matches the person speaking, not the thing. So a woman says Mám ráda čaj , whatever the drink. Use chutná mi for food you taste and líbí se mi for a place, a thing or a person.
 EX: Líbí se mi Praha. | LEE-bee seh mih PRAH-hah | I like Prague.
 EX: Mám ráda čaj. | maahm RAAH-dah chahy | I like tea. (a woman)
CARDS NOW: mi = (to) me (unstressed) [card] || Líbí se mi. = I like it. [card] || Chutná mi. = I like it (food). [card] || Mám rád čaj. = I like tea. (a man) [card] || rád = glad [card] || Mám ráda čaj. = I like tea. (a woman) [card] || Líbí se mi Praha. = I like Prague. [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 5)
Crowns and strangers
 After five, the form is korun : pět korun , sto korun . Prices are where this shows up most. And to anyone you do not know, the verb ends in -te: jste , máte , mluvíte . The friend forms jsi , máš and mluvíš are ones you only need to recognize.
 EX: pět korun | pyeht KOH-roon | five crowns
 EX: Mluvíte česky? | MLOO-vee-teh CHEHS-kih | Do you speak Czech?
 Most common mistake: copying the English "I like". Já líbím Praha. → Líbí se mi Praha. Prague is the subject, and you are mi.
