## LESSON greetings — 5 · Greetings and thank you — deck "Greetings and courtesy"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
English speakers reach for a time of day: good morning, good evening. In Dhaka the everyday greeting works at any hour.
 Salam and the reply
 আসসালামু আলাইকুম assalamu alaikum , "peace be upon you", is the usual greeting in Bangladesh, morning or night, to anyone. The answer is ওয়ালাইকুম আসসালাম walaikum assalam , "and upon you peace".
 EX: আসসালামু আলাইকুম। | assalamu alaikum. | Hello. (literally: Peace be upon you.)
 EX: ওয়ালাইকুম আসসালাম। | walaikum assalam. | Hello. (the reply: And upon you peace.)
 Hindu speakers, and most people in West Bengal, greet with নমস্কার nômoshkar , and the reply is the same word.
 EX: নমস্কার। | nômoshkar. | Hello. (Hindu greeting)
CARDS NOW: আসসালামু আলাইকুম = peace be upon you [card] || আসসালামু আলাইকুম। = Hello. (literally: Peace be upon you.) || ওয়ালাইকুম আসসালাম। = and upon you peace [card] || নমস্কার। = Hello. (Hindu greeting)

### SECTION 1   (has 5)
How are you?
 English has one "How are you?". Bengali changes the verb for the person you are talking to. To a stranger, an older person or a shopkeeper, say আপনি apni with আছেন achhen . To a friend your own age, say তুমি tumi with আছো achho . You can drop আপনি or তুমি, because the verb ending already shows who you mean.

 EX: আপনি কেমন আছেন? | apni kæmon achhen? | How are you? (polite)

 When in doubt, use আপনি. Nobody minds being treated politely. You will also see আছো written আছ, without the last sign; it is the same word.

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
I'm fine
 English says "I am fine". Bengali says ভালো আছি bhalo achhi , literally "well am". The verb is now আছি achhi , because it matches the speaker, you, not the person who asked. আমি ami , "I", is optional.
 EX: ভালো আছি। | bhalo achhi. | I'm fine.
 EX: আমি ভালো আছি। | ami bhalo achhi. | I'm fine.
 English likes a full sentence here; Bengali is happy with two words. To return the question, simply ask it back with the verb that fits the other person.
CARDS NOW: ভালো আছি = I'm well. [card] || আমি = I [card] || ভালো আছি। = I'm fine. || আমি ভালো আছি। = I'm fine.

Thank you
 ধন্যবাদ dhonnobad means "thank you". Bengali speakers say it far less than Americans say "thanks". Keep it for a real favor or for good service, not for every small thing at the family table.
 EX: ধন্যবাদ। | dhonnobad. | Thank you.
 You will also see শুভ সকাল shubho shôkal , "good morning", and শুভ সন্ধ্যা shubho shondha , "good evening". They are formal, for cards, messages and announcements, not the everyday hello.
 Most common mistake: greeting by the time of day. Wrong: শুভ সকাল to a neighbor in the morning → right: আসসালামু আলাইকুম, at any hour.
CARDS NOW: ধন্যবাদ = thank you [card] || ধন্যবাদ। = Thank you.

## LESSON pronouns — 6 · Three words for you — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
English has one "you" for everyone. Bengali has three, and the verb changes with each.
 Three words for "you"
 আপনি apni is polite: strangers, elders, shopkeepers, anyone you do not know. Start here.
 তুমি tumi is familiar: friends, people your own age once you know them.
 তুই tui is very familiar: close friends, small children. From a stranger it can sound rude. Recognise it; you do not need to use it.
CARDS NOW: আপনি = you (polite) [card] || তুমি = you (familiar) [card] || তুই = you (very familiar) [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 4)
The verb ending follows the person
 In practice: in a shop, on the street, with a rickshaw driver or with anyone older, say আপনি. Students of the same age quickly move to তুমি. If an older person calls you তুমি, keep answering with আপনি.
 You met আছি achhi , আছো achho and আছেন achhen in greetings. Here is the whole set for "be" in "how are you" and "I'm fine".
 Person ; Bengali ; Line ; 
 I ; আমি আছি ; ami achhi ; 
 you (familiar) ; তুমি আছো ; tumi achho ; 
 you (polite) ; আপনি আছেন ; apni achhen ; 
 he, she ; সে আছে ; she achhe ; 
 he, she (polite) ; তিনি আছেন ; tini achhen ; 
 সে she means both he and she: Bengali has no he/she split. তিনি tini is the respectful he or she, for someone older or important, and it takes the same ending as আপনি.
 EX: সে ভালো আছে। | she bhalo achhe. | He is fine. She is fine.
 EX: তিনি ভালো আছেন। | tini bhalo achhen. | He is fine. She is fine. (respectful)
CARDS NOW: সে = he; she [card] || তিনি = he; she (respectful) [card] || সে ভালো আছে। = He is fine. She is fine. || তিনি ভালো আছেন। = He is fine. She is fine. (respectful)

### SECTION 2   (has 8)
More than one
 English changes "am" to "are" for "we". Bengali does not: the plural keeps the singular ending. আমরা amra "we" takes আছি, like আমি. তোমরা tomra takes আছো, আপনারা apnara takes আছেন, তারা tara "they" takes আছে.
 EX: আমরা ভালো আছি। | amra bhalo achhi. | We are fine.
 EX: আপনারা কেমন আছেন? | apnara kæmon achhen? | How are you all? (polite)
 EX: তোমরা কেমন আছো? | tomra kæmon achho? | How are you all? (to friends)
 EX: তারা ভালো আছে। | tara bhalo achhe. | They are fine.

(polite) || তোমরা কেমন আছো? = How are you all? (to friends) || তারা ভালো আছে। = They are fine.

Dropping the pronoun
 English always needs "I" or "you" in front of the verb. Bengali often does not, much as Spanish drops yo . Because the ending shows the person, the pronoun is often left out: ভালো আছি bhalo achhi is complete. With আছে, keep সে or তারা, because the ending alone does not say who.
 EX: আমি ভালো আছি। | ami bhalo achhi. | I'm fine.
 Most common mistake: one "you" for everybody. Wrong: তুমি কেমন আছো? to an older stranger → right: আপনি কেমন আছেন?
CARDS NOW: ভালো আছি = I'm well. [card] || আমি ভালো আছি। = I'm fine.

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
His or her: one word
 English picks his or her by the owner. Bengali uses তার tar for both, just as সে covers he and she. তাঁর tãr , with the nasal mark, is the respectful version that goes with তিনি. The two differ only by that mark (lesson 3), so listen for the nasal vowel. Use তাঁর for a teacher, an elder, or anyone you would call তিনি.
 EX: তার বন্ধু ভালো আছে। | tar bondhu bhalo achhe. | His friend is fine. Her friend is fine.
CARDS NOW: তার বন্ধু ভালো আছে। = His friend is fine. Her friend is fine.

### SECTION 2   (has 3)
The your matches the you
 If you say আপনি to someone, say আপনার apnar for "your". If you say তুমি, say তোমার tomar . When you ask about someone's parents, the verb is usually আছেন achhen , because parents get the respectful ending. You will also hear the plain আছে, especially for your own parents; it is not wrong, just less formal.
 EX: আপনার বাবা কেমন আছেন? | apnar baba kæmon achhen? | How is your father? (polite)
 EX: তোমার মা কেমন আছেন? | tomar ma kæmon achhen? | How is your mother? (to a friend)
 EX: আমার বাবা ভালো আছেন। | amar baba bhalo achhen. | My father is fine.
CARDS NOW: আপনার বাবা কেমন আছেন? = How is your father? (polite) || তোমার মা কেমন আছেন? = How is your mother? (to a friend) || আমার বাবা ভালো আছেন। = My father is fine.

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 0)
Nothing else changes
 English speakers sometimes expect "my" to change with the thing owned, the way it does in French or Spanish. It does not. The possessive never changes for the thing owned: আমার amar stays the same before father, mother or house, one or many. Learn one word per owner and you are done.
 Most common mistake: expecting the possessive to change with the owner's sex or with the thing owned. Wrong: hunting for a separate word for "her" → right: one তার for his and her, and one আমার before any noun: তার বন্ধু, আমার বাবা, আমার মা.

## LESSON intro — 8 · Introducing yourself — deck "About me"
### SECTION 0   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
English needs "am", "is" and "are" in every sentence about who or what someone is. Bengali leaves them out.
 No word for "am"
 English says "I am a student". Bengali says আমি ছাত্র ami chhatro , literally "I student". There is no verb at all. This is called a zero copula , and it is completely normal, not baby talk. Think of the two parts as a label and what it says: "I", "student"; "my name", "Rahim". English glues them together with am or is; Bengali just sets them side by side.
 EX: আমি ছাত্র। | ami chhatro. | I am a student.
 EX: আমি বাংলাদেশি। | ami bangladeshi. | I am Bangladeshi.
CARDS NOW: আমি ছাত্র। = I'm a student. [card] || আমি বাংলাদেশি। = I am Bangladeshi.

### SECTION 1   (has 4)
"Is" disappears too
 The same goes for he, she and things. Your name, your job, where someone is from: just put the two parts side by side.
 EX: আমার নাম রাহীম। | amar nam rahim. | My name is Rahim.
 EX: আমার বাবা ডাক্তার। | amar baba ḍaktar. | My father is a doctor.
 EX: উনি আমার শিক্ষক। | uni amar shikkhôk. | He is my teacher.
 Every new job or nationality word you learn drops straight into this frame.
CARDS NOW: আমার নাম রাহীম। = My name is Rahim. [card] || আমার বাবা ডাক্তার। = My father is a doctor. || আমার বাবা = my father [card] || উনি আমার শিক্ষক। = He is my teacher.

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
Keep the "I"
 In greetings you could drop আমি, because the ending of আছি already meant "I". A sentence with no verb has no ending to show the person, so keep the subject: আমি ছাত্র ami chhatro , not just ছাত্র. And mind the pair বাংলাদেশ bangladesh , the country, and বাংলাদেশি bangladeshi , a person from it, just like Bangladesh and Bangladeshi.
CARDS NOW: আমি বাংলাদেশি = I'm Bangladeshi. [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
So when do you say আছি?
 You learned আছি achhi in greetings. It is for how you are, as in ভালো আছি bhalo achhi , and later for where you are. For who or what you are (name, job, nationality) use no verb.
 How you are: verb ; Who you are: no verb ; 
 আমি ভালো আছি। ; আমি ছাত্র। ; 
 I'm fine. ; I'm a student. ;
CARDS NOW: ভালো আছি = I'm well. [card]

### SECTION 4   (has 4)
Asking and introducing
 To ask a name politely, say আপনার নাম কী? apnar nam ki? , literally "your name what?" (more on question words in lesson 9). To introduce someone politely, use ইনি ini , "this person", which takes respect like তিনি. For someone farther away, উনি uni .
 EX: আপনার নাম কী? | apnar nam ki? | What is your name?
 EX: ইনি আমার বাবা। | ini amar baba. | This is my father.

 Notice that "no" and "not" are different words here: না answers the question, and নই does the work of "am not".

## LESSON eta-ota — 10 · This and that — deck "This and that"
### SECTION 0   (has 5)
English points with "this" and "that". Bengali has two short words for the same job, and it adds a small ending, -টা, that English does not have at all.
 এটা and ওটা
 এটা eṭa is "this one", something near you. ওটা oṭa is "that one", over there. Remember the zero copula from the intro lesson: "This is a book" has no word for "is". You just put the two words side by side.

## LESSON ache — 12 · Having and being there — deck "Home and having"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)

## LESSON time — 20 · The clock and the day — deck "Time and routine"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
English says "It's three o'clock", with "it's" in front. Bengali says the number with the classifier -টা and then the verb বাজে ( baje ), which literally means "strikes": "three strikes".
 Asking and telling the time
 To ask, use কয়টা ( kôyṭa ), "how many", with বাজে. To answer, put the hour with -টা in the same slot.
 EX: কয়টা বাজে? | kôyṭa baje? | What time is it?
 EX: এখন তিনটা বাজে। | ækhon tinṭa baje. | It's three o'clock now.
 The hour always carries -টা: তিনটা, চারটা, দশটা. A bare number is not a time. You learned in the numbers lesson that counted things take -টা, and the clock is counted the same way. The question and the answer have the same shape, so once you can ask, you can answer.

## LESSON postpositions — 23 · Endings and postpositions — deck "Getting around"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
English puts small words like with , for and from in front of the noun: with my mother . Bengali puts them after it: "my mother's with". These words that follow the noun are postpositions. Endings stuck onto the noun itself, like -কে, are case endings.
 -কে: the person you do it to
 When you see, call or give to a person, that person takes -কে. You already know আমাকে, তোমাকে and আপনাকে: they are আমি, তুমি and আপনি with this ending.
 EX: আমি গতকাল ভাইকে দেখলাম। | ami gôtokal bhaike dekhlam. | I saw my brother yesterday.
 EX: আমাকে একটা বই দিন। | amake ækṭa boi din. | Give me a book, please.
 A thing takes no ending. English speakers who have just learned -কে often put it on everything. Keep it for people. The sentence order does not change: the person with -কে sits before the verb, like any object.
 EX: আমি একটা বই চাই। | ami ækṭa boi chai. | I want a book.

## LESSON compare — 25 · Describing and comparing — deck "Compliments"
### SECTION 0   (has 9)
English changes the adjective to compare: big, bigger, biggest , or adds more . Bengali never changes the adjective. বড় ( bôṛo ) is "big" and also "bigger". What changes is the thing you compare with.
 Praising
 To praise, use ভালো ( bhalo ) (good) or সুন্দর ( shundôr ) (beautiful), with খুব ( khub ) for "very". There is no verb for "is".

## LESSON shopping — 27 · Shopping — deck "Shopping"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
At a Dhaka market you count some things and weigh others. English says "four mangoes" and "two kilos of rice". Bengali counts with the classifier -টা on the number, and a measure word like কেজি takes the classifier's place, with no word for "of".
 Asking the price
 দাম ( dam ) is "price" and কত ( kôto ) is "how much". There is no verb for "is". The thing you ask about takes the -র form.
 EX: দাম কত? | dam kôto? | What's the price?
 EX: এটার দাম কত? | eṭar dam kôto? | How much is this?
 EX: এক কেজি আলুর দাম কত? | æk keji alur dam kôto? | How much is a kilo of potatoes?
 To ask about one item, point and use এটার, "this one's". For food sold by weight, the price is per kilo, so ask about এক কেজি.
 The answer is a number and টাকা, the Bangladeshi taka. Prices on signs may be in Bengali digits or in Western digits.
 EX: পঞ্চাশ টাকা। | pônchash ṭaka. | Fifty taka.
