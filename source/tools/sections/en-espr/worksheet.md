## LESSON sounds — 1 · Sounds, spelling and stress — deck "Sounds and spelling"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
Spanish spelling is regular, so a few rules let you read almost anything. Every Spanish phrase here has a pronunciation line in English letters; CAPITALS mark the stressed syllable.
 Five short, pure vowels
 English vowels slide: say no slowly and your lips end on a w . Spanish vowels stay put. Each is short and keeps one sound. The line spells them ah, eh, ee, oh, oo .
 Letter ; Sounds like ; Example ;

 o ; go , with no w at the end ; no ;

 EX: la casa | lah KAH-sah | the house
 EX: No. | noh | No.
CARDS NOW: no = no; not [card] || sí = yes [card] || uno = one (the number) [card] || la casa = house; home [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Where the stress goes
 Spanish stress follows one rule. A word ending in a vowel, n or s is stressed on the next-to-last syllable. Any other ending: the last syllable. A written accent mark ( á é í ó ú ) overrides both. Watch words that look English: hospital and animal are stressed at the end.
 EX: el hospital | ehl ohs-pee-TAHL | the hospital
 EX: Adiós. | ah-DYOHS | Goodbye.
CARDS NOW: el hospital = the hospital [card] || Adiós. = Goodbye. [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Tap and trill
 A single r between vowels is a tap: one quick flick of the tongue, like the tt in American butter . Double rr , and r at the start of a word, is a trill (a rolled r). It changes the word: pero means "but", perro means "dog". The line writes the tap r and the trill rr.
 EX: pero | PEH-roh | but
 EX: el perro | ehl PEH-rroh | the dog
CARDS NOW: pero = but [card] || el perro = the dog [card]

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
el and la
 Nouns here come with a small word in front: el perro , la llave . Both mean "the". Every noun is masculine or feminine, so learn each one with its el or la . For more than one: los perros , las llaves .
CARDS NOW: el = the (masculine) [card] || la = the (feminine) [card]

### SECTION 5   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Use the crutch, then drop it
 The line cannot show everything: d between vowels is soft, close to the th in this , and p , t , k have no puff of air. Listen to the audio first, and hide the line once the spelling feels natural.
 EX: nada | NAH-dah | nothing
 Puerto Ricans soften some of these sounds, such as a final s . The next lesson trains your ear; the spelling stays the same.
 Most common mistake: saying the h the English way. hola with a breathy h → hola starting straight on the vowel. The breathy sound belongs to j , never to h .
CARDS NOW: nada = nothing [card] || Hola. = Hi. / Hello. [card]

## LESSON pr-sounds — 2 · Hearing Puerto Rico: s, r and -ao — deck "Puerto Rican sounds"
### SECTION 0   (has 3)
Puerto Ricans write the same Spanish you are learning, but relaxed island speech changes a few sounds. These are normal features of the island's Spanish, not mistakes. This lesson trains your ears: you never have to copy these sounds, and the pronunciation line in this course stays the careful one.
 The s that turns into a breath
 English always says its s. In Puerto Rico, an s before a consonant or at the end of a word often turns into a breath, like a soft English h, or drops completely. You'll hear ¿Cómo estás? as "KOH-moh ehh-TAH".
 EX: ¿Cómo estás? | KOH-moh ehs-TAHS | How are you?
 EX: ¿Cómo están ustedes? | KOH-moh ehs-TAHN oos-TEH-dehs | How are you all?
 Write it the standard way, with every s .
CARDS NOW: Puerto Rico = Puerto Rico [card] || ¿Cómo estás? = How are you? [card] || ¿Cómo están ustedes? = How are you all? [card]

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Listen to the little words
 When the s drops, how do you know there is more than one? The article and the verb ending still tell you. You'll hear "loh ah-MEE-goh": loh can only be los , so it is los amigos , the friends. In "ehh-TAH-moh", the -moh is the -mos of "we".
 EX: los amigos | lohs ah-MEE-gohs | the friends
 Write the plural in full: los amigos .
CARDS NOW: los = the (masculine plural) [card] || los amigos = the friends [card]

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
r that sounds like l
 An r before a consonant or at the end of a word can sound like an English l. You'll hear "PWEHL-toh RREE-koh" for Puerto Rico and "pehl-DOHN" for perdón . Not everyone does it, so understand it, but don't copy it.
 EX: Puerto Rico | PWEHR-toh RREE-koh | Puerto Rico
 EX: Perdón. | pehr-DOHN | Sorry.
 Write the r : Puerto , perdón .
CARDS NOW: Perdón. = Sorry. [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)
The back rr
 Some speakers, more often in the countryside, say rr far back in the mouth, close to a Spanish j . El carro (the car) can sound close to "ehl KAH-hoh".
 EX: el carro | ehl KAH-rroh | the car
 It is still rr in writing.
CARDS NOW: el carro = the car [card]

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
cansao for cansado
 A d between vowels in -ado and -ido often disappears. Cansado (tired) sounds like "kahn-SAH-oh", and vendido (sold) like "behn-DEE-oh".
 EX: Estamos cansados. | ehs-TAH-mohs kahn-SAH-dohs | We're tired.
 Write the d : cansado , vendido .
CARDS NOW: cansado = tired [card] || Estamos cansados. = We're tired. [card]

### SECTION 5   (has 4)
Short words: pa' and mai
 Para (for) is often just "pah": para la casa sounds like "pah lah KAH-sah". With family you'll also hear mai and pai for madre and padre , mom and dad.
 EX: para la casa | PAH-rah lah KAH-sah | for the house
 In writing, always para . Whatever you hear, write it the standard way: the app accepts only standard spelling.
 Most common mistake: writing only what you hear. los amigo → los amigos . The article and the noun both keep their s on the page.
CARDS NOW: mai = mom (informal) [card] || para = for; in order to [card] || para la casa = for the house; home (heading home) [card] || pai = dad (informal) [card]

## LESSON greetings — 3 · Greetings: tú and usted — deck "Greetings and courtesy"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
Hola works with anyone, at any hour. To sound warmer or more polite, add the greeting for the time of day.
 Morning, afternoon, evening
 English says "good morning". Spanish says "good days": buenos días . After about noon it is buenas tardes , and after dark buenas noches , which means both "good evening" and "good night".
 EX: Buenos días. | BWEH-nohs DEE-ahs | Good morning.
 EX: Buenas noches. | BWEH-nahs NOH-chehs | Good evening. Good night.
 Why buenos in one and buenas in the others? Días is a masculine word and tardes and noches are feminine, and the word for "good" changes to match. This is your first taste of agreement, which runs through all of Spanish.
CARDS NOW: Hola. = Hi. / Hello. [card] || Buenos días. = Good morning. [card] || Buenas tardes. = Good afternoon. [card] || Buenas noches. = Good evening. / Good night. [card]

### SECTION 1   (has 10)
Two ways to say "you"
 English has one "you". Spanish has two for one person. Use tú with friends, family, children and people your age in relaxed settings. Use usted with strangers, older people, customers and staff. The verb changes too: estás goes with tú , está with usted .
 ; To a friend (tú) ; To a stranger (usted) ; 
 How are you? ; ¿Cómo estás? ; ¿Cómo está usted? ; 
 And you? ; ¿Y tú? ; ¿Y usted? ; 
 What's your name? ; ¿Cómo te llamas? ; ¿Cómo se llama usted? ; 
 Excuse me. ; Disculpa. ; Disculpe. ; 
 EX: ¡Hola! ¿Cómo estás? | OH-lah KOH-moh ehs-TAHS | Hi! How are you?
 EX: Buenas tardes. ¿Cómo está usted? | BWEH-nahs TAHR-dehs KOH-moh ehs-TAH oos-TEHD | Good afternoon. How are you?
 EX: Bien, gracias. ¿Y tú? | byehn GRAH-syahs ee too | Fine, thanks. And you?
 When in doubt, start with usted . If the other person wants tú , they will switch, and you can follow.
 In Puerto Rico tú is used widely, even with shop and restaurant staff about your age. Keep usted for elders and for strangers much older than you.

(formal) [card] || ¿Cómo te llamas? = What's your name? [card] || ¿Cómo se llama usted? = What's your name? (formal) [card] || Disculpe. = Excuse me. [card] || ¡Hola! ¿Cómo estás? = Hi! ### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Names
 To give your name, say me llamo , literally "I call myself". It already contains "my name is", so nothing else goes in between. When you meet someone, say mucho gusto , "nice to meet you".
 EX: Me llamo Ana. | meh YAH-moh AH-nah | My name is Ana.
 EX: Mucho gusto. | MOO-choh GOOS-toh | Nice to meet you.
CARDS NOW: Mucho gusto. = Nice to meet you. [card] || Me llamo Ana. = My name is Ana.

### SECTION 3   (has 6)
Small polite words
 Por favor is "please", gracias is "thank you", and the answer is de nada , "you're welcome". To leave, say adiós or hasta luego , "see you later".
 EX: Gracias. De nada. | GRAH-syahs deh NAH-dah | Thank you. You're welcome.
 In Spain you will also hear vosotros for "you all". Latin America uses ustedes for any group, and so does this course.
 Most common mistake: one form of "good" for every greeting. Buenos tardes → buenas tardes . Only días takes buenos .

## LESSON pr-greetings — 4 · Talking like a boricua: pana, nena, wepa — deck "Boricua greetings and slang"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
The greetings you already know work everywhere in Puerto Rico. With friends, people also use island words and slang. Learn to understand them first, then use them with people your age in relaxed moments.
 What's up?
 English friends say "What's up?". Puerto Rican friends say ¿Qué es la que hay? . The short ¿Qué hay? , heard all over the Spanish-speaking world, works too. Saludos , literally "greetings", is a friendly hello.
 EX: ¿Qué es la que hay? | keh ehs lah keh ai | What's up?
 EX: Saludos. ¿Cómo estás? | sah-LOO-dohs KOH-moh ehs-TAHS | Hi there. How are you?
CARDS NOW: ¿Qué es la que hay? = What's up? [card] || ¿Qué hay? = What's new? [card] || Saludos. = Hi there; greetings. [card] || Saludos. ¿Cómo estás? = Hi there. How are you?

### SECTION 1   (has 6)
Friends and family
 Mi pana is "my buddy", from English "partner". It is the same for a man or a woman. Your group of friends is el corillo , the crew. Nene and nena mean boy and girl, and they are also an affectionate word for friends and family. At home you'll hear mai and pai for mom and dad.
 EX: Él es mi pana. | ehl ehs mee PAH-nah | He's my buddy.
 EX: ¿Qué hay, nena? | keh ai NEH-nah | What's up, girl?
CARDS NOW: mi pana = my buddy [card] || el corillo = the crew; my group of friends [card] || mai = mom (informal) [card] || pai = dad (informal) [card] || Él es mi pana. = He's my buddy. || ¿Qué hay, nena? = What's up, girl?

### SECTION 2   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Boricua
 Boricua means Puerto Rican. It comes from Borikén , the Taíno name of the island, and people use it with pride. It has one form for a man or a woman. For more than one, add -s : boricuas .
 EX: Ella es boricua. | EH-yah ehs boh-REE-kwah | She's Puerto Rican.
CARDS NOW: boricua = Puerto Rican [card] || Ella es boricua. = She's Puerto Rican.

### SECTION 3   (has 4)
Wepa, bendito, acho
 ¡Wepa! is pure joy: a goal, good news, a great song. ¡Ay bendito! is sympathy, like "aww, poor thing", and sometimes frustration. ¡Acho! is like "man!" or "wow!".
 EX: ¡Wepa! ¡Muchas gracias! | WEH-pah MOO-chahs GRAH-syahs | Yay! Thank you so much!
 EX: ¡Ay bendito! | ai behn-DEE-toh | Aww, poor thing!
CARDS NOW: ¡Wepa! = Woo-hoo! / Yay! [card] || ¡Acho! = Man! / Dude! [card] || ¡Ay bendito! = Aw, poor thing! [card] || ¡Wepa! ¡Muchas gracias! = Yay! Thank you so much!

### SECTION 4   (has 5)
Cool, great, awesome
 Chévere (cool), nítido (great) and brutal (awesome) describe things, so they agree like any describing word. Chévere and brutal have one form for both genders and add -s or -es for more than one. Nítido changes its ending like any -o word: nítida , nítidos , nítidas .
 EX: Está chévere. | ehs-TAH CHEH-beh-reh | It's cool.
 EX: Las fiestas están chéveres. | lahs FYEHS-tahs ehs-TAHN CHEH-beh-rehs | The parties are cool.
 EX: La playa está nítida. | lah PLAH-yah ehs-TAH NEE-tee-dah | The beach is great.
CARDS NOW: Está nítido. = It's great. [card] || ¡Brutal! = Awesome! [card] || Está chévere. = It's cool. [card] || Las fiestas están chéveres. = The parties are cool. [card] || La playa está nítida. = The beach is great.

### SECTION 5   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
When not to use them
 Slang belongs with friends. With your partner's grandmother, a boss or a stranger, use the greetings from the greetings lesson: Buenos días , ¿Cómo está usted? .
 Most common mistake: slang with an elder. ¿Qué es la que hay? to your partner's grandmother → Buenos días, ¿cómo está usted?
CARDS NOW: Buenos días. = Good morning. [card] || ¿Cómo está usted? = How are you? (formal) [card]

## LESSON gender — 5 · Gender and number of nouns — deck "People and family"
### SECTION 0   (has 9)
English has one word for "the" and treats every noun the same. In Spanish every noun has a gender: it is either masculine or feminine. That goes for things as well as people. A house is feminine, la casa ; a dog is masculine, el perro . The gender is part of the word, so always learn a noun with its el or la .
 -o and -a: the usual pattern
 Most nouns ending in -o are masculine and take el . Most nouns ending in -a are feminine and take la . For many people words, you just swap the ending.
 Masculine ; Feminine ; English ; 
 el hermano ; la hermana ; brother, sister ; 
 el hijo ; la hija ; son, daughter ; 
 el abuelo ; la abuela ; grandfather, grandmother ; 
 el amigo ; la amiga ; friend ; 
 EX: el hermano y la hermana | ehl ehr-MAH-noh ee lah ehr-MAH-nah | the brother and the sister
CARDS NOW: la casa = house; home [card] || la = the (feminine) [card] || el perro = the dog [card] || el = the (masculine) [card] || el hijo = the son [card] || la hija = daughter [card] || el amigo = friend (male, or a mixed group) [card] || la amiga = friend (female) [card] || el hermano y la hermana = the brother and the sister

### SECTION 1   (has 3)
Exceptions worth memorizing
 A few very common nouns break the pattern. Día , mapa and problema end in -a but are masculine. Mano (hand) ends in -o but is feminine.
 EX: el día | ehl DEE-ah | the day
 EX: el problema | ehl proh-BLEH-mah | the problem
 EX: la mano | lah MAH-noh | the hand
CARDS NOW: el día = the day [card] || el problema = the problem [card] || la mano = hand [card]

### SECTION 2   (has 4)
Other endings
 Nouns ending in -e or a consonant can go either way: el padre , la madre , el hotel , la mujer . One helpful clue: nouns ending in -dad or -ción are feminine, like la ciudad (the city).
CARDS NOW: el padre = father; priest [card] || la madre = mother [card] || la mujer = woman; wife [card] || la ciudad = city [card]

### SECTION 3   (has 4)
More than one
 To make a noun plural, add -s after a vowel and -es after a consonant. The article changes too: el becomes los , and la becomes las .

 la mano ; las manos ;

 EX: las ciudades | lahs syoo-DAH-dehs | the cities
CARDS NOW: los = the (masculine plural) [card] || las = the (feminine plural) [card] || los amigos = the friends [card] || las ciudades = the cities

### SECTION 4   (has 3)
Mixed groups
 For a group of men and women together, Spanish uses the masculine plural. So los hermanos can mean "the brothers" or "the brothers and sisters", and los padres means "the parents". Careful: los parientes means "the relatives", not "the parents".
 EX: los hermanos | lohs ehr-MAH-nohs | the brothers; the siblings
 EX: los padres | lohs PAH-drehs | the parents
 Most common mistake: trusting the ending every time. la problema → el problema , and el mano → la mano .
CARDS NOW: los hermanos = the brothers; the siblings || los padres = parents [card] || los parientes = relatives [card]

## LESSON articles — 6 · el / la, un / una — deck "Food and drink"
### SECTION 0   (has 4)
English has three small words for this job: the , a and some . Spanish has the same three, but each one has four forms, because it agrees with the noun in gender and number.
 the: el, la, los, las
 These are the definite articles, and you already know them from the last lesson: el and los for masculine nouns, la and las for feminine ones.
CARDS NOW: el = the (masculine) [card] || la = the (feminine) [card] || los = the (masculine plural) [card] || las = the (feminine plural) [card]

a and some: un, una, unos, unas
 These are the indefinite articles. English a never changes. Spanish un goes with masculine nouns and una with feminine ones. In the plural, unos and unas mean "some" or "a few".

el agua: a feminine word with el
 Agua (water) is feminine, but it starts with a stressed a sound, and la agua would run two a sounds together. So in the singular it takes el : el agua . It is still feminine, as you will see when adjectives come along.
 EX: un vaso de agua | oon BAH-soh deh AH-gwah | a glass of water
CARDS NOW: el agua = water [card] || un vaso de agua = a glass of water [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Things in general keep the article
 English drops the article when it talks about something in general: "I like coffee", "Tostones are great". Spanish keeps it. To say you like something, use the set phrase me gusta plus the noun with its article. A later lesson explains how gustar works.
 EX: Me gusta el café. | meh GOOS-tah ehl kah-FEH | I like coffee.
CARDS NOW: gustar = to be pleasing (how "like" works) [card] || Me gusta el café. = I like coffee.

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Ordering
 In a café or a restaurant, a noun with its article plus por favor already makes a polite order. Ask for the check the same way.
 EX: La cuenta, por favor. | lah KWEHN-tah pohr fah-BOHR | The check, please.

## LESSON connectors — 22 · y, o, pero, porque — deck "Questions and connectors"
### SECTION 0   (has 9)
A few small words turn short sentences into real conversation. Four of them do most of the work: y (and), o (or), pero (but) and porque (because).
 y and o, and how they change
 y means "and" and o means "or", just as in English. When y joins two people, the verb takes the ellos form, because together they are "they". In a list, y goes only before the last item, as in English: arroz, habichuelas y tostones .
 EX: Ana y Carlos son de Ponce. | AH-nah ee KAHR-lohs sohn deh POHN-seh | Ana and Carlos are from Ponce.
 EX: ¿Quieres café o té? | KYEH-rehs kah-FEH oh teh | Do you want coffee or tea?
 One twist English doesn't have: before a word that starts with an "ee" sound (spelled i- or hi- ), y becomes e . Before a word that starts with an "oh" sound ( o- or ho- ), o becomes u . This keeps the two sounds from running together.
 EX: Sofía e Isabel trabajan aquí. | soh-FEE-ah eh ee-sah-BEHL trah-BAH-hahn ah-KEE | Sofía and Isabel work here.
 EX: Hay siete u ocho personas. | ai SYEH-teh oo OH-choh pehr-SOH-nahs | There are seven or eight people.

## LESSON preterite — 27 · The preterite: what you did — deck "Likes and free time"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
English has one simple past: "I worked, I ate, I went." Spanish has more than one past, and the preterite is the one for finished actions: what you did yesterday, last night or last weekend.
 Regular endings
 Take off -ar , -er or -ir and add these endings. The verb ending matches the subject, as in the present. -er and -ir verbs share one set.
 Who ; hablar ; comer ; vivir ; 
 yo ; hablé ; comí ; viví ; 
 tú ; hablaste ; comiste ; viviste ; 
 él, ella, usted ; habló ; comió ; vivió ; 
 nosotros ; hablamos ; comimos ; vivimos ; 
 ellos, ellas, ustedes ; hablaron ; comieron ; vivieron ; 
 The nosotros form of -ar and -ir verbs looks just like the present. A time word such as ayer shows you it's the past.
 EX: Anoche comí mofongo en un restaurante. | ah-NOH-cheh koh-MEE moh-FOHN-goh ehn oon rrehs-tow-RAHN-teh | Last night I ate mofongo at a restaurant.
 EX: ¿Trabajaste el sábado? | trah-bah-HAHS-teh ehl SAH-bah-doh | Did you work on Saturday?
 EX: Mis amigos vivieron en Orlando. | mees ah-MEE-gohs bee-BYEH-rohn ehn ohr-LAHN-doh | My friends lived in Orlando.
CARDS NOW: ir = to go [card] || yo = I [card] || tú = you (one person, friendly) [card] || nosotros = we (men or a mixed group) [card] || ayer = yesterday [card] || Anoche comí mofongo en un restaurante. = Last night I ate mofongo at a restaurant. || ¿Trabajaste el sábado? = Did you work on Saturday? || Mis amigos vivieron en Orlando. = My friends lived in Orlando.

### SECTION 1   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
The accent mark changes the meaning
 In hablo the stress is on HA , and it means "I speak". In habló the stress moves to the end, and it means "he or she spoke". The written accent is the only difference on paper, so always write it, and say it with the stress at the end.
 EX: Hablo con mi jefa todos los días. | AH-bloh kohn mee HEH-fah TOH-dohs lohs DEE-ahs | I talk to my boss every day.
 EX: Ayer mi jefa habló conmigo. | ah-YEHR mee HEH-fah ah-BLOH kohn-MEE-goh | Yesterday my boss talked to me.
CARDS NOW: Hablo con mi jefa todos los días. = I talk to my boss every day. || Ayer mi jefa habló conmigo. = Yesterday my boss talked to me.

### SECTION 2   (has 7)
Three irregular verbs: fui, hice, tuve
 Three very common verbs have their own forms, with no accent marks: ir (yo fui , tú fuiste , él fue ), hacer ( hice , hiciste , hizo ) and tener ( tuve , tuviste , tuvo ).
 EX: El fin de semana pasado fui a la playa. | ehl feen deh seh-MAH-nah pah-SAH-doh fwee ah lah PLAH-yah | Last weekend I went to the beach.
 EX: ¿Qué hiciste ayer? | keh ee-SEES-teh ah-YEHR | What did you do yesterday?
 Useful time words: ayer (yesterday), anoche (last night), el fin de semana pasado and la semana pasada , where pasado agrees with its noun.
CARDS NOW: hacer = to do; to make [card] || ¿Qué hiciste el fin de semana? = What did you do on the weekend? [card] || tener = to have [card] || El fin de semana pasado fui a la playa. = Last weekend I went to the beach. || el fin de semana = the weekend [card] || ¿Qué hiciste ayer? = What did you do yesterday? || anoche = last night [card]

### SECTION 3   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 2)
Ya: "have you…?" in Latin America
 Where English says "Have you eaten yet?" or "I've been to El Yunque", Latin American Spanish usually uses the preterite, often with ya (already, yet).
 EX: ¿Ya comiste? | yah koh-MEES-teh | Have you eaten yet?
CARDS NOW: ya = already; now (right away) [card] || ¿Ya comiste? = Have you eaten yet?

### SECTION 4   <-- NEEDS CARDS (has 1)

## LESSON se — 28 · Impersonal se: se habla español — deck "Work and school"
### SECTION 0   (has 8)
English often uses "you", "they" or "people" for no one in particular: "How do you say this?", "They sell coffee here", "You can't come in." Spanish usually says this with se and the él form of the verb, and no person is named. This is the impersonal se. It feels strange at first, but the idea is simple: something is done, and who does it doesn't matter.
 se + verb: no one in particular
 Put se right before the verb. With no , the order is no se + verb.
 EX: Aquí se habla español. | ah-KEE seh AH-blah ehs-pah-NYOHL | Spanish is spoken here.

[card] || ¿Cómo se dice esto en español? = How do you say this in Spanish? || ¿Cómo se dice esto? = How do you say this? ### SECTION 2   (has 4)
Don't translate "you" and "they" word for word

## LESSON pr-culture — 29 · Food, music and fiestas — deck "Food, music and places"
### SECTION 0   (has 9)
Food, music and holidays come up at every Puerto Rican family gathering. This lesson gives you the names, with a quick review of gustar and agreement.
 At the table
 El mofongo is mashed fried green plantain with garlic. Los tostones are fried green plantain slices. El arroz con gandules is rice with pigeon peas, a favorite at holiday meals. Las alcapurrias are fried fritters with a filling, and los pasteles are not cakes: they are a savory Christmas dish of green banana dough with a meat filling, wrapped in plantain leaf. For dessert, el tembleque , a coconut pudding; on a hot day, una piragua , shaved ice with syrup.
