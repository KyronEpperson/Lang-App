English counts most nouns directly: three books, this ticket. It needs an extra word only for some things: three cups of tea, a pair of shoes. Chinese needs that extra word every time a noun is counted or pointed at. It is called a measure word (or "classifier").

Number + measure word + noun

The measure word sits between the number and the noun. Leave it out and the phrase is simply wrong: 三书 is not Chinese.

{{ex:zh.s0118}}{{ex:zh.s0119}}

Remember: two before a measure word is 两, and 一 changes its tone to match the next syllable: 一本 yì běn, 一个 yí ge.

这 and 那 need one too

"This" is 这 zhè and "that" is 那 nà. Before a noun, they also take a measure word. So do 哪 nǎ (which) and 几 (how many).

{{ex:zh.s0120}}{{ex:zh.s0121}}

Which measure word?

Each noun has its own, often linked to its shape. Learn each noun together with its measure word, the way the cards show it.

Measure wordUsed forExample
个people and many everyday things一个人
本books, notebooks三本书
杯a cup or glass of a drink一杯咖啡
张flat things: tickets, paper, tables一张票
件tops, coats, most clothes一件衣服
只most animals一只猫
双pairs一双鞋
瓶 / 碗a bottle / a bowl of something一瓶水，一碗面条
{{ex:zh.s0122}}{{ex:zh.s0123}}

个 is the most common measure word, and it is right for people and many objects. But it is not a safe choice for everything: books, cups of tea and tickets sound wrong with it.

这个 and 那个 on their own

In a shop you can point and say 这个 zhège (this one) or 那个 nàge (that one), with no noun at all.

{{ex:zh.s0124}}
Most common mistake: leaving out the measure word, as English does. 这书 → 这本书; 三书 → 三本书.
