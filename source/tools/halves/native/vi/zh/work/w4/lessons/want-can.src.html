English has "want", "would like", "can", "know how to" and "may". Chinese covers them with five short words that sit right before the verb. Like every Chinese verb, they never change their form.

想 and 要: would like, want

想 xiǎng + verb is "would like to" or "feel like": soft and common. 要 yào is firmer: "want", and with a verb also "need to" or "am going to". With a thing and no verb, use 要: 我要一杯咖啡.

会: know how to

For a skill you learned, such as a language, swimming or driving, use 会 huì.

{{ex:zh.s0166}}{{ex:zh.s0167}}

能 and 可以: able to, may

能 néng is about being able right now: time, health, the situation. 可以 kěyǐ asks for or gives permission, the way English uses "may" or "can I…?".

{{ex:zh.s0168}}{{ex:zh.s0169}}

Saying no

Put 不 in front: 不想 (don't want to), 不会 (can't, never learned), 不能 (can't, not able or not allowed). Remember that 不 becomes bú before a fourth tone: bú huì, bú yào.

Asking and answering

Add 吗 to ask, as with any yes/no question. To answer, repeat the modal verb on its own: 会 or 不会, 可以 or 不可以. There is no separate word for yes or no.

{{ex:zh.s0170}}

Where it sits

Time still comes first. Then the modal verb, then 在 + place, then the action: 我今天想在家吃饭.

{{ex:zh.s0171}}

A polite note: 我要… is a normal way to order food, but between people a softer 我想… often sounds friendlier.

Most common mistake: using 能 for a skill you learned. 我能说中文 is heard, but for a language you learned Chinese speakers normally say → 我会说中文. 能 is about being able right now.
