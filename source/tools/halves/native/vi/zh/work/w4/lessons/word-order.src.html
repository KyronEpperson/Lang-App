English builds a sentence like a train: who, the verb, what, and then the details trail behind: "I'm eating at home tomorrow." Chinese does the opposite. The details come first, and the action waits until the end. Once you know the line-up, you can build long sentences without guessing.

The line-up

WhoWhenWhereVerb + what
我明天在家吃饭
我们今天下午在图书馆学习
{{ex:zh.s0157}}{{ex:zh.s0158}}

You can leave out the when or the where, but the slots never swap: when comes before where, and both come before the verb.

Time can also go first

A time word may move to the very front, before who. 明天我去北京 Míngtiān wǒ qù Běijīng means the same as 我明天去北京; putting the time first just sets the scene. What a time word can never do is go to the end, after the verb, the way it does in English.

{{ex:zh.s0159}}{{ex:zh.s0147}}

How you do it: also before the verb

"By subway" is a detail too, so it comes before the main verb. 坐 zuò means to sit, and also to ride: 坐地铁去上班 is literally "sit subway go to work". You'll see more of this pattern in the lesson on two verbs in a row.

{{ex:zh.s0160}}

Big before small

Chinese moves from the big picture down to the detail. A time goes day, then part of the day, then the hour. A date goes month, then day. A place goes country, then city.

{{ex:zh.s0161}}

A few verbs break the rule

住 zhù (to live) is the one you have already met: with it, 在 + place comes after the verb. Say 我住在北京, and notice the big-before-small order of the place.

{{ex:zh.s0162}}
Most common mistake: leaving the time or place at the end, as in English. 我去北京明天 → 我明天去北京. 我吃饭在家 → 我在家吃饭.
