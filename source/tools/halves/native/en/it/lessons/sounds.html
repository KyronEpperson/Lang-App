Italian spelling is regular: once you know a handful of rules, you can read almost any word. Under each Italian phrase in this course there is a pronunciation line in English letters. The syllable in CAPITALS is the stressed one.

The alphabet has 21 letters; j, k, w, x, y turn up mainly in words and names from other languages. These letters sound much as in English: c and g as in cat and go, until an e or i follows, and q always comes with u, as in quick. The rest of this lesson is about the letters and groups that work differently, starting with the vowels. Tap a letter to hear it.

{{tiles:it.tl001}}

Five short, pure vowels

English vowels slide: say no slowly and your lips end on a w. Italian vowels stay put. The pronunciation line writes them ah, eh, ee, oh, oo, one sound each, with no glide.

{{ex:it.s0001}}{{ex:it.s0002}}

When an unstressed i or u comes right before another vowel, it usually glides into it as a y or a w, in the same syllable: la chiave is lah KYAH-veh, il cuore is eel KWOH-reh. A stressed i or u keeps its own syllable, as in mio, MEE-oh. After c, g or sc the i is often not said at all, as the next sections show. Tap a vowel to hear it.

{{tiles:it.tl002}}

‹c› and ‹g›: soft or hard

Before e or i, c sounds like the ch in church and g like the j in jam. Before a, o, u they are hard, as in cat and go. To keep them hard before e or i, Italian adds an h: ch is always a k sound, gh always a hard g. In cia, cio, gia, gio the i is not said; it only softens the letter before it.

{{tiles:it.tl004}}

Double consonants

A double letter is held a little longer, and it changes the word. La palla is a ball; la pala is a shovel. The pronunciation line writes the letter on both sides of the hyphen.

{{ex:it.s0007}}{{ex:it.s0008}}

You hear it clearly with r: one r is a quick tap of the tongue, rr a longer roll. Tap to hear the difference.

{{tiles:it.tl005}}

Stress

Most Italian words are stressed on the next-to-last syllable. A written accent appears only on a stressed final vowel, as in città. Many other words put the stress earlier, with nothing in the spelling to warn you, so learn the stress with the word. Words that look English often surprise: telefono is stressed on its second syllable.

{{ex:it.s0009}}{{ex:it.s0010}}

The accent marks are à, è, é, ì, ò, ù. In a longer word, a mark shows that the final vowel is stressed. è is a slightly more open eh than é (il caffè, but perché); the pronunciation line writes both as eh. On short words a mark often tells two words apart: è means is, e means and; tè is tea, te is you. Tap a mark to hear it.

{{tiles:it.tl006}}

Read whole words

Now put it together. Read a word in two steps. First give each letter or group its Italian sound: every vowel short and pure, c and g soft only before e or i, double letters held. Then place the stress: on the final vowel if it has a mark, otherwise usually on the next-to-last syllable. So il prosciutto is eel proh-SHOOT-toh, and il caffè is eel kahf-FEH. Many words, like il medico, put the stress earlier, and the card shows it. Each card's note names the pieces. Say the word aloud before you flip it.

{{ex:it.s0011}}

‹il› and ‹la›, and their plurals

Nouns here come with a little word for "the". Every noun is masculine or feminine: masculine nouns take il, feminine nouns take la. For more than one, il becomes i and la becomes le, and the noun changes its last letter: -o becomes -i, -a becomes -e, -e becomes -i. So it's le notti and le chiavi. Some words take other forms: gli gnocchi, gli spaghetti. Lesson 4 explains them all.

Finally, p, t, k have no puff of air. Listen first; the pronunciation line is a crutch to drop later.

Most common mistake: reading sch the English way. bruschetta with an English sh sound → bruschetta with a hard sk sound, as the line shows.
