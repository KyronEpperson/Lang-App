English puts a plain verb after "can", "must" and "want to": "I can come", "I have to go". Italian works the same way. The modal verbs potere (can), dovere (have to, must) and volere (want) are followed by an infinitive, with no word for "to" in between.

All three are irregular, so learn the forms. Only the modal changes for the subject; the infinitive after it never does. To say you can't, don't have to or don't want to, put non in front of the modal: Non posso, Non devo, Non voglio. A question is the same sentence with a rising voice.

potere: can, may

Use it for permission and for what is possible. With a stranger, use the Lei form può. Può aiutarmi? is a set phrase worth learning whole: the mi on the end means "me".

dovere: have to

Dovere covers both "must" and "have to". It is the verb you need at the pharmacy or the doctor's, for what you are told to do.

{{ex:it.s0117}}{{ex:it.s0118}}

volere and vorrei

Voglio means "I want". It is fine for plans and wishes, but said to a waiter, a pharmacist or a stranger it sounds demanding. To ask for something, use vorrei, "I would like". It works with a noun or with an infinitive.

{{ex:it.s0119}}{{ex:it.s0120}}

can: potere or sapere?

English "can" covers two ideas. If it is a skill you have learned, such as swimming or playing an instrument, Italian uses so (I know how), from sapere, which gets its own lesson later. Posso is for permission or what is possible right now.

{{ex:it.s0121}}{{ex:it.s0122}}
Most common mistake: using voglio to order or ask for help. Voglio un caffè → Vorrei un caffè.
