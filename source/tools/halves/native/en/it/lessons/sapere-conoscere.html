English has one verb, "know". Italian splits it in two verbs: sapere and conoscere. The choice depends on what comes right after it in the sentence.

A quick test: if English could say "know that", "know where" or "know how to", use sapere. If it could say "be familiar with" or "have met", use conoscere.

sapere: facts and skills

Use sapere for a fact, often followed by dove, come, che or se, and for something you know how to do, followed by an infinitive. It is irregular:

For a skill, English says "can". Italian says sapere: So nuotare means "I can swim" (I know how). Posso means you are able or allowed right now.

conoscere: people and places

Use conoscere for a person or a place you are familiar with. Notice the sound change: conosco and conoscono have a hard sk, the others a soft sh.

{{ex:it.s0192}}{{ex:it.s0193}}

To a stranger, both take the lui / lei form: Sa dov'è la farmacia?

{{ex:it.s0194}}

Lo so, non lo so

On its own, "I know" is usually Lo so, with lo (it) before the verb, just like the object pronouns you learned. For "I don't know" you will hear both Non lo so and Non so.

{{ex:it.s0195}}

False friends

Some Italian words look like English ones but mean something else. They are called false friends (or "false cognates"). Three to learn first:

  • i parenti are your relatives; your parents are i genitori.
  • la camera is a room; a camera is la macchina fotografica.
  • la libreria is a bookstore; a library is la biblioteca.

This lesson's deck has more of these traps, including words that look friendly but mean something else entirely, so review it before you rely on a lookalike.
