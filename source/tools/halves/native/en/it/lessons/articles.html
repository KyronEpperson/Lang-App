English has one "the" and two forms of "a". Italian has seven forms of "the" and four of "a". You choose by three things: the gender of the noun, singular or plural, and the sound the next word starts with.

Masculine: ‹il›, ‹lo›, ‹l'›

Il is the usual masculine "the". Lo comes before z, gn, ps and s + consonant. Before a vowel, the vowel drops and an apostrophe takes its place: l'. This is called elision.

{{ex:it.s0025}}{{ex:it.s0026}}

Feminine: ‹la›, ‹l'›

Feminine nouns take la, or l' before a vowel.

{{ex:it.s0027}}

More than one: ‹i›, ‹gli›, ‹le›

Masculine il becomes i. Both lo and masculine l' become gli. All feminine forms become le, even before a vowel. So the masculine plural depends on the first sound of the noun, but the feminine plural is always le.

The word for "a"

The same sound rules apply. Masculine: un, or uno before ‹z›, ‹gn›, ‹ps› and ‹s› + consonant. Feminine: una, or un' before a vowel. Masculine un never takes an apostrophe: un amico is a friend who is a man, un'amica a friend who is a woman.

"The" where English has none

English says "I like coffee" for coffee in general. Italian uses the article: il caffè. Take mi piace ("I like") as a set phrase for now.
