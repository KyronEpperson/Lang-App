In English, "I want a coffee" sounds abrupt to a waiter, so you say "I'd like". Italian works the same way, and it also has short command forms, called the imperative, for telling people what to do.

Ordering: vorrei

Vorrei means "I would like". It is the polite way to order or ask for anything. Voglio (I want) to a waiter sounds like a demand. Vorrei works with a noun or with an infinitive.

Add per favore at the end, say grazie when your order arrives, and you will sound perfectly polite. In a busy café you can even just name the thing you want and add per favore.

Telling a friend: the tu imperative

For -are verbs, the command ends in -a: guarda! (look!), scusa! (sorry!), aspetta! (wait!). For -ere and -ire verbs it ends in -i, the same as the tu form: prendi (take), senti (listen).

{{ex:it.s0151}}{{ex:it.s0152}}

English adds "don't" for a negative command. For one friend, Italian uses non plus the infinitive: non toccare (don't touch), never non tocca.

{{ex:it.s0153}}

Let's…: the noi form

English says "let's go". Italian simply uses the noi form of the present, with no extra word.

{{ex:it.s0154}}

What you will hear: the Lei imperative

Waiters, shop assistants and strangers giving directions speak to you with Lei, and its command endings are swapped: -are verbs end in -i (giri, turn; scusi, excuse me) and -ere and -ire verbs end in -a (prenda, take; senta, listen). You mostly need to understand these for now.

Prego is everywhere: "you're welcome", "go ahead", "please come in" and, from a waiter, "what can I get you?".

Most common mistakes: ordering with voglio: Voglio un caffè → Vorrei un caffè. And a negative command with the wrong form: Non tocca! → Non toccare!
