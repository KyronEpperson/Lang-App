English "my", "your" and "his" never change. In Italian the possessive agrees in gender and number with the thing owned, and it usually comes with the article: il mio libro, literally "the my book".

suo: his or her

English chooses "his" or "her" by the owner. Italian looks only at the thing owned. La sua casa can mean "his house" or "her house": sua is feminine because casa is. The formal "your", for Lei, is the same word with a capital S: il Suo.

Family: no article in the singular

With one family member, drop the article: mia madre, tuo padre, suo fratello. In the plural it comes back: le mie sorelle, i miei genitori. Loro always keeps it. The rule is for the plain family words you met earlier, such as madre, padre, fratello, sorella, figlio, figlia, marito, moglie. Everything else, from friends to cars, keeps the article: il mio amico, la mia macchina.

{{ex:it.s0091}}{{ex:it.s0092}}

questo: this

Questo has the usual four endings: questo, questa, questi, queste. It can stand alone: Questa è mia sorella, "This is my sister". Introducing people this way is very common, and questo agrees with the person: questa for a woman, questi for a group. Before a vowel it often drops its last letter: quest'anno, this year.

quello: that

Before a noun, quello changes the way the article does: if the noun takes il, it is quel; with lo, quello; with gli, quegli.
