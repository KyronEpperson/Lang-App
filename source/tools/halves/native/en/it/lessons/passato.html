English has several ways to talk about the past: "I ate", "I have eaten", "I did eat". For finished actions, everyday Italian uses one: the passato prossimo. Ho mangiato covers all three.

Two parts: ‹avere› + past participle

The ‹passato prossimo› is built like "I have eaten": the present of avere as the auxiliary, then the past participle. The form of avere matches the subject. The participle stays the same.

Who‹mangiare›English
ioho mangiatoI ate
tuhai mangiatoyou ate
lui, lei, Leiha mangiatohe, she ate; you ate (formal)
noiabbiamo mangiatowe ate

Making the participle

Swap the infinitive ending: -are becomes -ato, -ere becomes -uto, -ire becomes -ito: parlato, venduto, dormito.

With avere, the participle never changes, whoever the subject is: one woman or a group of friends, it is still mangiato. Only avere changes, and its form already tells you who did it, so you can drop the subject pronoun just as you do in the present.

Six common irregular participles

Many -ere verbs have their own participle. Learn these six first: fatto (‹fare›), visto (‹vedere›), preso (‹prendere›), letto (‹leggere›), scritto (‹scrivere›), detto (‹dire›).

"I visited" is ‹passato prossimo›

Don't keep the present for a finished event, and don't hunt for a different tense for "I visited". In speech, Italian uses the ‹passato prossimo› even for things that happened long ago.

{{ex:it.s0163}}

Time words and ‹non›

Questions need no extra word. English adds "did" ("Did you sleep well?"), but Italian just uses the statement with a rising voice, as in the present tense.

Useful words: ieri (yesterday), ieri sera (last night), due anni fa (two years ago), la settimana scorsa and l'anno scorso. Scorso agrees with its noun. Non goes before avere.

{{ex:it.s0164}}
Most common mistake: keeping the present for the past: Ieri mangio una pizza → Ieri ho mangiato una pizza. And with ‹avere› the participle does not change: Giulia ha mangiata → Giulia ha mangiato.
