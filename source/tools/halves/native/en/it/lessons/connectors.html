So far most of your sentences have been short and simple. Small joining words let you say more in one breath, and most of them work just like their English partners.

‹e›, ‹o›, ‹ma›

E is "and", o is "or", ma is "but". Before a word that starts with e, you will often see ed, which is easier to say: Marco ed Elena.

These little words never change for gender or number; only the words around them do. Put a comma before ma, as in English, when the sentence is long.

‹perché›: why and because

English has two words, "why" and "because". Italian has one: perché. So a question with perché is often answered with perché. Write it with an accent that leans right: é.

{{ex:it.s0182}}

‹quando› and ‹se›

Quando means "when", both in questions and in the middle of a sentence. Se means "if". Don't confuse three short words: se (if), sì (yes, with an accent) and si (the reflexive pronoun, as in si chiama).

{{ex:it.s0183}}{{ex:it.s0184}}

‹allora›: so, then

Allora introduces a result, like "so". It is also the word Italians say while they think, like "well…".

{{ex:it.s0185}}

‹anche› and ‹neanche›

Anche means "also" or "too". "Me too" is anch'io (also written anche io). For a negative, use neanche: "me neither" is neanch'io. With piacere, the thing liked is the subject, so "me too" is anche a me.

Putting it together

A connector never changes the verbs around it: each verb still matches its own subject. Here one sentence has two subjects and two verb forms. As in English, a sentence can also start with quando or se, followed by a comma.

{{ex:it.s0188}}
Most common mistake: writing yes without its accent, which turns it into another word: Si, vengo → Sì, vengo. And with ‹piacere›: Mi piace il gelato. Anch'io! → Anche a me!
