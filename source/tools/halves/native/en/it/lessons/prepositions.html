English picks "in", "at" or "to" by meaning. Italian often picks by the kind of place: a city, a country, a shop, a person. In this lesson vado means "I go" or "I'm going"; the whole verb comes in the next lesson.

‹a› and ‹in›

Cities take a, for both "in" and "to". Countries and regions take in. So do many everyday places and means of transport, with no article: in banca, in centro, in treno. Also learn a casa (at home, home) and a piedi (on foot).

‹di›: of, and from a city

Italian has no 's. "Marco's car" is "the car of Marco": la macchina di Marco. Di also says which city you are from. For a country, use the nationality: Sono americano.

{{ex:it.s0099}}{{ex:it.s0100}}

‹da›: from, at someone's place, since

Da means "from" a place, and "to" or "at" a person's place: the doctor's office, a friend's home. It also gives the time something has been going on when it is still true now. English says "I've been here for a week"; Italian says "I am here since a week", in the present.

{{ex:it.s0101}}

Three more: su (on), con (with) and per (for).

Contracted prepositions

When a, di, da, in or su comes before the article, the two join into one word. Grammar books call this an articulated preposition (‹preposizione articolata›). The article part keeps its gender and number.
