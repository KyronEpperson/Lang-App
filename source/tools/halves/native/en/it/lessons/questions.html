English needs a helper to ask most questions: "Do you speak Italian?", "Where does she live?" Italian has no helper at all. You keep the normal sentence and change your voice, or you put a question word in front.

Yes / no questions: just raise your voice

Take a statement and let your voice rise at the end. The words do not change, and nothing is added. In writing, only the question mark shows the difference. The same goes for strangers: use the Lei form of the verb, and still add nothing.

The question words

ItalianEnglish
che cosa, cosa, chewhat
chiwho
dovewhere
quandowhen

qualewhich, what
quantohow much, how many

The three words for "what" mean the same thing: che cosa, cosa and che are all fine. The question word comes first and the verb right after it. If you name the subject, it usually goes after the verb.

Di dove sei? is literally "Of where are you?". To a stranger, it is Di dov'è?

‹quanto› agrees with its noun

English "how many" never changes. Before a noun, quanto takes the gender and number of that noun: quanto, quanta, quanti, quante.

{{ex:it.s0063}}{{ex:it.s0064}}

‹quale› and ‹qual è›

Quale means "which", and often "what" when you choose from a set. Before è it shortens to qual, with no apostrophe: qual è. For more than one, it is quali. Unlike quanto, it does not change for gender.

{{ex:it.s0065}}

‹perché›: why and because

One word does both jobs. You ask with perché, and the answer often starts with perché too.

{{ex:it.s0066}}
Most common mistake: leaving quanto in one form. Quanti sorelle hai? → Quante sorelle hai?, because sorelle is feminine plural.
