In the last lesson the passato prossimo used avere: ho mangiato. A small group of very common verbs, mostly about going, coming and changing, use essere as the auxiliary instead. English has no model for this, so learn the list.

The essere verbs

For now, these ten: andare (go), venire (come), arrivare (arrive), partire (leave), tornare (come back), uscire (go out), entrare (go in), stare (stay, be), essere (be) and nascere (be born). Their participles: andato, venuto, arrivato, partito, tornato, uscito, entrato, stato (for both stare and essere) and nato.

The participle agrees like an adjective

With essere, the past participle behaves like an adjective: it agrees with the subject in gender and number, just like stanco, stanca, stanchi, stanche.

You already do this after sono with adjectives. If the speaker is a woman, the adjective and the participle both end in -a; for a group of women, both end in -e. With avere verbs nothing changes, so this agreement is the extra step that comes with essere.

A group of men and women takes the masculine plural, -i.

Born, been

English "was born" is passive; Italian simply uses nascere with essere. Sono stato or sono stata means "I was" or "I've been", as in a place you visited.

Reflexive verbs take essere too

Every reflexive verb uses essere in the passato prossimo. The pronoun goes first, then essere, and the participle agrees.

{{ex:it.s0171}}

avere or essere?

A quick test: if the verb has a direct object (something you eat, see, buy or take), it uses avere. The ten verbs above never have one. The test only works one way: many verbs with no object still take avere, such as ho dormito. So one story can mix both auxiliaries.

{{ex:it.s0172}}
Most common mistakes: using avere with a movement verb: Ho andato → Sono andato. And forgetting the agreement: Giulia è andato → Giulia è andata.
