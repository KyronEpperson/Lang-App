Italian numbers are regular once you know a few small rules. You need them for prices, times and dates from your first day in Italy.

Building the numbers

1–1011–19Tens
uno, due, tre, quattro, cinqueundici, dodici, tredici, quattordici, quindiciventi, trenta, quaranta, cinquanta
sei, sette, otto, nove, diecisedici, diciassette, diciotto, diciannovesessanta, settanta, ottanta, novanta

‹Zero› is zero. From twenty on, the ten and the unit are written as one word: ventidue, trentacinque. Two small changes: before uno and otto, the ten drops its last vowel, and a final tre gets an accent mark.

A hundred is cento and a thousand is mille, with no word for "a" or "one" in front. Two thousand is duemila: mille becomes mila in the plural.

Telling the time

To ask the time, say Che ore sono? (or Che ora è?). The answer uses the word ore (hours), which is feminine plural, so it is Sono le + the number. One o'clock is a single hour, so it is È l'una. For half past, add e mezza.

Dates

English says "September 15". Italian says "the fifteen September": il + number + month. Months are lowercase. The first of the month is the only one with "first": il primo.

Prices

Ask Quanto costa? about one thing and Quanto costano? about more than one: the verb agrees with what you are buying. Euro never changes: un euro, dieci euro. A cent is un centesimo, and the plural is centesimi.
