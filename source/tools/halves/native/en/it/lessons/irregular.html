Four of the most common Italian verbs do not follow the regular patterns: andare (to go), venire (to come), fare (to do, to make) and stare (to be, to stay). You will use them in almost every conversation, so learn them as a set.

The ‹noi› and ‹voi› forms are close to regular. The changes are in the other four, so practice those most. With a stranger, use the Lei form, which is the same as the ‹lui› / ‹lei› form: Dove va? (Where are you going?), Come sta? (How are you?). For a group of friends, the ‹voi› form: Dove andate?

‹andare›: going

Andare takes the prepositions from the last lesson: a for cities and a casa, in for countries. With a day, no word for "on" is needed.

‹venire›: coming

English says "I'm coming" when you move toward the person you are talking to. Italian does the same with venire: vengo, not vado. Venire da also says where someone comes from: with a city, da alone; with a country, the contracted preposition (dagli Stati Uniti, dall'Italia).

{{ex:it.s0108}}{{ex:it.s0109}}

‹fare›: doing, and the weather

English says "it is cold" about the weather. Italian says "it makes cold": fa freddo, fa caldo, fa bel tempo. Fare also appears in everyday phrases such as fare colazione, to have breakfast. To ask about the weather, say Che tempo fa?, literally "What weather does it make?". The answer uses fa every time, because the weather has no real subject.

‹stare›: how you are

You already know Come stai? Use stare for how you feel: sto bene, sto male. Stare per + an infinitive means "to be about to".

Most common mistake: using essere for the weather, as English does. È freddo oggi → Fa freddo oggi.
