English says "I get up", "I wash", "I get dressed" and leaves out the "myself". Italian says it every time: mi alzo is literally "I get myself up". Verbs that work like this are called reflexive verbs, and the small word in front is the reflexive pronoun.

Spotting them

In the dictionary a reflexive verb ends in -si: alzarsi (to get up), svegliarsi (to wake up), lavarsi (to wash), vestirsi (to get dressed), chiamarsi (to be called). Drop the -si and you have an ordinary verb with the usual endings.

Two things match the subject

The pronoun changes with the person, and so does the verb ending. Both have to match the subject.

Notice the last one: English says "their hands", Italian says "the hands". The pronoun si already tells you whose.

My name is…

You have used mi chiamo since the first lessons. Now you can see why: it means "I call myself". Without mi, Chiamo Emma means "I'm calling Emma", on the phone.

{{ex:it.s0136}}

With an infinitive

After devo, posso or voglio, the pronoun can join the end of the infinitive, which loses its last -e. It can also go before the conjugated verb. Both are correct, and the pronoun still matches the subject.

{{ex:it.s0137}}{{ex:it.s0138}}

Two everyday phrases

Mi faccio la doccia is the usual way to say "I take a shower". Ci vediamo! ("we see each other") means "See you!", and you can add a time: Ci vediamo domani!

Most common mistake: leaving out the pronoun, as in English. Alzo alle sette → Mi alzo alle sette. And remember: Chiamo Emma (I'm calling Emma) → Mi chiamo Emma (My name is Emma).
