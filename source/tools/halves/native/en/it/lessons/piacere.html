English says "I like pizza": "I" is the subject. Italian turns the sentence around: "pizza is pleasing to me", Mi piace la pizza. The thing you like is the subject, so the verb agrees with it, not with you.

‹piace› or ‹piacciono›

One thing takes piace. More than one takes piacciono. The article stays, as you saw in the lesson on articles.

Who likes it

The small word in front says who likes it. Notice that gli covers "he" and, in everyday speech, "they"; le is "she", and with a capital L it is the formal "you", just as lei and Lei work. It means "to me", "to you" and so on, and grammar books call it an indirect object pronoun. It goes before the verb.

Liking to do something

With an infinitive (liking to do something), the verb is always piace: an activity counts as one thing, even if you list several. The infinitive comes after piace, as in English "I like to cook".

{{ex:it.s0128}}

Not liking it

Put non in front of the small word, never between it and the verb: Non mi piace. To ask a friend, just raise your voice: Ti piace? The answer can be as short as Sì, mi piace or No, non mi piace; you do not need a word for "it", because the thing you like is already the subject.

{{ex:it.s0129}}

Me too, me neither

English answers "me too" and "me neither". Because Italian says "to me", the answers are anche a me and neanche a me, never anch'io. A me is the stressed form of mi; you can also start with it for emphasis: A me piace.

Most common mistake: making the person the subject, as in English. Io piace la pizza → Mi piace la pizza, and Mi piace i libri → Mi piacciono i libri.
