English has many small place words (in, at, on, to, from) and one "where". Indonesian sorts places by one question: are you there, going there, or coming from there?

di: at, in (where something is)

di covers English "at", "in" and often "on" when nothing is moving. There is still no word for "am" or "is".

{{ex:id.s0115}}

ke: to (where you are going)

ke marks a destination. After pergi (go) or pulang (go home) you need it, just as English needs "to". In everyday speech ke can even do the job alone: Saya ke kantor, "I'm going to the office".

{{ex:id.s0116}}

dari: from

dari marks where someone or something comes from, as in Saya dari Amerika.

{{ex:id.s0117}}

Three ways to ask "where"

Because the place words split three ways, so does "where": di mana (where, at), ke mana (where to), dari mana (where from). The question word can stay where the answer would go.

On, under, in front of

For more exact places, add a position word after di, then the place. The main place word comes first and the owner comes last, as always.

Spelling: two words

di and ke before a place are separate words: di rumah, ke Bali, di mana. Indonesians themselves sometimes run them together, but it is a spelling mistake. (Later you will meet a di- that is joined to a verb; that is a different thing.)

Most common mistake: using di for movement because English "go to" and "be at" both feel like places. Saya pergi di Bali → Saya pergi ke Bali.
