Telling the time in Indonesian uses the numbers you know plus one key word, jam. Its position changes the meaning completely, so watch where it goes.

jam before the number: o'clock

English says "three o'clock", with the number first. Indonesian says jam tiga, literally "hour three". Put the number first and you get a length of time instead: tiga jam means "three hours".

{{ex:id.s0099}}{{ex:id.s0100}}{{ex:id.s0101}}

So Jam berapa? asks "what time?", while Berapa jam? asks "how many hours?". In news, timetables and invitations you will see pukul in place of jam: pukul tujuh is a formal "seven o'clock".

Half past: count toward the next hour

This is the big trap. English "half past two" counts up from two. Indonesian setengah tiga means "half (way to) three", which is 2:30, not 3:30.

{{ex:id.s0102}}

Minutes and quarters

lewat means "past" and kurang means "to" (literally "less"). A quarter is seperempat.

TimeIndonesian
3:10jam tiga lewat sepuluh
4:15jam empat lewat seperempat
2:45jam tiga kurang seperempat

Morning, afternoon, night

English says "at three"; in everyday Indonesian there is no word for "at" before a clock time: you just say the time.

{{ex:id.s0103}}

Instead of a.m. and p.m., add the part of the day after the hour: pagi (morning), siang (midday, about 11 to 3), sore (late afternoon), malam (evening and night).

{{ex:id.s0104}}{{ex:id.s0105}}

Days and dates

Days usually take hari (day) in front: hari Senin, Monday. The week runs Senin, Selasa, Rabu, Kamis, Jumat, Sabtu, Minggu. Dates take tanggal and put the day before the month.

Most common mistake: putting the number first for a clock time. tiga jam for three o'clock → jam tiga. And remember setengah tiga is 2:30.
