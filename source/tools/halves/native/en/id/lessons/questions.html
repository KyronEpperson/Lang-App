Indonesian questions are simpler than English ones: there is no "do" or "does", and nothing changes order.

Yes / no questions: just raise your voice

English turns "You are hungry" around into "Are you hungry?". Indonesian keeps the sentence as it is and lets your voice rise at the end. In writing and formal speech, Apakah can start the question, and in speech you also hear a plain Apa at the start. All three are correct. Ya means "yes".

Three words for "where"

English "where" covers everything. Indonesian asks di mana for where something is, ke mana for where someone is going, and dari mana for where someone comes from. Pergi means "go". On its own, Dari mana? is also a friendly street greeting: "Where have you been?"

The question word can stay put

English moves the question word to the front. In Indonesian it can stay right where the answer would go: Saya tinggal di Jakarta becomes Kamu tinggal di mana?. Putting it first is fine too.

{{ex:id.s0039}}

Whose: the thing, then siapa

For "whose", follow the owner rule: the thing first, then siapa in the owner's place. "Whose car?" is mobil siapa, literally "car who".

Names, prices and "How are you?"

Remember that names take siapa: Siapa nama Anda? And "How are you?" is Apa kabar?, never a word-for-word Bagaimana kamu? For prices, berapa plus harganya (the price) is the everyday question.
