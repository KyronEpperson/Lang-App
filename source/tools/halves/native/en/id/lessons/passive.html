English says "The room has been booked". Indonesian builds the same idea with the prefix di-: Kamarnya sudah dipesan. The thing comes first, then the di- verb.

Making a di- verb

Take a meN- verb, take off meN- to get the root back, and put di- on the root. Any letter that dropped after meN- comes back: menulis lost its t, and ditulis has it again. Only verbs that take an object, the meN- kind, have a di- form.

Time words still do the work

English changes "is", "was" and "will be". Indonesian keeps the di- verb as it is and adds a time word when time matters: sudah dipesan, akan dikirim.

{{ex:id.s0175}}

"By": oleh

To say who did it, add oleh ("by"). In speech, oleh is often left out when the person comes right after the verb.

{{ex:id.s0176}}{{ex:id.s0177}}

Indonesians like the passive

English uses the passive mostly in formal writing. Indonesian uses it all the time in speech, often where English would say "you" or "they": "Where do you buy tickets?" is naturally Tiketnya dibeli di mana?. Using it will make you sound more natural, not more formal.

{{ex:id.s0178}}

One word or two?

The di- of a verb is written joined: dimakan. The word di before a place ("at, in") is written apart: di rumah. The same spelling rule applies in every sentence.

{{ex:id.s0179}}
Most common mistake: mixing up the two kinds of di. di makan dirumah → dimakan di rumah.
