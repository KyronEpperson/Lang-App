Indonesian has no word for "my" or "your" and no apostrophe-s. The owner simply comes after the thing.

The owner goes last

English says "my book" and "Budi's car", owner first. Indonesian says buku saya, "book I", and mobil Budi, "car Budi". Any pronoun, name or title can be the owner.

{{ex:id.s0068}}{{ex:id.s0069}}

A chain of owners keeps the same order, so it reads backwards from English: "my friend's house" is rumah teman saya, "house friend I".

{{ex:id.s0070}}

To say "your" politely, use the title: rumah Ibu is "your house" to an older woman, and mobil Bapak is "your car" to an older man.

Short endings: -ku, -mu, -nya

Three owners can also be written as an ending on the noun:

EndingMeaningExample
-kumy (from aku; friendly)bukuku
-muyour (from kamu; to a friend)bukumu
-nyahis, her, its, theirbukunya
{{ex:id.s0071}}{{ex:id.s0072}}{{ex:id.s0073}}

Buku saya is right everywhere; bukuku sounds friendly, and bukumu is only for people you would call kamu.

-nya as "the"

The ending -nya also points to a thing you both know, much like English "the". At a market, harganya is "its price" or "the price".

{{ex:id.s0074}}

Sentences with an owner

A phrase with an owner works like any other noun phrase. Add a describing word after it, with no word for "is".

{{ex:id.s0075}}{{ex:id.s0076}}

Having: punya

To say what you have, put punya (have) between the person and the thing.

{{ex:id.s0077}}

So in standard Indonesian, Saya punya buku is "I have a book", and "my book" is buku saya. (In parts of eastern Indonesia you will also hear saya punya buku for "my book".)

Most common mistake: the English order, owner first. saya buku → buku saya; Budi mobil → mobil Budi.
