Meeting people in Indonesia, you will answer the same few questions again and again. The good news: none of the answers needs a word for "am".

Name and country

English says "My name is Emma" and "I'm from the US". Indonesian says "name my Emma" and "I from America": no "is", no "am".

{{ex:id.s0022}}{{ex:id.s0032}}

Amerika usually means the United States. To give your nationality, put orang (person) before the country: orang Amerika, "an American", literally "person America".

{{ex:id.s0033}}

Where you live

Tinggal is "live, stay", and di means "in" or "at" a place.

{{ex:id.s0034}}

Your age

English uses "be" for age: "I'm 25". Indonesian says "my age 25 years": umur saya plus the number plus tahun (year). You will learn the numbers in a later lesson; for now, dua puluh lima is 25. Never use punya (have) for age.

{{ex:id.s0035}}

Job and languages

For your job, say Saya bekerja sebagai (I work as) plus the job. For a language, bisa (can) plus the language means "speak it", and sedikit is "a little".

{{ex:id.s0036}}{{ex:id.s0037}}

Asking back

Names are asked with siapa, "who", not apa, "what": literally "Who is your name?". To ask where someone is from, Dari mana?, "From where?", is enough. Use Anda with a stranger when no title fits; with an older man say Bapak, with an older woman Ibu.

{{ex:id.s0031}}{{ex:id.s0038}}{{ex:id.s0039}}

Short answers are normal: to Dari mana? you can simply say Dari Amerika. Once everyone knows who is talking, Indonesians often drop saya as well, so do not worry about repeating it in every sentence.

Most common mistake: asking a name with "what". Apa nama Anda? → Siapa nama Anda?
