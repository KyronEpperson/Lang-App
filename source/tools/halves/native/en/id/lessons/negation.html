English has one little word for "not" and tucks it in after "am", "is" or "do": I'm not hungry, I don't have a car. Indonesian has no "am" or "do" to lean on, so it chooses its negative word by what comes right after it.

tidak: not with verbs and describing words

Put tidak straight before a describing word (hungry, hot, big) or a verb (live, have, go). Nothing else changes, and there is still no word for "am" or "is".

bukan: not with nouns

When the thing after "not" is a noun (a teacher, my house, an American), Indonesian uses bukan. English says "I'm not a teacher"; Indonesian says Saya bukan guru, literally "I not-a teacher".

{{ex:id.s0088}}{{ex:id.s0089}}

A quick test: if you could put "a", "my" or "this" after the English "not", you want bukan. If you could put "very" there, or a verb, you want tidak.

Short answers

To answer no, echo the negative word the question needs. Kamu lapar? (Are you hungry?) gets Tidak. Itu mobil kamu? (Is that your car?) gets Bukan. Yes is Ya.

Two more words for "not"

You will meet two more soon. belum means "not yet", and it is the polite answer to many questions where English says a flat no. jangan means "don't" in a command: never use tidak to tell someone not to do something.

Most common mistake: using tidak before a noun. Saya tidak guru → Saya bukan guru. And the reverse: Dia bukan lapar → Dia tidak lapar.
