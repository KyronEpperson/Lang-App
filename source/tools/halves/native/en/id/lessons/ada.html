English says "there is" and "there are", and asks "Is there…?" or "Do you have…?". Indonesian covers all of these with one short word, ada.

ada: there is, there are

Put ada in front of the thing. It never changes for one or many, or for past and present. A number still goes before the noun, and a describing word after it.

{{ex:id.s0108}}

To ask, just raise your voice at the end. The answer is the same word: Ada. (Yes, there is.)

{{ex:id.s0109}}{{ex:id.s0110}}

tidak ada: there isn't any

For "there isn't" or "there aren't any", put tidak before ada. On its own, Tidak ada. is the short answer "No, there isn't."

{{ex:id.s0111}}{{ex:id.s0112}}

ada for "do you have…?"

In shops, cafes and hotels, Indonesians ask what is available with ada, not with a word for "have". The staff answer Ada or Tidak ada. To say you own something, use punya: Saya punya mobil.

{{ex:id.s0113}}

ada before a place

To say where someone is, you need no verb at all: Dia di rumah, "she's at home". You will also hear ada slipped in before the place, with the same meaning. Both are correct.

{{ex:id.s0114}}

ada is not adalah

They look alike, but they are different words. ada says that something exists or is somewhere. adalah only links two noun phrases in careful speech (Ibu kota Indonesia adalah Jakarta). It never means "there is", and it never goes before a place.

Most common mistake: reaching for adalah because it looks like "is". Adalah apotek di dekat sini? → Ada apotek di dekat sini?
