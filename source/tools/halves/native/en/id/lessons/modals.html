English puts "to" after "want" and "need", and has odd rules for "can", "must" and "may". Indonesian is simpler: a helping word goes straight before the verb, and nothing else changes.

Six helping words

IndonesianEnglish
mauwant to (everyday)
inginwant to, wish to (a little more careful)
bisacan, be able to
harusmust, have to
bolehmay, be allowed to
perluneed to

The helping word is the same for "I", "you" and "she", and it does not change for the past or the future either. When time matters, a time word goes in front: Saya sudah bisa berenang means "I can swim now".

No word for "to"

English says "I want to eat". Indonesian has nothing in that spot: Saya mau makan. Adding untuk ("for, in order to") is a calque, a word-for-word copy from English.

{{ex:id.s0149}}{{ex:id.s0150}}

bisa: can, and "speak"

bisa is about being able to. With a language it means "speak": Bisa bahasa Inggris? is "Do you speak English?". In writing you will also see dapat.

{{ex:id.s0151}}{{ex:id.s0152}}

boleh: may, allowed

boleh is about permission, not ability. To ask "May I…?", start with Boleh saya. English speakers often say "Can I…?" for permission; in Indonesian, think "Am I allowed?" and use boleh. The short answers are Boleh ("Sure, go ahead") and Tidak boleh ("That's not allowed").

{{ex:id.s0153}}{{ex:id.s0154}}

Saying no

tidak goes in front of the helping word: tidak bisa (can't), tidak boleh (not allowed), tidak perlu (no need to).

{{ex:id.s0155}}

Asking for help

Bisa tolong + verb is a polite "Could you…?". The next lesson is all about tolong.

Most common mistake: copying the English "to". Saya mau untuk pergi → Saya mau pergi.
