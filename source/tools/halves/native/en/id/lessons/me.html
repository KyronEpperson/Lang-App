The second big prefix is me-. Where a ber- verb usually stands alone, a me- verb usually does something TO something: you read a book, write a letter, cook rice. In careful speech these verbs wear their prefix.

The prefix changes shape

The tricky part is that the prefix ends in a nasal sound (m, n, ng or ny), and the nasal changes to fit the first sound of the root. English does something similar: "in-" becomes "im-" in "impossible". Here are the patterns with the verbs you need most.

Rootme- verbEnglish
lihatmelihatto see
bacamembacato read
carimencarito look for
pakaimemakaito use, to wear
tulismenulisto write
kirimmengirimto send
sewamenyewato rent
  • Before l, r, m, n, w, y the prefix is plain me-: melihat, memasak.
  • Before b it is mem-, and before c it is men-: membaca, mencari.
  • A root starting with p, t, k or s loses that letter, and the nasal takes its place: pakai → memakai, tulis → menulis, kirim → mengirim, sewa → menyewa.

Some everyday me- verbs are best learned whole, such as menyanyi (to sing) and mengerti (to understand).

Time and order stay the same

The verb still never changes for time; time words go in front of it, and the thing after the verb keeps its own noun-first order.

In everyday speech

Casual speech often uses the bare root: Saya baca buku. You will hear it constantly, and it is fine in conversation. In this course, typed answers use the full me- form, the one that is right everywhere.

Most common mistake: sticking the prefix on without changing it. mengbaca → membaca, mempakai → memakai, mentulis → menulis.
