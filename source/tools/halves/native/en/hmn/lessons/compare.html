English changes the describing word to compare: big, bigger, biggest; expensive, more expensive. Hmong never changes the word. It adds tshaj falling, "more than", right after it.

Bigger than

The pattern is: the first thing, the describing word, tshaj, then the second thing. In a formula: X loj tshaj Y, "X is bigger than Y". There is no "is" and no separate "than".

Everything you know about noun phrases still applies on both sides: the counting word before the noun, and no or ntawd after it. This is handy at the market, when you hold up two things and compare prices:

{{ex:hmn.s0186}}

Both sides are full noun phrases, so the sentence looks long, but every piece is one you already know: counting word, noun, then this or that.

The most

Leave out the second thing and tshaj means "the most": zoo tshaj is "the best".

{{ex:hmn.s0187}}

To ask "which one", "who", keep the question word where the answer goes, as always.

{{ex:hmn.s0188}}

Very and extremely

English puts "very" before the describing word. Hmong puts heev rising after it. kawg falling-breathy, in the same place, makes it stronger still: "extremely".

One word list also gives dua mid for "more than". This course uses tshaj; if your family says dua, follow them.

Most common mistake: adding a word for "more" in front, the English way. Kav fes ntau kim tshuaj yej. → Kav fes kim tshaj tshuaj yej.
