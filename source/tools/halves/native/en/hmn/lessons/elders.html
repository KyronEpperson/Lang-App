With grandparents and older relatives, a few set phrases do a lot of work. They come from Hmong courses and phrase lists; ask your family which ones they use.

When an elder arrives

English says "Hi, welcome!". Hmong points out the obvious, warmly: Koj tuaj los? falling · falling · low, "You've come?". The final los makes it a question that expects a yes. A visitor answers Peb tuaj os, "Yes, we've come".

Use the family word

English speakers say "you" to anyone. Many Hmong families put weight on kinship words, so with your grandmother, start with pog falling-breathy (father's mother) or niam tais creaky · low, and with your grandfather, yawg falling-breathy. Your family can tell you exactly which words they expect.

{{ex:hmn.s0193}}

Watch the tone letter: pog (breathy tone) is grandmother, pob (high tone) is a ball, and pov (rising tone) is to throw.

Have you eaten?

A common check-in is not "How are you?" but Koj noj mov lawm los tsis yog?, "Have you eaten yet?". Answer that you have eaten, or that you have not yet: tsis tau goes before the verb.

When an elder leaves

To a guest who is leaving, say Mus ho tuaj low · mid · falling, "come again", or mus zoo low · mid, "go well". To ask them to stay a little longer: Nyob. Wb tham mentsis tso maj. high · high · creaky · mid · low · mid · falling

Soft words

thov before the verb and os at the end make any request gentle, and many families expect them when younger people speak to elders.
