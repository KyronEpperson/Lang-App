English changes the verb for the past: go, went. Hmong verbs never change. A time word tells you when, and it usually comes at the start of the sentence.

Today, yesterday, tomorrow

hnub notoday
nag hmoyesterday
tag kistomorrow (also written taskis)

The verb mus is the same both times. Only the time word changed.

Parts of the day

sawv ntxov rising · rising is morning, tav su rising · mid noon and early afternoon, tsaus ntuj low · falling evening and hmo ntuj mid · falling night.

{{ex:hmn.s0105}}

For clock times, US Hmong texts write the digit followed by the part of the day. teev rising means "hour" or "o'clock".

{{ex:hmn.s0106}}

Days and weeks

Day is hnub high. US Hmong agencies name the weekdays with the English name after hnub: hnub Monday, hnub Friday. Some families and books number the days (hnub ib, hnub ob …), but the books disagree on whether hnub ib is Sunday or Monday, so ask your family. hnub so high · mid is a day off (older texts use it for Sunday). A week is lim tiam creaky · creaky (also as thiv), and every day is txhua hnub mid · high.

{{ex:hmn.s0107}}{{ex:hmn.s0108}}

Months and years

Months are numbered: ib hlis ntuj high · low · falling is January, ob hlis ntuj February, and so on up to kaum ob hlis ntuj, December. Copy each name as written, because a few have hli instead of hlis: rau hli ntuj (June), yim hli ntuj (August), kaum hli ntuj (October). A year is xyoo mid. Next year is lwm xyoo, and last year is tsaib no.

{{ex:hmn.s0109}}

To ask "when?", use thaum twg creaky · falling-breathy.

Most common mistake: hunting for a past form of the verb. For "Yesterday I went to the store", Kuv mus tom khw on its own → Nag hmo kuv mus tom khw. The time word does the work.
