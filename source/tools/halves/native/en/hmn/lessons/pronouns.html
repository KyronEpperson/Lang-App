English has I, you, he, she, it, we and they. Hmong has nine pronouns, because it counts: one person, two people, or three or more.

One word for he, she and it

nws low means he, she or it. Hmong does not mark the person's sex here; the situation tells you. English speakers sometimes hunt for a separate word for she: there is none.

The two of us

English "we" can be two people or twenty. Hmong splits it. wb high is exactly two (you and I, or she and I), and peb high is three or more. The same split holds for you (neb high two, nej falling three or more) and for they (nkawd rising-final two, lawv rising three or more). Some books write nkawv for nkawd.

I, me and my are one word

Hmong pronouns never change form. kuv is I, me and my; koj is you and your. Where the word sits tells you which.

One "you" for everyone

English has one "you", and so does Hmong for one person: koj. Phrase books use it with strangers as well as friends. Politeness comes from other words, such as thov (please) and the soft os, and, with older relatives, from family words that a later lesson teaches.

Watch the tone letters

neb and nej differ only in the tone letter, and so do kuv and kub (hot). peb is also the number three. Green Hmong uses other forms for some of these (mej for nej, nwg for nws, puab for lawv); this course writes the White ones.

Most common mistake: using peb for just two people. "The two of us are fine" as Peb nyob zoo → Wb nyob zoo.
