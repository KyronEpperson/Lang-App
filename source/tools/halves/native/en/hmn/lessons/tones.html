This course teaches White Hmong (Hmoob Dawb high · high) as written by Hmong Americans, in the Hmong Latin alphabet (RPA). Green Hmong (Mong Leeg) speakers will understand you, and the notes show where Green Hmong differs.

Every Hmong syllable is spelled in up to three parts: a first consonant, a vowel, and a tone letter. Hmoob is hm + oo + b. Each syllable is written separately, with a space, even inside one word: phooj ywg (friend) is one word. This lesson starts with the part English does not have, the tone letter, then shows every consonant and vowel.

The last letter is not a sound

English says every letter it writes. Hmong does not: no consonant is ever said at the end of a syllable. When a syllable is written with b, j, v, s, g, m or d at the end, that letter is silent. It is the tone letter: it tells you the tone, the pitch and voice of the syllable. So Hmoob high is "Hmong" said with a high tone, never "h-moo-b".

{{ex:hmn.s0001}}

A syllable with no tone letter has the mid tone: zoo mid (good). So Nyob zoo is high, then mid.

Jay Xiong, a Hmong dictionary author, calls the -s tone "middle" where Wikipedia calls it "low"; this course says low. And -d appears only in a few words at the end of a phrase, such as ntawd rising-final (that) and tod rising-final (over there).

One letter, a new word

English can say "dog" high or low and it is still a dog. In Hmong the tone letter changes the word: dej is water but dev rising is dog; kuv is I but kub high is hot. When you type an answer, the tone letter must be right: there is no "almost".

First consonants

A syllable starts with a consonant, a cluster of letters that stands for one sound, or no consonant at all (ib, one). White Hmong has 56 first consonants, all on the tiles below. Many are close to English: p, t, k, d, f, v, h, l, m, n and y (but say p, t and k with no puff of air, like the p in spin). Others have a new value (x, s, z, r, c, q). And three patterns build most clusters: an h after a consonant is a puff of air, an n in front is a short hum through the nose (but ny is one sound, the ny of canyon), and an h in front of m, n or l lets air out first. On a tile, +puff means a puff of air follows, +air means breathy, and curled means the tongue is curled back. Read each row from the plain consonant on the left. Lessons 2 and 3 practise them one by one.

Vowels

After the consonant comes one of 13 vowels. Five are plain: a (ah), e (ay, with no y glide), i (ee), o (aw) and u (oo). w is always a vowel, never a consonant. A doubled vowel, ee or oo, goes through your nose. And five are glides from one vowel to another: ai, au, aw, ia, ua.

Putting a syllable together

Now read whole words. Every syllable is read left to right in three steps. First the consonant: take the longest spelling that fits, so ntxhais starts with ntxh, not n. Then the vowel. Then look at the last letter: if it is b, j, v, s, g, m or d, it is the tone letter, not a sound; if the word ends in a vowel, the tone is mid. So ntxhais is ntxh + ai + s: a hum, ts with a puff, "eye", low tone. Each card's note splits the word into its three parts. Say it aloud before you flip it.

No voice yet

No computer voice speaks Hmong yet, so this course has no sound. Read the tone line under each card, and get the real sound from family, friends and Hmong video lessons.

Most common mistake: saying the tone letter as a consonant. Hmoob as "h-moo-b" → Hmoob, "Hmong" with a high tone and no b sound. Ask a Hmong speaker to say pob, poj, pov, po, pos, pog, pom for you.
