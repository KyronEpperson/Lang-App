English is relaxed about these words: "I'm American too", "We all like tea", "We like tea, all of us". Chinese is strict. 也 yě (also), 都 dōu (all, both) and 还 hái (still) sit in one place only: after the subject, right before the verb or adjective.

也: also, too

English often puts "too" at the end. Chinese never does: 也 goes before 是, before other verbs, and before adjectives with 很.

{{ex:zh.s0202}}{{ex:zh.s0203}}

也 with no: "not … either"

English switches "too" to "either" in a negative sentence. Chinese keeps 也 and puts 不 or 没 right after it: 也不, 也没.

{{ex:zh.s0204}}

都: all, both

都 looks back at the people or things already named, so they have to come first: 我们都…, never 都我们…. It covers "both" as well as "all".

{{ex:zh.s0205}}{{ex:zh.s0206}}

也 and 都 together

When you need both, 也 comes first: 也都, "all … too".

{{ex:zh.s0207}}

都 with 不

The order changes the meaning. 都不 means "none": all of them, not. 不都 means "not all".

{{ex:zh.s0208}}
ChineseMeaning
我们都不是老师。None of us is a teacher.
我们不都是老师。Not all of us are teachers.

还: still, also, not yet

还 also goes before the verb. With 没 it means "not yet": 还没 + verb, and no 了, because nothing has happened yet. In shops and restaurants, 还要 means "also want": "anything else?"

{{ex:zh.s0209}}{{ex:zh.s0210}}

On its own, before a verb, 还 means "still": the action has not stopped. It combines well with 在 from the lesson on actions in progress.

{{ex:zh.s0211}}
Most common mistake: putting 也 at the end like "too", or 都 before the subject. 我是美国人也 → 我也是美国人. 都我们是学生 → 我们都是学生.
