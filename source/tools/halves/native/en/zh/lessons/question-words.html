The question word stays where the answer goes

English moves its question word to the front: "You live in New York" becomes "Where do you live?". Chinese does not move anything. Take the answer, put the question word in its place, and the question is done.

QuestionAnswer
你叫什么名字？我叫王明。
他是谁？他是我朋友。
你是哪国人？我是美国人。

What and who: 什么, 谁

什么 shénme is "what", and 谁 shéi is "who" (some people say shuí). To ask someone's name, say "you are called what name?". 谁 can also be the subject, again in the answer's place: 谁是王明？ Shéi shì Wáng Míng? (who is Wang Ming?) matches 他是王明。

Where and which country: 哪儿, 哪国人

哪儿 nǎr is "where". To ask where someone does something, 在 (at) + 哪儿 goes before the verb, just as a place would in the answer. 哪 means "which", so 哪国人 nǎguórén asks "a person of which country?".

To answer, swap in your own word: 你是哪国人？ becomes 我是美国人。, with 美国 in the place of 哪国.

How many: 几

几 jǐ asks "how many" when you expect a small number. Like a number, it needs a measure word before the noun: 几个.

For numbers you expect to be bigger, Chinese uses another word, 多少 duōshao, which you will meet with prices.

No 吗

A question word already makes a question, so it never takes 吗 as well. Add 请问 in front to sound polite with a stranger.

Short answers

As in English, you can answer with just the missing part: to 你叫什么名字？, simply 王明。 A full sentence is also fine, and it is good practice while the order is new. In the north people say 哪儿; in the south and in Taiwan you will hear 哪里 nǎlǐ. Both mean "where", and both are correct.

Most common mistake: moving the question word to the front, or adding 吗. 什么你叫名字？ → 你叫什么名字？; 你住哪儿吗？ → 你住哪儿？
