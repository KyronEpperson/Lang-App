A few small words let you join short sentences into longer ones. Most work like their English partners; the traps are in spelling and sound.

e, ou, mas

E (and), ou (or) and mas (but) join words or whole sentences, as in English. E sounds like "ee". Two people joined by e are plural, so the verb takes the eles form.

{{ex:pt.s0192}}{{ex:pt.s0193}}{{ex:pt.s0194}}

mas or mais?

Mas means "but"; mais means "more". In much of Brazil they sound almost the same, so the spelling is what you must get right. A quick test: if you could say "more" in English, write mais; if you could say "but", write mas.

{{ex:pt.s0195}}

porque and por que

Porque, one word with no accent, means "because". The question "why?" is Por que...? at the start and Por quê? at the end, as in the lesson on question words.

{{ex:pt.s0196}}

então, quando, também

Então means "so": it introduces the result. You will also hear it alone at the start of a sentence, like English "so..." or "well...".

{{ex:pt.s0197}}

Quando (when) joins two sentences about habits or about the past. Either half can come first, just as in English.

{{ex:pt.s0198}}{{ex:pt.s0199}}

Também is "also" or "too", and Eu também is "me too". English switches to "either" in a negative sentence; Portuguese keeps também and adds não: Eu também não (me neither).

{{ex:pt.s0200}}

Putting them together

Two or three of these words are enough to turn short answers into a real conversation. Each one keeps its own job, and the verbs still match their subjects.

{{ex:pt.s0201}}
Most common mistake: writing mais for mas. Estou cansado, mais feliz → Estou cansado, mas feliz.
