Some of the most common verbs don't follow the regular endings. Four of them carry a lot of everyday Portuguese: ir (to go), fazer (to do, to make), querer (to want) and poder (can).

The four verbs

‹ir›‹fazer›‹querer›‹poder›
‹eu›voufaçoqueroposso
‹você›, ‹ele›, ‹ela›, ‹a gente›vaifazquerpode
‹nós›vamosfazemosqueremospodemos
‹vocês›, ‹eles›, ‹elas›vãofazemquerempodem

The ‹ele› form still goes with você and a gente: a gente vai, never a gente vamos.

‹ir›: going places

English says "go to". Portuguese says ir a or ir para, and the a fuses with the article: ao shopping (‹a› + ‹o›), à praia (‹a› + ‹a›). In casual speech you will also hear em: vou na praia.

‹fazer›: do, make, and the weather

fazer covers both "do" and "make". It also describes the weather with a noun: faz calor (it's hot), faz frio (it's cold). And it counts time: Faz dois anos que moro aqui means "I've lived here for two years", literally "it makes two years that I live here".

‹querer› and ‹poder›

querer + a noun or a verb is "want". In a shop or a café, Eu queria… ("I wanted") sounds softer than Eu quero…. poder + a verb is "can": Posso…? asks permission, and Pode…? or Você pode…? asks a favor. The second verb stays in the infinitive, with nothing in between.

{{ex:pt.s0120}}{{ex:pt.s0121}}{{ex:pt.s0122}}

Some other verbs change a vowel in the ‹eu› form only, like dormir (to sleep): eu durmo, ele dorme. These are called stem-changing verbs; learn the ‹eu› form with the verb.

Most common mistake: giving these verbs regular endings. Eu fazo → Eu faço; Eu podo → Eu posso.
