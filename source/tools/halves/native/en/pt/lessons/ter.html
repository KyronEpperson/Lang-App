English uses "to have" for owning things, and "to be" for age, hunger and cold. Portuguese uses ter (to have) for more of these, and estar com ("to be with") for how your body feels right now.

The forms of ter

ter is irregular, but it has only four forms. As always, você and a gente take the ele form, and vocês takes the eles form.

Ieu tenho
you, he, she, we (a gente)você, ele, ela, a gente tem

In everyday Brazilian speech tem and têm usually sound the same (some speakers stretch têm a little). The circumflex on têm shows the plural in writing, so don't forget it.

Age: you "have" years

English says "I am twenty-five". Portuguese says "I have twenty-five years": Tenho vinte e cinco anos. Age never uses ser. To ask, say Quantos anos você tem?, literally "how many years do you have?"

{{ex:pt.s0079}}{{ex:pt.s0080}}

tem = there is, there are

In everyday Brazilian speech, tem also means "there is" or "there are". In this meaning it never changes: one thing or many, it stays tem. In writing and formal speech you will see há with the same meaning.

{{ex:pt.s0081}}{{ex:pt.s0082}}

estar com: hungry, thirsty, cold

English says "I am hungry", with an adjective. Portuguese uses a noun: "I am with hunger", Estou com fome. The same pattern works with sede (thirst), frio (cold), calor (heat), sono (sleepiness) and medo (fear). You will also hear ter with these nouns (Tenho fome), which is correct too; estar com is the everyday choice.

{{ex:pt.s0083}}{{ex:pt.s0084}}{{ex:pt.s0085}}

Because these words are nouns, they don't change for a man or a woman: Ela está com frio.

Most common mistake: age with "to be". Eu sou vinte e cinco → Eu tenho vinte e cinco anos. And keep the com: Estou fome → Estou com fome.
