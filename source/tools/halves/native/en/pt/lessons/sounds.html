Many Portuguese letters do not sound the way an English reader expects. Under every Portuguese phrase there is a pronunciation line in English letters; the syllable in CAPITALS is stressed.

Same letters, new sounds

Portuguese uses the alphabet you know: 26 letters, with k, w and y only in borrowed words and names. It adds ç and four marks over vowels: the acute (á), the circumflex (ê), the tilde (ã) and the grave (à). What changes is the sound. This lesson goes one piece at a time: the vowels, ‹t› and ‹d›, the nasal vowels, the letters that differ from English, and the stress. Then you read whole words.

These ten letters sound much as in English (m and n only at the start of a syllable, since at the end they make the vowel nasal, as you will see; and z at the end of a word sounds s, as in dez, ten). Tap a letter to hear it.

Vowels, and the end of a word

The line spells vowels ah, ee, oo, plus two pairs: eh (open, as in bet) and ay (closed, as in bait without the y), aw (open, as in law) and oh (closed, as in go without the w). At the end of a word, an unstressed e sounds ee, o sounds oo, and a is a weak uh. So de, te and que sound jee, chee and kee.

Some vowel pairs glide into one syllable: ai as in eye, ei as in day, oi as in boy. A mark on e or o tells you which of the pairs you hear: é and ó are open, ê and ô closed. Tap a vowel to hear it.

‹t› and ‹d› before an "ee" sound

In most of Brazil, t and d before an ee sound turn into ch and j. That includes a final te or de, because that e sounds ee.

{{ex:pt.s0003}}{{ex:pt.s0004}}{{tiles:pt.tl003}}

Nasal vowels

English has no real nasal vowels. A tilde (ã, õ) or a final m sends the vowel through the nose. The line writes ng after it: hum it through your nose and do not finish with a hard g. Pão (bread) is nasal; pau (stick) is not. An m or n before another consonant also makes the vowel before it nasal, and here the line keeps the m or n: o tempo (time; weather), oo TAYM-poo.

Where the stress goes

A written accent shows the stress: café, você. Without one, words ending in a, e, o (with or without s), am, em or ens stress the next-to-last syllable; all other words stress the last. The marks also show the vowel: avó (grandmother) has an open aw, avô (grandfather) a closed oh.

{{ex:pt.s0010}}{{ex:pt.s0011}}{{ex:pt.s0012}}

Two more marks. â, as in a câmera (the camera), is a stressed ah, a little closed and nasal, since it almost always comes before ‹m› or ‹n›. The grave à shows up almost only in à and às (to the, at the) and does not change the sound. Tap a mark to hear its vowel.

Read whole words

Now put the pieces together. Read a word in two steps. First give each letter or spelling its Portuguese sound: ch is sh, lh is ly, rr is h, and an unstressed final e is ee, which turns a t or d before it into ch or j. Then place the stress: an accent mark shows it, and so does a tilde when the word has no other mark (órfão stresses the ‹ó›); with no mark, the ending decides, as above. So sorvete is sohr-VAY-chee, and mulher is moo-LYEHR. Each card's note breaks the word into its pieces. Say the word aloud before you flip it.

‹o› and ‹a›

Nouns here come with o or a in front. Both mean "the": every noun is masculine (o carro) or feminine (a casa), as lesson 3 explains. For more than one, both add s: os carros, as casas.

The line is a crutch: listen to the audio first, and hide it once the spelling feels natural.

Most common mistake: reading with English letter values. cidade said "see-DAH-day" → cidade said "see-DAH-jee", with the final de sounding jee.
