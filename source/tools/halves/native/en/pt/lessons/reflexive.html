English says "I get up" and "her name is Ana". Portuguese says them with a small extra word that points back at the subject: Eu me levanto (literally "I raise myself") and Ela se chama Ana ("she calls herself Ana"). Verbs used this way are reflexive verbs, and the small word is a reflexive pronoun.

The pronouns

Who‹levantar› (get up)
eume levanto
você, ele, ela, a gentese levanta
nósnos levantamos
vocês, eles, elasse levantam

The pronoun follows the subject, and so does the verb ending: a gente takes se and the ‹ele› form, just like ele.

The same verb asks what things are called, which is handy when you do not know a word: point at it and ask.

Before the verb

In Brazil the pronoun goes before the verb, and in speech it can even start the sentence: Me chamo Lucas. In Portugal: the pronoun comes after the verb, with a hyphen (chamo-me). You will see that in books, but it sounds foreign in Brazilian conversation.

Often dropped in speech

Brazilians often leave the pronoun out of routine verbs: Eu levanto às sete is normal everyday speech, and this course accepts both versions. Acordar (wake up) usually takes no pronoun in Brazil, so you wake up with acordo and then get out of bed with me levanto.

A day in Portuguese

Here is a routine with the verbs you need. Saio is the ‹eu› form of sair (to leave, go out), tomar café (in full, tomar café da manhã) is the everyday way to say "have breakfast", and às (‹a› + ‹as›) goes before clock times (but à uma for one o'clock).
