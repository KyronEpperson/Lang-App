Portuguese numbers are easy to build once you know two habits: a few numbers agree with the noun, and e ("and") joins the parts.

One and two have a feminine form

English "one" and "two" never change. In Portuguese, um and dois become uma and duas before a feminine noun. The hundreds from 200 on do the same: duzentos reais, but duzentas pessoas. The other numbers never change.

Building big numbers

Put e between the parts: vinte e cinco (25), cento e dez (110), trezentos e quarenta e dois (342). A round 100 is cem; from 101 to 199 the number starts with cento e. 1,000 is mil, with no "one" in front. Phone numbers are read one digit at a time, and many Brazilians say meia (half a dozen) instead of seis for 6 there.

{{ex:pt.s0095}}

Telling the time

English says "it is" for every hour. Portuguese counts the hours: one o'clock is singular, É uma hora; from two on, the verb is plural, São duas horas. hora is feminine, so it's uma and duas. Half past is e meia (and a half hour). Noon is meio-dia, midnight meia-noite. For "at", use às (‹a› + ‹as horas›), but à uma (at one), ao meio-dia and à meia-noite.

Prices in ‹reais›

Brazil's money is the real; the plural is reais. A price tag that says R$ 25 is read vinte e cinco reais, never "dollars". Cents are centavos. To ask a price, say Quanto custa? for one thing and Quanto custam? for more than one.
