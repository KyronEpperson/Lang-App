English says "I like coffee": the verb goes straight to the thing. Portuguese always puts de after gostar: Eu gosto de café, literally "I like of coffee". Leaving out de is the classic beginner mistake.

‹gostar de› + noun or verb

gostar is a regular ‹-ar› verb, so it takes the usual endings: eu gosto, você gosta, nós gostamos, eles gostam. After it comes de, then a noun or an infinitive.

{{ex:pt.s0129}}{{ex:pt.s0130}}{{ex:pt.s0131}}

‹de› + the article

For things in general, use de alone: Gosto de música. For a specific thing, or a place that takes an article, de fuses with the article, and the contraction agrees with the noun: do, da, dos, das.

{{ex:pt.s0132}}{{ex:pt.s0133}}{{ex:pt.s0134}}

Who does the liking

Portuguese works like English here: the person who likes is the subject, and the verb ending matches that person: ela gosta, nós gostamos. If you know Spanish, don't copy its order: Eu gosto de…, never Me gosta….

‹adorar›: no ‹de›

adorar (to love, to adore) is the stronger word, and it takes no de.

{{ex:pt.s0135}}

Answering and agreeing

To answer a yes/no question, repeat the verb: Você gosta de praia? Gosto! or Não gosto. To agree, say Eu também (me too); to disagree with someone who likes something, say Eu não (I don't). To agree with a negative, say Nem eu or Eu também não (me neither).

{{ex:pt.s0136}}
Most common mistake: dropping the de, or keeping it apart from the article. Eu gosto café → Eu gosto de café; Gosto de o Brasil → Gosto do Brasil.
