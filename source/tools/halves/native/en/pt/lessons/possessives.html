English "my" never changes. Portuguese meu changes to agree with the thing owned, not with the owner. A man and a woman both say minha casa, because casa is feminine.

Number agrees too: meus livros, minhas amigas. The owner can be one person or many; only the thing owned decides the ending. In Brazil the article before a possessive is optional: minha irmã and a minha irmã are both normal.

his and her: dele, dela

In Brazil, seu and sua usually mean "your". For "his" and "her", Brazilians put dele (de + ele) or dela (de + ela) after the noun, with the article in front. This contraction agrees with the owner: dele for a man, dela for a woman.

{{ex:pt.s0111}}{{ex:pt.s0112}}

For "their", the same pattern gives deles and delas: os pais deles (their parents). In formal writing seu can still mean "his", "her" or "their", which is exactly why everyday speech prefers the clear dele and dela.

this and that

In everyday Brazilian speech, esse is the usual word for "this" (and for "that" near the person you're talking to); este means the same and is more common in writing. aquele is "that one over there". They agree like adjectives: esse livro, essa casa, aqueles sapatos. For a thing you don't name, use isso (or isto) and aquilo, which never change.
