English builds questions with extra words: "Do you speak English?" Portuguese doesn't. A yes/no question is the same sentence as a statement; only your voice rises at the end.

Yes/no questions: just raise your voice

{{ex:pt.s0086}}{{ex:pt.s0048}}

There is no word for "do" or "does", and the words stay in the same order. To answer, Brazilians usually repeat the verb instead of saying just sim: Você fala inglês? Falo!

To check something you think is true, add né? (short for não é?, "isn't it?") at the end, like English "right?".

The question word usually comes first, and in Brazil você normally stays before the verb: Onde você mora? In relaxed speech you will also hear the question word at the end: Você mora onde?

{{ex:pt.s0088}}{{ex:pt.s0089}}

With é, English "what" is often qual in Portuguese, because you are picking one answer out of many possible ones: Qual é o telefone do hotel?, not O que é o telefone?

{{ex:pt.s0090}}

‹quanto› agrees

quanto changes like an adjective, to agree with the noun after it: quanto with a masculine singular noun, quanta feminine singular, quantos masculine plural, quantas feminine plural. On its own, quanto asks a price: Quanto custa? (How much is it?)

{{ex:pt.s0091}}

Why and because

Three spellings that sound almost alike: por que (two words) starts a question, por quê (with a circumflex) ends one or stands alone, and porque (one word) means "because".

{{ex:pt.s0092}}
Most common mistake: forgetting that quanto agrees. Quantos irmãs você tem? → Quantas irmãs você tem? (irmãs is feminine).
