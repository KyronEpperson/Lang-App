English keeps small words apart: "in the car", "from the city". Portuguese fuses some of them. When em (in, on, at) or de (of, from) meets o, a, os, as, the two always join into one word, called a contraction.

The article inside still agrees with the noun: no aeroporto (from o aeroporto), na rodoviária, the bus station (from a rodoviária). Plurals work the same way: nas praias, dos amigos.

These fused forms are not casual speech: em o and de a are wrong in speech and in writing alike. When you are unsure, find the noun's article first (a praia), then pick the matching form (na praia, da praia).

Countries and cities

Most countries take an article, so they contract: o Brasil gives no Brasil and do Brasil; os Estados Unidos gives nos Estados Unidos. A few countries, like Portugal, take none: em Portugal. Most cities take none either: em São Paulo, de Boston. ‹Rio› is the famous exception: no Rio, do Rio. Some Brazilian states take one too: a Bahia, so da Bahia.

Other contractions

  • em + um, uma = num, numa (in a): num hotel. In writing you will also see em um. de + um can fuse into dum, but Brazilians usually write de um.
  • de + ele, ela = dele, dela. Brazilians use them for "his" and "her", after the thing: a mala dele, his suitcase.
  • a + a = à (to the), with a grave accent: à direita, on the right.

Most common mistake: keeping the two words apart. em o Brasil → no Brasil. And keep the gender: no praia → na praia.
