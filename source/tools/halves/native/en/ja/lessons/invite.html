English invites with "Do you want to…?" or "Would you like to…?". In Japanese, asking people straight out what they want can sound pushy. Instead, you ask a negative question, "Won't you…?", which leaves the other person room to say no.

ませんか: won't you…?

Take the negative ません and add the question particle か. With いっしょに ("together") it is a clear, friendly invitation. Do not let the negative fool you: the speaker is not asking whether you won't go. A happy answer is はい、いきましょう.

{{ex:ja.s0099}}{{ex:ja.s0100}}

ましょう: let's

Replace ます with ましょう to say "let's". Use it to accept a plan, or to suggest one when you already know the other person is keen. It is more direct than ませんか.

{{ex:ja.s0101}}

ましょうか: shall we…? shall I…?

Add か to ましょう and you ask "shall we…?" about a plan, or "shall I…?" to offer help. If someone offers help this way, accept with おねがいします ("yes, please"), or decline with だいじょうぶです ("I'm fine, thanks").

{{ex:ja.s0102}}{{ex:ja.s0103}}

Saying yes, and saying no nicely

To accept, say いいですね, "sounds good", often followed by a ましょう. To refuse, Japanese rarely says a flat no. Say すみません, name the problem, and let the sentence trail off with ちょっと… ("a little…"). The listener understands the rest, and nobody has to hear the word no.

{{ex:ja.s0104}}{{ex:ja.s0105}}

Yes and no to a negative question

Not every negative question is an invitation. にくは たべませんか can simply ask "Don't you eat meat?" In English you answer "No, I don't". In Japanese, はい agrees with the question as it was asked: "Yes, that's right: I don't."

{{ex:ja.s0106}}
Most common mistake: turning down an invitation with a bare no. いいえ。 → すみません、ちょっと…。 A bare いいえ sounds cold. Read the kana first, then check the romaji.
