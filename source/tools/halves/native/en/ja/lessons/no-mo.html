English shows ownership with "my", "your" and "'s", and it adds "too" at the end of a sentence. Japanese does both with particles: の and も.

の joins two nouns

The linking particle の goes between two nouns, and the main noun always comes last. わたしの ほん is "my book", word for word "I-の book". The first noun can be an owner, a subject of study or a place: にほんごの せんせい is a teacher of Japanese, and とうきょうの だいがく is a university in Tokyo.

English can flip the order ("the book of my teacher"); Japanese cannot. The owner comes first, then の, then the thing. You can chain several in a row, still reading from the owner outward: たなかさんの ともだちの かばん is Mr. Tanaka's friend's bag.

も means "too"

English puts "too" at the end. Japanese puts the also particle も right after the word it adds. も replaces は, so it is わたしも, never わたしはも. It replaces を in the same way: これも ください. In a question it works just as well: エミリーさんも がくせいですか, "Is Emily a student too?"

My family, your family

English uses "mother" for anyone's mother. Japanese uses a plain word for your own family when you talk to other people, and a polite word for someone else's family. You also use the polite word when you call your own family members, as in "Mom!".

The same goes for お in front of a word: it makes the word polite, so you use it for other people's things (おなまえ) and leave it off your own (わたしの なまえ).

Most common mistake: keeping は next to も. わたしはも がくせいです → わたしも がくせいです. Read the kana first, then tap the romaji to check.
