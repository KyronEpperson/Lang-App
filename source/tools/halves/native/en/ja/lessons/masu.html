English verbs change very little ("eat", "eats") and sit in the middle of the sentence. Japanese verbs sit at the very end, and their endings carry politeness, "not" and tense. This lesson uses the polite ending ます and its negative, ません.

The verb goes last

English says "I eat sushi": subject, verb, object. Japanese says "sushi を eat": the thing with the object particle を, then the verb. Whatever else you add (when, how often) also comes before the verb. Questions work as they do with です: keep the order and add か, as in コーヒーを のみますか, "Do you drink coffee?"

ます and ません

The ます form is the polite form you use with anyone you don't know well. The part before ます is called the stem: たべ in たべます. For "not", replace ます with ません. The same form covers "I eat" and "I will eat", so ます is both present and future.

EnglishYesNot
eatたべますたべません
drinkのみますのみません
goいきますいきません
doしますしません
comeきますきません

Three kinds of verbs

Japanese verbs fall into three groups: ru-verbs (たべます, みます, おきます), u-verbs (のみます, いきます, よみます), and two irregular verbs, します (do) and きます (come). For now, learn each verb as a word in its ます form; the groups matter later, when you change forms.

Time words

Words like まいにち (every day), きょう (today) and あした (tomorrow) take no particle at all. Clock times take the time particle に, as in lesson 6. Time words usually come early in the sentence.

How often

よく (often) and ときどき (sometimes) go with ます. あまり means "not much" and needs ません: あまり のみません is "I don't drink much". English "I drink not much" sounds odd, and so does あまり with ます.
