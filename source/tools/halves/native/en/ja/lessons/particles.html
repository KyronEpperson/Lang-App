English shows how a noun fits into the sentence with small words in front of it: "to Tokyo", "at home", "by train". Japanese puts particles after the noun: とうきょうに, うちで, でんしゃで. This lesson sets the four busiest ones side by side.

を: what you act on

The object particle を marks what you eat, drink, read, watch or buy. English shows the object by its place after the verb; Japanese shows it with を, and the verb still waits at the end.

{{ex:ja.s0074}}

に and へ: where you go

With いきます (go), きます (come) and かえります (go home), the destination particle に marks where you are headed. へ, said e, means "toward" and works just as well here; both are correct.

{{ex:ja.s0075}}{{ex:ja.s0076}}

に for a time

As in lesson 6, a clock time takes the time particle に: さんじに, "at three". Words like あした and まいにち still take nothing.

{{ex:ja.s0077}}

で: where something happens

English uses "at" or "in" both for where you are and for where you do something. Japanese splits them. The place-of-action particle で marks where an action happens: where you eat, study, buy or play. Where someone or something simply is takes に instead; that is the next lesson.

{{ex:ja.s0078}}{{ex:ja.s0079}}

で: by and in

で has a second job: the means particle で marks how you do something, such as how you travel or which language you use.

{{ex:ja.s0080}}{{ex:ja.s0081}}

After the noun, never before

A particle never goes in front like an English preposition: とうきょうに, not に とうきょう. Several phrases can line up before the verb, each with its own particle, and the verb still comes last.

{{ex:ja.s0082}}
Most common mistake: using に for every "at". レストランに たべます → レストランで たべます. Eating is an action, so the place takes で. Read the kana first; tap the romaji to check.
