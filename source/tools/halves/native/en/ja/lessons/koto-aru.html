English uses "have" with forms like "been" and "eaten" for experiences: "I have been to Kyoto", "I've eaten natto". Japanese says, in effect, "there is the experience of having gone": いった ことが あります.

Past short form + ことが あります

Take the past short form you learned for describing nouns (いった, たべた, みた), add こと ("thing, experience") with the subject particle が, and finish with あります, "there is". The polite ending is on あります, at the very end. こと turns the whole phrase into a noun, "the experience of having gone", which is why it can take が like any other noun.

Never: ありません

To say you have never done something, make あります negative: ありません. The verb before こと stays in the past; only the end changes. In negative sentences you will often hear ことは instead of ことが. Both are fine.

{{ex:ja.s0206}}{{ex:ja.s0207}}

Asking: ～た ことが ありますか

Add か to ask "Have you ever…?" The answer does not need to repeat the whole verb: はい、あります or いいえ、ありません.

{{ex:ja.s0208}}

Not the dictionary form

English speakers often reach for the dictionary form. With it, the meaning changes to "there are times when…": いく ことが あります means "I sometimes go", not "I have been".

{{ex:ja.s0209}}

Ever, not at a set time

This pattern is about "ever", at any time up to now, so it does not go with a particular time like "last year" or "on Monday". For something you did at a set time, use the plain polite past.

{{ex:ja.s0210}}

A good trip conversation uses both: first the experience question, then the details in the past. ほっかいどうに いった ことが ありますか. はい、きょねん いきました: "Have you ever been to Hokkaido? Yes, I went last year."

Most common mistake: the dictionary form before こと. For "I've been to Okinawa", おきなわに いく ことが あります (that means "I sometimes go") → おきなわに いった ことが あります. Read the kana first, then check the romaji.
