English puts a number straight in front of a noun: "two apples". Japanese adds a small word after the number, a counter, and the counter depends on what you are counting. English does this now and then too: two sheets of paper, three bottles of water. Japanese does it every time.

The all-purpose set: ひとつ to とお

For things with no special counter, such as apples, rice balls, cups of coffee or anything you point at in a shop, use this set. It stops at ten. To ask how many, say いくつ. In a café or a shop this set is often all you need: point and say これを ふたつ ください.

Where the number goes

English says "two apples, please". Japanese says "apples を two please": the number and its counter come after the particle, right before the verb. This works with any verb, and with あります and います too. No particle follows the number itself.

Sound changes

Some numbers squeeze together with the counter. With ほん you get いっぽん (1), さんぼん (3) and ろっぽん (6); with さつ, いっさつ (1). Learn these as whole words, the way you learned さんびゃく and ろっぴゃく with the numbers.

Counting people

One and two people have their own words, ひとり and ふたり. From three on, add にん: さんにん, よにん (4). In a restaurant, when the staff ask how many you are, answer with the number and です. Note that four people is よにん, not よんにん. The same order as with things: "I have two friends" is ともだちが ふたり います.
