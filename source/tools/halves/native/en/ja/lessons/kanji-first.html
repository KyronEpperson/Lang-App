Real Japanese mixes kana with kanji: characters that came from Chinese and each carry a meaning. English has a few signs that work the same way. "3" and "$" mean the same thing however you say them. Most kanji have several readings, so this course teaches only 30 kanji words, each with one reading, and always lets you answer in kana.

How kanji are built

A kana letter is a sound. A kanji is a meaning, drawn with strokes in a fixed order: top to bottom, and left to right. Simple kanji are pictures: 人 is a person walking, 木 is a tree, 日 is the sun. Bigger kanji are built from smaller ones, and the parts often hint at the meaning: 日 (sun, day) sits on the left of 時 (time, o'clock) and of 曜 in every day of the week, and 本 (origin, book) is 木 with a mark at its root. So you rarely learn a kanji from nothing: look for a part you already know.

Numbers and money

One, two and three are drawn as lines, which makes them an easy start. Prices on signs and menus use 円 (yen).

Days and time

Each day of the week has one kanji of its own, followed by the same ending. The first kanji gives the meaning: moon, fire, water, tree, gold, earth, sun.

People and Japan

人 (ひと) is a person, and 日本 (にほん) is Japan, "the origin of the sun". Together, 日本人 (にほんじん) is a Japanese person. Add 男の人 (おとこのひと, man), 女の人 (おんなのひと, woman) and 女の子 (おんなのこ, girl).

One kanji, several readings

English has the opposite puzzle: "ough" sounds different in though, through and tough. A kanji can be read differently in different words. The sun kanji is に in 日本, び at the end of every day of the week, and にち at the start of 日曜日. 人 is ひと on its own and じん in 日本人. That is why every kanji card starts its note with the reading.

Kanji are never required in your answers: kana are always right.

Most common mistake: reading a kanji the way you met it first. 日本 read as ひほん → にほん. Read the kana reading in the card note first, then the romaji.
