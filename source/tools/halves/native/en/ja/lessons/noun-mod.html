English describes a noun with a clause after it: "the book that I bought yesterday". Japanese puts the whole describing clause before the noun, the way an adjective goes before a noun: きのう かった ほん, "yesterday bought book".

A short form right before the noun

Put the verb in its short form directly in front of the noun. There is no word for "that", "who" or "which", and the noun always comes last. The clause can be as long as you like: everything in front of the noun describes it, so ともだちと きのう みた えいが is "the movie I saw with a friend yesterday".

Past short forms

For "that I bought" or "that I saw", you need the past short form. It is easy if you know the て form: change て to た, and で to だ. So ru-verbs swap る for た, and u-verbs follow their て form. する becomes した and くる becomes きた.

て formPast shortEnglish
たべてたべたate
みてみたsaw
かってかったbought
いっていったwent
のんでのんだdrank
してしたdid

The past negative changes ない to なかった: たべなかった, "didn't eat".

Who did it: が, not は

Inside the clause, the person who does the action takes が, never は. は belongs to the main sentence, outside the clause.

{{ex:ja.s0179}}

Describing people

The same pattern describes people. A clause with ている tells what someone is doing or wearing right now, which is how Japanese points someone out: "the glasses-wearing person".

The polite ending stays at the end

The clause inside is short, but the sentence still ends politely with です or ます. Only the verb right before the noun is short.

Most common mistake: English order, with the clause after the noun. ほん きのう かった → きのう かった ほん. And は inside the clause: ははは つくった ケーキ → ははが つくった ケーキ. Read the kana first, then check the romaji.
