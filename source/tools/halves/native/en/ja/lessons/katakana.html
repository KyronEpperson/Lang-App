English borrows words and keeps their spelling: café, karaoke. Japanese borrows thousands of words too, and writes them in katakana, a second set of 46 letters with the same sounds as hiragana. Katakana also spells foreign names, including yours.

Same sounds, sharper shapes

Every hiragana letter has a katakana twin: か and カ are both ka, す and ス are both su. The grid, the two dots and the circle work the same way. Watch two famous pairs: シ (shi) has its short strokes on the left and its long stroke sweeping up, while ツ (tsu) has them on top and its long stroke falling. ソ (so) and ン (n) differ the same way.

The other six rows: ナ to ン

The rest of the grid. ヲ is almost never used, and ン is n alone. More look-alikes: ヌ (nu) and ス (su), and ワ (wa), ウ (u) and フ (fu).

ー: the long-vowel mark

Hiragana adds a vowel letter for a long vowel. Katakana uses a dash, ー, which makes the vowel before it last two beats. The romaji puts a macron on that vowel. Leaving ー out changes the word: ビール is beer, ビル is a building.

Small letters

Small ッ is a pause, as in hiragana. Small ャ ュ ョ join the letter before them. Katakana also has small ァ ィ ゥ ェ ォ to build sounds Japanese did not have: ティ ti, ファ fa, ウィ wi, ジェ je.

English words in Japanese beats

Japanese beats end in a vowel (or ん), so a borrowed word gets extra vowels and English stress disappears. "McDonald's" becomes マクドナルド, six beats: ma-ku-do-na-ru-do. Say loanwords the Japanese way, or people may not recognize them.

Writing your name

Your name is written by sound, not by English spelling: Emily is エミリー, Mike is マイク, Jason is ジェイソン. In a sentence, katakana sits right next to hiragana, and particles stay in hiragana.

Most common mistake: dropping the long-vowel mark. コーヒ → コーヒー. Each ー is a full beat. Read it aloud and count: ko-o-hi-i is four beats.
