English has two pointing words, "this" and "that". Japanese has three, plus a question word, and they come in sets that start with こ, そ, あ and ど. Teachers call them the ko-so-a-do words.

Near me, near you, over there

こ is near the speaker, そ is near the listener, あ is far from both of you, and ど asks "which?". English "that" covers both そ and あ, so ask yourself whose side the thing is on.

Near meNear youOver thereWhich?
a thing, aloneこれそれあれどれ
before a nounこのそのあのどの
a placeここそこあそこどこ

これ alone, この with a noun

これ, それ, あれ and どれ stand alone, like "this one". この, その, あの and どの always need a noun right after them, like "this bag". なん is "what".

The words switch when the speaker switches. If a clerk holds up a bag and asks about これ, the bag is near the clerk, so in your answer it becomes それ. If you both look at a sign across the street, you both say あれ.

Places: ここ, そこ, あそこ, どこ

The same pattern gives "here", "there (near you)", "over there" and "where". The question word stays where the answer goes; it does not jump to the front as in English.

Whose? だれの

だれ is "who". Add の and it becomes "whose": だれの かさ, "whose umbrella". The thing owned comes after の.

{{ex:ja.s0050}}

In a shop: を ください

To ask for something, name it, add the object particle を, and finish with ください, "please give me". There is no word for "the" or "a" to add, and no "can I have": the thing, を, ください. いくらですか asks the price; the numbers for the answer come in lesson 6.

Most common mistake: putting これ before a noun. これ ほん → この ほん. Remember to read the kana first and tap the romaji only to check.
