English uses "-ing" for an action going on right now: "I'm watching TV." Japanese uses the て form followed by います. The same pattern also describes a state that began earlier and is still true, which English often says in a completely different way.

Doing it right now

The て form and います are written as two words, and only います carries the polite ending.

{{ex:ja.s0153}}{{ex:ja.s0154}}{{ex:ja.s0155}}

For the past, change only the end: みて いました, "I was watching". いま なにを して いますか is also an everyday way to ask what someone is up to, in a call or a message.

States: married, living, knowing

Some verbs describe a change: getting married, moving somewhere, getting to know someone. With て います they describe the state after that change. English says "I'm married", "I live in Tokyo", "I know him", with no "-ing" at all, but Japanese uses the same pattern for all of them.

{{ex:ja.s0156}}{{ex:ja.s0157}}

すんで います takes the location particle に, just like います on its own.

{{ex:ja.s0158}}

Jobs work the same way. Where English says "My father works at a bank" for a lasting fact, Japanese usually says he is "working" there.

{{ex:ja.s0159}}

Watch the ます form on its own

Without て います, these verbs talk about the change itself, usually in the future. So けっこんします means "I'm going to get married", not "I'm married".

{{ex:ja.s0160}}

"I don't know": しりません

The negative of しって います is irregular. Say しりません, not しって いません. If the question is about understanding rather than knowing, such as a word or an answer, わかりません ("I don't understand, I can't tell") is often the better choice.

{{ex:ja.s0161}}
Most common mistake: using the ます form on its own for a state. けっこんします for "I'm married" → けっこんして います. And for "I don't know": しって いません → しりません. Read the kana first, then the romaji.
