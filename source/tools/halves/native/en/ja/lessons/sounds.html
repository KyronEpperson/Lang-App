English cares about syllables and stress. Japanese cares about beats: every beat takes about the same time, like notes in a march. Most kana are one beat each, but small letters and long vowels change the count, and the count can change the meaning.

The dotted letters

Lesson 1 showed the two marks. Here is every letter they make. Two dots turn k into g, s into z, t into d and h into b; the small circle turns h into p. Three do not follow their row: じ is ji, ぢ is ji too, and づ is zu, the same as ず. ぢ and づ are rare.

Small ゃ, ゅ, ょ

Written small after a letter from the i column (き, し, ち, に, ひ, み, り and their dotted forms), ゃ, ゅ and ょ blend into one beat: き + ゃ = きゃ kya. Full size, they are a beat of their own: きや is ki-ya.

{{ex:ja.s0015}}{{ex:ja.s0016}}

Small っ: a short pause

A small っ is a silent beat. Stop for a moment before the next consonant, as in the middle of "bookcase". The romaji doubles that consonant.

{{ex:ja.s0017}}{{ex:ja.s0018}}

Long vowels

A long vowel lasts two beats. In hiragana it is written with an extra vowel letter, and in the romaji with a line over the vowel (a macron): ō means "hold the o".

ん is a beat too

ん counts as a full beat. Before b, m or p it sounds like m: しんぶん. When a vowel or y follows it, the romaji writes n' to show the break: ほんや (bookstore) is hon-ya, not ho-nya.

{{ex:ja.s0022}}{{ex:ja.s0023}}

Count beats, not syllables

Linguists call a beat a mora. English hears "Tokyo" as two syllables, but とうきょう has four beats: と, う, きょ, う. Give each beat its full time, or the word turns into a different one.

Pitch, by ear

English makes one syllable louder. Japanese moves the voice up and down: each beat is high or low. A few words differ only in pitch: はし (chopsticks or bridge), あめ (rain or candy), かみ (paper or god). The romaji does not mark pitch, so listen to the audio and copy the melody.

{{ex:ja.s0024}}{{ex:ja.s0025}}
Most common mistake: cutting a long vowel short. おばさん (aunt) for grandmother → おばあさん. The extra あ is a full beat; hold it.
