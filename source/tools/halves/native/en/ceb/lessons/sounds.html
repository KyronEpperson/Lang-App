Cebuano is written in plain English letters, and most of them sound the way you expect. Two things are never written: which syllable is stressed, and a short stop in the throat called the catch. That is why every Cebuano phrase in this course has a pronunciation line under it. This course has no audio, so keep that line switched on.

The alphabet

The alphabet has 28 letters: the 26 you know, plus ñ and ng, which count as letters of their own. c, f, j, ñ, q, v, x and z turn up only in names and borrowed words, such as Cebu and Santo Niño. Cebuano mostly spells by sound: the letters tell you how to say a word, all but the stress and most catches. The everyday exceptions are mga, said "manga", and names and borrowed words that keep their own spelling, such as Cebu, said seh-BOO. Tap a letter to see its sound. The vowels and ng come in their own sections below.

Reading the line

A syllable in CAPITALS is stressed: balay is bah-LAI. Moving the stress can turn one word into another, so do not stress Cebuano the English way. A word printed all in lowercase, such as dee-lee for dili, has a stress the course has not yet checked in a dictionary: give its syllables equal weight rather than guess.

The catch

The catch is the stop in the middle of "uh-oh". Cebuano has it between two vowels: maayo is three syllables, ma-a-yo, not "my-yo". It comes after a hyphen: bag-o (new). And some words end in it; the line writes it as q. Stop the breath sharply and add no k.

ng and mga

ng is the sound in "singer", never "finger", and Cebuano can start a word with it. Say "singing", then drop the "si". The plural word mga is said "manga".

Other letters

ay sounds like "Thai" and aw like "how"; uy, oy and iw glide the same way (ooy, oy, eew in the line). g is always hard, as in "go"; before ee or eh the line writes gh, so you do not read it as in "gem". ts is said ch and dy is said j. r is a quick tap, like the tt in American "butter". p, t, k have no puff of air: say them as in "spin", not "pin".

My: akong

The deck for this lesson pairs words with akong, "my". Like English "my", it goes before the noun, but it always ends in -ng, which joins it to the noun.

Most common mistake: reading mga letter by letter. m-ga → manga. A close second: ako balay → akong balay.

Read whole words

Now put the pieces together. First cut the word into syllables: every vowel letter is its own syllable (mga, said manga, is the one everyday exception), so tiil is ti-il, with a catch between the two i's. Then give each letter its Cebuano sound: full vowels, a hard g, ng as in "singer", ay as in "Thai". Last, take from the line the two things the spelling never shows: the stressed syllable in CAPITALS, and a final catch, q. Each card's note takes the word apart. Say it aloud before you flip it.
