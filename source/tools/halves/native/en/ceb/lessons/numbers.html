English has one set of numbers. Cebuano uses two every day: its own numbers for counting things, and Spanish ones for money, age and the clock. In Cebu City you will hear English numbers for prices too, but this course uses Spanish for money and time, so that one answer is right.

ka between the number and the thing

English says "two bananas". Cebuano puts ka between the number and the noun, and the noun does not change: there is no plural ending.

{{ex:ceb.s0101}}{{ex:ceb.s0102}}{{ex:ceb.s0103}}

To ask "how many", use pila with the same ka.

{{ex:ceb.s0104}}

Numbers with an owner

There is no verb for "have" here either. "I have two children" is Duha akong anak, literally "two my children": the number comes first, and akong goes before the noun.

The tens are baynte (20), traynta (30), kuwarenta (40), singkuwenta (50), sesenta (60), setenta (70), otsenta (80), nobenta (90) and siyen (100). For 25, say the ten, then the one: baynte singko.

{{ex:ceb.s0107}}{{ex:ceb.s0108}}

Age uses Spanish numbers and anyos (years), then the short pronoun.

{{ex:ceb.s0050}}

Cebuano has bigger numbers of its own, such as kawhaan (20) and usa ka gatos (100), but for money people say baynte and siyen.

Most common mistake: leaving out ka. duha saging → duha ka saging. A Cebuano number always needs ka before the thing it counts.
