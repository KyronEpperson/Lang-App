English says "I want coffee". Cebuano says Gusto ko og kape, literally "want I some coffee". The wanting comes first, the short pronoun ko sits right after it, and the thing comes last.

Want: gusto

Put gusto first and ko in second place. A thing you want, when it is not one particular thing, takes og, just as it does after a mo- verb.

{{ex:ceb.s0162}}{{ex:ceb.s0163}}

Want to do something

English adds "to": "I want to go". Cebuano adds nothing. After gusto ko comes the verb in its future form, with mo-, even if you mean right now.

{{ex:ceb.s0164}}{{ex:ceb.s0165}}

You will also hear kong, which is ko with the linker: Gusto kong mokaon. Both are fine.

Need: kinahanglan

kinahanglan works exactly like gusto: first the word, then ko, then og and the thing, or a mo- verb.

{{ex:ceb.s0166}}

Asking what someone wants

A host or a vendor will often ask Unsay imong gusto?, "What would you like?" Learn it as a whole for now. Answer with Gusto ko og…, or simply name the thing.

{{ex:ceb.s0167}}

Like: ganahan

For "like", use ganahan. It is a describing word, so it works like Gigutom ko: ganahan first, then ko for "I" or ka for "you".

{{ex:ceb.s0168}}{{ex:ceb.s0169}}

Not wanting: dili

dili goes first, and ko moves up right behind it.

{{ex:ceb.s0170}}{{ex:ceb.s0150}}

Don't!: ayaw

dili describes you. To tell someone else not to do something, Cebuano has a separate word, ayaw. On its own, Ayaw! means "Don't!" or "Stop that!"

{{ex:ceb.s0171}}
Most common mistake: copying the English "to". Gusto ko sa moadto → Gusto ko moadto. And keep ko right after gusto: Gusto moadto ko → Gusto ko moadto.
