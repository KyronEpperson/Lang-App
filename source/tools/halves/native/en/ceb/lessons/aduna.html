English needs two ideas here, "there is" and "have". Cebuano covers both with one small word, naa. Its opposite, wala, covers "there isn't" and "don't have".

naa: there is

Put naa first, join -y to it, then name the thing. The -y is a short og, the small word for "some" or "a". So naay means "there is some".

{{ex:ceb.s0080}}{{ex:ceb.s0081}}

naa + ko: I have

Cebuano has no verb for "have". It says "there is, mine, some": Naa koy tubig. The short pronoun ko goes in second place, right after naa, and the -y moves onto it. For "you have", use ka: Naa kay…?

{{ex:ceb.s0082}}{{ex:ceb.s0083}}

In a shop or a sari-sari store, you ask the staff as a group: Naa ba moy…? Here mo is short for kamo (you all).

{{ex:ceb.s0084}}

naa: is here, is there

Without the -y, naa says where someone or something is. Names still take si, and other nouns take ang.

{{ex:ceb.s0085}}{{ex:ceb.s0086}}

wala: there isn't, don't have

wala works the same way: walay for "there is no", Wala koy… for "I don't have", and Wala si… for "is not here".

{{ex:ceb.s0087}}{{ex:ceb.s0088}}{{ex:ceb.s0089}}

aduna: the careful word

aduna means the same as naa for "there is" and "have". You will meet it in writing and in careful speech: Adunay tubig, Aduna koy…. For where someone is, keep naa. In everyday talk, naa is the word you will use most.

{{ex:ceb.s0090}}
Most common mistake: using dili (no, not) for "there isn't". Dili tubig. → Walay tubig. For "there is no" and "don't have", the word is always wala.
