English uses "can" and "could" as separate little words. Cebuano builds them into the verb with a prefix: maka- for "can" and naka- for "managed to" or "have ever".

Can: maka-

Join maka- to the root. It works like mo-: the doer is the focus, so it takes the ako-set, and the short pronoun goes in second place.

{{ex:ceb.s0228}}{{ex:ceb.s0229}}{{ex:ceb.s0230}}

Can't: dili + maka-

Put dili first. As always, the short pronoun moves up right behind it. This is the polite way to turn down an invitation.

{{ex:ceb.s0231}}

Managed to, have ever: naka-

naka- is the past form of maka-. It means you did manage to do something. With na (already) in a question, it asks whether something has ever happened, or has happened yet.

{{ex:ceb.s0232}}{{ex:ceb.s0233}}

That last one is a very common friendly greeting, especially around mealtimes. It is a kind question, not an invitation you must accept.

Couldn't: wala + maka-

For "couldn't" or "didn't get to", use wala. Just as with mo-, the verb after wala keeps the future form: maka-, not naka-.

{{ex:ceb.s0234}}

Know how to: kahibalo

English "can" also means a skill: "I can cook". Cebuano usually says that with kahibalo, "know how", followed by the verb. Keep maka- for whether something is possible right now.

{{ex:ceb.s0235}}
Most common mistake: reading naka- as only "could". Nakaadto na ka sa Bohol? is not "Could you go to Bohol?" → it is "Have you ever been to Bohol?" And keep ko up front: Dili makaadto ko → Dili ko makaadto.
