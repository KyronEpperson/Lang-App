Some verbs take mag- instead of mo-. They work the same way: the prefix shows whether the action has happened, and the person doing it is the ang word. Which prefix a root takes is something you learn with each verb, the way English learners learn "went" with "go". Common mag- verbs: magtrabaho (work), magluto (cook), magtuon (study), magdula (play).

mag-: not done yet

mag- is the future form, like mo-.

nag-: done, or going on

Here is the surprise for English speakers: nag- covers both something already done and something going on now. Nagluto siya is "she cooked" or "she is cooking", and the situation tells you which. It also covers ongoing situations, such as a job or where you live: you met Nagpuyo ko sa Cebu (I live in Cebu) as a phrase.

Let's: mag- with ta

With ta (we, including you), the mag- form makes a suggestion, like English "let's".

{{ex:ceb.s0142}}

A hyphen before a vowel

When the root starts with a vowel, a hyphen separates the prefix from it and marks the catch: mag-ampo (pray), nag-ampo (prayed, is praying).

{{ex:ceb.s0143}}

Same rules as mo-

Everything from the last lesson still holds, and the prefix never changes for the person: magluto ko, magluto siya, magluto si Ana. The verb comes first and a short pronoun follows it; a question word goes before both; names take si; a thing that is not a specific one takes og.

{{ex:ceb.s0144}}
Most common mistake: using nag- for something still to come. Nagtrabaho ko ugma. → Magtrabaho ko ugma. nag- is for what is done or going on; tomorrow needs mag-.
