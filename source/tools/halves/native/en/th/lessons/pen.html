English uses one verb, "to be", for very different jobs: "I am a teacher", "This is Anna", "The bathroom is over there", "The food is good". Thai splits these jobs, and for one of them it uses no verb at all.

เป็น: jobs, roles and nationality

Use เป็น bpen to say what someone is: a job, a role, a nationality. There is no word for "a".

{{ex:th.s0112}}{{ex:th.s0113}}{{ex:th.s0114}}{{ex:th.s0115}}

คือ: pointing out and defining

คือ khuue links two things that are the same: "this is…", "the answer is…". You hear it most when introducing someone with นี่ nîi (this).

{{ex:th.s0116}}

อยู่: where something is

For place, Thai uses อยู่ yùu, "to be at, to stay, to live". เป็น never means "is at".

{{ex:th.s0117}}{{ex:th.s0118}}{{ex:th.s0119}}{{ex:th.s0120}}{{ex:th.s0121}}

Listen for ใกล้ and ไกล here: near has a falling tone, far a mid tone. In a sentence about place the two sound very close, so say the tone clearly and, if a driver looks unsure, point.

No verb before describing words

Thai describing words, such as hungry, delicious or far, work like verbs on their own. "The food is delicious" is just "food delicious". The same goes for feelings: tired, happy and full need no verb either. Adding เป็น here is the classic English-speaker mistake, and Thais will understand you but hear it as odd.

{{ex:th.s0122}}{{ex:th.s0123}}

Not a teacher: ไม่ใช่

To say someone is not something, use ไม่ใช่ mâi châi before the noun, not ไม่เป็น. ไม่ได้เป็น is also correct.

{{ex:th.s0124}}{{ex:th.s0125}}

ใช่ châi on its own means "that's right"; it answers a question about what something is.

{{ex:th.s0126}}{{ex:th.s0127}}
Most common mistake: เป็น before a describing word or a place. ผมเป็นหิว → ผมหิว, I'm hungry; ผมเป็นที่บ้าน → ผมอยู่ที่บ้าน, I'm at home.
