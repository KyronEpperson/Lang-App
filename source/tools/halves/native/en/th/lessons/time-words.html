English changes the verb for time: go, went, gone, going. Thai verbs never change; time words do the work. ไป bpai is "go", "went" and "going", and the words around it say when.

Past: just say when

A time word such as เมื่อวาน mûuea-waan (yesterday) is enough. The verb stays as it is. If the time is already clear from the conversation, Thai often says nothing more at all, and the listener understands.

{{ex:th.s0234}}

Later: จะ before the verb

จะ jà goes right before the verb for something that will happen. With a time word like พรุ่งนี้ (tomorrow) it is often left out.

{{ex:th.s0235}}

Now: กำลัง … อยู่

For something happening right now, put กำลัง gam-lang before the verb, อยู่ yùu after it, or both. All three are natural.

{{ex:th.s0236}}

Done: แล้ว at the end

แล้ว láaeo at the end means "already, done". Use it when the point is that something is finished, not on every past verb: "Yesterday I went to the market" needs no แล้ว. It is also the everyday "already" in short answers: อิ่มแล้ว, I'm full now.

{{ex:th.s0237}}

Ever: เคย

เคย khooei before the verb means "have ever". Answer with เคย (yes, I have) or ไม่เคย (never).

{{ex:th.s0238}}{{ex:th.s0239}}

Yet: ยัง and หรือยัง

หรือยัง rǔue-yang at the end asks "… yet?". Answer with the verb and แล้ว for yes, or with ยัง yang (not yet). ยังไม่ before a verb means "not … yet". Thais often greet friends with กินข้าวหรือยัง; it is a friendly hello, not an invitation.

{{ex:th.s0240}}{{ex:th.s0241}}
Most common mistake: answering a yes/no question with ใช่. กินข้าวหรือยัง, answered ใช่ → กินแล้ว or ยัง. ใช่ only confirms "is it …?".
