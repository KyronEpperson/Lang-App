English needs a pronoun in every sentence: "I'm going." Thai has more pronouns to choose from, and then leaves them out most of the time.

I and you

"I" depends on who is speaking. A man says ผม phǒm. A woman says ฉัน chǎn in everyday speech and ดิฉัน dì-chǎn in formal situations. The polite "you" for adults is คุณ khun.

{{ex:th.s0088}}{{ex:th.s0089}}

เขา khǎo means he or she, and เรา rao means we. พวก phûuak in front makes a group: พวกเขา phûuak-khǎo, they.

{{ex:th.s0090}}{{ex:th.s0091}}

Family words as you and I

Thais often use family words instead of pronouns. พี่ phîi (older sibling) means "you" to someone a little older, and น้อง náawng (younger sibling) "you" to someone younger, including a young server. You can also call yourself พี่ or น้อง. When you call out to someone, a woman uses คะ.

{{ex:th.s0092}}{{ex:th.s0093}}

Leave it out

Once it is clear who you mean, drop the pronoun. Saying ผม and คุณ in every sentence sounds stiff and foreign.

{{ex:th.s0094}}{{ex:th.s0095}}

The polite particle still goes at the end: ครับ for men, ค่ะ and คะ for women. นะ ná before it makes a sentence softer and friendlier.

{{ex:th.s0096}}

The owner comes after the noun

English puts the owner first: "my friend". Thai puts the noun first and the owner after the noun: "friend I". The pronoun itself does not change.

{{ex:th.s0097}}{{ex:th.s0098}}{{ex:th.s0099}}{{ex:th.s0100}}

You may also see ของ khǎawng, "of", between them: พี่สาวของฉัน. Both are correct; a later lesson covers ของ.

Most common mistake: English order for owners. ผมเพื่อน → เพื่อนผม, my friend. And a man using ฉัน or ค่ะ → ผม and ครับ.
