You can already work out the tone of a syllable with no tone mark: its consonant class and whether it is live or dead decide it. Thai adds four tone marks on top. A mark sits above the consonant that starts the syllable, and what it does depends on that consonant's class. This is the one big surprise for English readers: the same mark gives different tones on different letters.

Mid-class letters: four marks, four tones

On a mid-class letter each mark gives its own tone: ่ low, ้ falling, ๊ high, ๋ rising. ป่า bpàa (forest) and ป้า bpâa (aunt) differ only in the mark.

High-class letters: low and falling

High-class letters take only the first two marks, with the same tones as on a mid-class letter: ่ low, ้ falling. Compare ขา khǎa (leg) with no mark.

{{tiles:th.tl008}}{{ex:th.s0012}}{{ex:th.s0144}}

Low-class letters: one step higher

Here is the trap. On a low-class letter, ่ gives a falling tone and ้ gives a high tone. So ไม่ mâi (not) is falling, not low, and ม้า máa (horse) is high.

The same marks on low-class ค: คา khaa, ค่า khâa (price, fee), ค้า kháa (to trade). ค่า sounds just like ข้า above.

Reading a new word

Find the first consonant and its class, see whether the syllable has a mark, then read the tone from the chart. Try โต๊ะใหม่ dtó mài (a new table): ๊ on mid-class ต is high; ่ on ใหม่ gives a low tone, because the silent ห in front makes ม high-class (more on that in the lesson on clusters).

Most common mistake: reading ่ as "low tone" everywhere. ไม่ read like ใหม่ (mài) → ไม่ (mâi), falling, because ม is a low-class letter.
