The low-class letters

Lesson 5 gave you the mid-class and high-class letters. The low class is the biggest: 24 letters, 14 of them common. Some sound like a high-class letter (ค like ข, ซ like ส); the others are m, n, ng, y, r, l and w, which only the low class has. ฆ ฌ ฑ ฒ ฬ are rare and turn up in formal words and names. ญ and ณ are less common, but you meet them every day in หญิง (woman) and คุณ (you). ฅ is no longer used. Tap a letter to hear its name.

Only eight final sounds

A Thai syllable can end in a consonant, but many letters share very few sounds at the end. Knowing how a syllable ends also tells you its tone.

English words can end in almost any sound. Thai has only eight final sounds: k, t, p, m, n, ng, w, y. Many letters are read as one of them.

Two warnings. Final k, t and p are held, not released: stop the sound with no burst of air. And final ร and ล are said n: อาหาร aa-hǎan ends in n, not r.

Live and dead syllables

A live syllable ends in a long vowel or in m, n, ng, w or y: you could hold it like a note. A dead syllable ends in a short vowel or in k, t or p: it stops short.

The tone rules with no tone mark

With no tone mark, three things give the tone: the class of the first consonant, live or dead, and, for low-class letters, vowel length.

First letterLiveDead
mid classmid: ตาlow: จาก
high classrising: ขาlow: สิบ
low class, short vowelmid: คนhigh: รัก
low class, long vowelmid: มาfalling: ราก

So รัก rák (love) and ราก râak (root) differ in vowel length and therefore in tone.

Putting it together

Now you can read a whole syllable with no tone mark: the first consonant and its class, the vowel and its length, the final sound, then the tone from the table. Some vowels change their look before a final: ั above the consonant is a short a (วัด wát), เ-ะ becomes เ-็ (เล็ก lék), and เ-อ becomes เ-ิ (เปิด bpòoet; in เงิน ngoen it is said short).
