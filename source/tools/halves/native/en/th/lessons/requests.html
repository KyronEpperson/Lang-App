English makes requests polite with "please", "could you" and "may I". Thai has no single everyday word for "please". Instead it uses a few small words around the verb, and they matter: a bare verb sounds like an order.

May I have: ขอ + thing

ขอ khǎaw asks for something: "may I have". Add หน่อย nàwi (a little) at the end to make it softer, and finish with ครับ or ค่ะ.

{{ex:th.s0250}}{{ex:th.s0251}}

ขอ + a verb asks permission to do something yourself.

{{ex:th.s0252}}

Could you: ช่วย + verb + หน่อย

To ask someone to do something, start with ช่วย chûuai (help) and end with หน่อย. Add ได้ไหม at the very end to make it a question, even more polite: "could you …?".

{{ex:th.s0253}}{{ex:th.s0254}}

Softeners: นะ and ด้วย

นะ ná at the end makes a request friendly, like "OK?". ด้วย dûuai after a request is a polite "please" in fixed phrases, as when you ask for the bill.

{{ex:th.s0255}}{{ex:th.s0256}}

Don't, and go ahead

อย่า yàa before a verb means "don't". It is not the same as ไม่, which only says "not". เชิญ chooen invites: "go ahead, after you, please sit".

What about กรุณา?

Dictionaries give กรุณา gà-rú-naa for "please". It is formal: you see it on signs and hear it in announcements, but people rarely say it to each other. In a shop or a taxi, ขอ and ช่วย are what you need.

{{ex:th.s0259}}
Most common mistake: giving a bare order. เปิดแอร์ for "Could you turn on the air conditioning?" → ช่วยเปิดแอร์หน่อย, with ครับ or ค่ะ. ช่วย and หน่อย turn an order into a request.
