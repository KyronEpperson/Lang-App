Thai numbers are very regular: learn ten words and a few rules, and you can say any price. Watch the tones, because three of the numbers have a twin word.

Zero to ten

012345
ศูนย์หนึ่งสองสามสี่ห้า
sǔunnùengsǎawngsǎamsìihâa
678910
หกเจ็ดแปดเก้าสิบ
hòkjètbpàaetgâaosìp

Tone twins to watch: ห้า hâa (five) has a falling tone, but หา hǎa with a rising tone means "to look for". เก้า gâao (nine) is falling and long, but เก่า gào means "old". สี่ sìi (four) is low; สี sǐi with a rising tone is "color".

Eleven to ninety-nine

Tens are a number plus สิบ: สามสิบ is thirty. Two exceptions. Twenty is ยี่สิบ yîi-sìp, never สองสิบ. And a 1 at the end of any bigger number is เอ็ด èt, not หนึ่ง (you may hear ยี่สิบหนึ่ง in casual speech, but ยี่สิบเอ็ด is the standard form).

Hundreds and up

ร้อย ráawi is a hundred and พัน phan a thousand. English stops there and counts thousands, but Thai has its own words for ten thousand, หมื่น mùuen, and a hundred thousand, แสน sǎaen. A million is ล้าน láan. Say the parts from big to small, as in English.

Prices

Prices are in baht, บาท bàat, which goes after the number. Price tags and menus use the same digits as English, but you read them in Thai.

{{ex:th.s0040}}{{ex:th.s0041}}

To ask the price, say เท่าไร thâo-rai (how much). In speech it is often เท่าไหร่ thâo-rài; both are right. It goes at the end of the sentence, with your polite particle after it.

Most common mistake: building 20 and 21 the English way. สองสิบ → ยี่สิบ, and ยี่สิบหนึ่ง → ยี่สิบเอ็ด.
