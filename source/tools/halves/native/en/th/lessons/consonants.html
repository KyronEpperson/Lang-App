How Thai writing works

Thai script looks dense at first, but it is built simply. Each syllable is built on a consonant. The vowel is written around that consonant: before it, after it, above it or below it (the next lesson). Words run together with no spaces; a space marks the end of a phrase, where English would put a comma or a period.

Thai runs left to right and has no capital letters. A syllable has up to four pieces: a consonant, a vowel written around it, sometimes a final consonant, and sometimes a tone mark on top. You read them in that order and get one syllable with one tone. This lesson gives you the consonants of two of the three classes; the vowels come in lesson 6, the low-class letters and the final consonants in lesson 8, and the tone marks in lesson 11.

Letters have names

The Thai alphabet has 44 consonants, but many share a sound, and about 30 cover almost everything you will read. Each letter has a name: its sound plus aaw, then a word that starts with it, the way English children say "A for apple".

The three classes

Every consonant belongs to one of three classes: mid, high or low. The class is not about the sound; it is part of how Thai spells tones, and you need it to read a word's tone.

อ is special: at the start of a syllable it is silent and only holds a vowel, as in อาหาร.

The mid-class letters

Nine letters are mid class. Seven are common. ฎ and ฏ are rare twins of ด and ต, found in a few formal words such as กฎ gòt (rule). None of the mid-class letters has a puff of air. Tap a letter to hear its name.

The high-class letters

Eleven letters are high class, and every one is a breathy sound: kh, ch, th, ph, f, s or h. ฐ is a rare spelling of th. ศ and ษ are less common spellings of s, mostly in words from Pali and Sanskrit, but you will see them often, as in ประเทศ (country) and ภาษา (language). ฃ is no longer used; it stays in the alphabet only. Tap a letter to hear its name.

Your first tone rule: a syllable that ends in a long vowel and has no tone mark takes a mid tone after a mid-class or low-class letter, but a rising tone after a high-class letter.

Rare letters

The rare letters appear in a few formal or borrowed words. You have met the mid-class ฎ ฏ and the high-class ฐ ศ ษ on the tiles above. The less common low-class letters, ฆ ฌ ญ ฑ ฒ ณ ฬ, come with the rest of the low class in lesson 8. ฃ and ฅ are no longer used: they keep their place in the alphabet, but no modern word is spelled with them.

Most common mistake: treating letters with the same sound as interchangeable. ซวย for "pretty" → สวย: the high-class ส gives the rising tone, and ซวย, with a mid tone, means unlucky.
