English puts "this", "that" and "my" in front of the noun: this shirt, my house. Thai puts all of them after it. Two pairs of words look almost the same, and only the tone tells them apart.

Standing alone: นี่, นั่น, โน่น

When "this" or "that" is the whole subject ("this is…", "that is…"), use the words with ่ and a falling tone: นี่ nîi (this), นั่น nân (that), โน่น nôon (that, over there).

{{ex:th.s0148}}{{ex:th.s0149}}

After a noun: noun + classifier + นี้, นั้น, โน้น

To say "this shirt" or "that book", start with the noun, add its classifier, and end with the word with ้ and a high tone: นี้ níi, นั้น nán, โน้น nóon. With no noun at all, the classifier carries it: อันนี้, this one.

{{ex:th.s0150}}{{ex:th.s0151}}{{ex:th.s0152}}

For people the classifier is คน khon, the same word as "person", so "that person" is just คนนั้น.

{{ex:th.s0153}}

A few fixed words have นี้ built in with no classifier, such as วันนี้ wan-níi, "today" (literally "day this"). Learn those as single words; for things you can count, keep the classifier.

A memory aid for the pair: the word that stands alone does the pointing, and its falling tone drops like a pointing finger. The word after a noun just closes the phrase, so it stays high at the end. Same consonant, same vowel: only the tone mark and the tone differ, so look at the mark every time you read one.

Whose: ของ

English says "my house". Thai says "house of me": บ้านของผม. In everyday speech ของ khǎawng is often dropped, so บ้านผม means the same. Either way, the owner comes after the thing.

{{ex:th.s0154}}{{ex:th.s0155}}{{ex:th.s0156}}

To ask "whose?", use ของใคร khǎawng khrai, "of whom". To answer "it's mine", say ของ + the owner.

{{ex:th.s0157}}{{ex:th.s0158}}
Most common mistake: English order. นี้เสื้อ → เสื้อตัวนี้, and ผมของบ้าน → บ้านของผม. The thing comes first; this, that and the owner come after it.
