English says "I am a student". Cantonese uses the same order: 我係學生 ngo5 hai6 hok6 saang1. The verb 係 joins two nouns: who someone is, and what they are.

No "a", and no changes

There is no word for "a" here, and 係 never changes: I am, you are, she is and they are are all just 係, and it does not change for time either: the same 係 covers "was" and "were". Nouns do not change either: English adds -s for "students", but 學生 is one student or many.

I, you, he, she, they

OneJyutpingMore than oneJyutping
我ngo5 (I, me)我哋ngo5 dei6 (we, us)
你nei5 (you)你哋nei5 dei6 (you all)
佢keoi5 (he, she, it)佢哋keoi5 dei6 (they, them)

哋 makes a pronoun plural. 佢 covers he, she and it, so only the context tells you which. There is only one "you": 你 works for your boss and your grandma alike, and politeness comes from other words. Newspapers write 他 and 她 for he and she; in speech it is always 佢.

Not: 唔係

Put 唔 right before 係: 唔係 m4 hai6 means "am not, is not, are not". 唔 is the everyday "not", and lesson 7 does much more with it.

{{ex:yue.s0051}}

My friend, and "too"

The owner goes before the noun, and for people close to you nothing goes in between: 我朋友 ngo5 pang4 jau5 is "my friend". For "too" or "also", put 都 dou1 right after the subject, before 係. English puts "too" at the end; Cantonese never does.

{{ex:yue.s0052}}{{ex:yue.s0053}}

No 係 with adjectives

English uses "is" with adjectives too: "she is tired". Cantonese does not. Use 好 (literally "very") instead: 佢好攰 keoi5 hou2 gui6. Lesson 22 comes back to this.

{{ex:yue.s0054}}
Most common mistake: putting 係 before an adjective, as English does with "is". 佢係攰 → 佢好攰.
