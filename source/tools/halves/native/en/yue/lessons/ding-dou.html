At a Hong Kong café the server will ask you to choose: iced or hot? Here to eat or to take out? This lesson gives you the two words for "or", the word for "and" between things, and the word for "also".

Two words for "or"

English has one "or". Cantonese has two. In a question that offers a choice, use 定 ding6: A 定 B？ The particle 呀 at the end keeps it friendly.

{{ex:yue.s0171}}{{ex:yue.s0172}}

To answer, just say the one you want, with 唔該 if you are ordering: 凍，唔該。 dung3, m4 goi1. No yes or no: you simply name your choice. You met another 定 question in the food lesson: 堂食定外賣呀？ (here or to go?).

It also makes "or not" questions, a little stronger than the plain verb-not-verb question.

{{ex:yue.s0173}}

In a statement, "or" is 或者 waak6 ze2: either one is possible. At the start of a sentence, 或者 also means "maybe".

{{ex:yue.s0174}}

"And" between things: 同

To join two nouns, use 同 tung4. 同埋 tung4 maai4 means the same and is common in longer lists. Keep the classifiers: each thing you count still takes its own. 同 joins things, not whole sentences, so do not use it for "I eat and I drink".

{{ex:yue.s0175}}

"Also" and "too": 都

English puts "too" at the end: "I'm American too." Cantonese puts 都 dou1 after the person and before the verb, never at the end.

{{ex:yue.s0176}}{{ex:yue.s0177}}

Because 都 goes before the verb, it also comes before 係 and before 好 with an adjective: 我都係美國人 (I'm American too), 佢都好高 (she's tall too).

With a plural subject, 都 often means "all" or "both": everyone in the group does it.

{{ex:yue.s0178}}

Not either

For "not … either", 都 goes first and 唔 after it, right before the verb.

{{ex:yue.s0070}}
Most common mistake: putting 都 at the end, where English puts "too". 我去都 → 我都去. And in a question, use 定, not 或者: 凍或者熱呀？ → 凍定熱呀？
