English needs two expressions: "I have a phone" and "there is a bank nearby". Cantonese uses one verb for both: 有 jau5. And its negative is its own word, 冇 mou5.

有: have

有 works like any verb: who, then 有, then the thing. Counted things keep their number and classifier.

{{ex:yue.s0117}}{{ex:yue.s0107}}

冇: don't have, never 唔有

You learned that 唔 makes a verb negative: 唔食, 唔去. 有 is the one verb where that does not work. "Not have" is a single word, 冇. Saying 唔有 is a typical learner slip, and it sounds plainly wrong.

{{ex:yue.s0071}}{{ex:yue.s0118}}

Notice that "any" disappears: 冇 + noun already means "no money", "no brothers". It also works for "there isn't": 附近冇銀行 means there is no bank nearby. Like every Cantonese verb, 有 never changes: 佢有 is "she has", with no extra ending.

"There is": the place first

English says "there is a bank nearby", with the place at the end. Cantonese starts with the place and then uses 有: literally "nearby has a bank". The classifier can stand without 一 here. You will also hear 有間銀行喺附近, but starting with the place is the usual way, so learn that one.

{{ex:yue.s0119}}{{ex:yue.s0120}}

Asking: 有冇…？

The verb-not-verb question for 有 is 有冇 jau5 mou5, "have or not have". Add 呀 at the end to keep it friendly and soft.

{{ex:yue.s0121}}{{ex:yue.s0122}}

Answering: 有 or 冇

As with other verb-not-verb questions, answer with the verb itself, not with 係: 有 means "yes, I do" or "yes, there is"; 冇 means "no". Answering 係 or 唔係 to a 有冇 question is a very English habit. To say more, repeat the verb in a full sentence: 有，我有兩個 (yes, I have two).

Most common mistake: making 有 negative with 唔, like other verbs. 我唔有錢 → 我冇錢.
