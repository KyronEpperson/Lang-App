To ask a price, Cantonese asks "how much money?": 幾多錢呀？ gei2 do1 cin2 aa3?. Notice the tone on 錢: in this question it is cin2, a changed tone, rising at the end.

Asking the price

Say the thing first, with 呢 + classifier if you are pointing at it, then 幾多錢.

You can also simply point at something and ask 幾多錢呀？; the seller will know what you mean.

Dollars: 蚊, not 元

Hong Kong money is 港幣 gong2 bai6 (Hong Kong dollars). Price tags write 元 or $, but nobody says 元 at the counter. Out loud, a dollar is 蚊 man1: number + 蚊.

{{ex:yue.s0131}}{{ex:yue.s0132}}

Two dollars, twenty dollars

蚊 behaves like a classifier, so two dollars is 兩蚊 loeng5 man1, never 二蚊. Inside a bigger number, 二 stays: 二十蚊, 十二蚊. In fast speech you will often hear 廿 for twenty: 廿五蚊 is the same as 二十五蚊.

{{ex:yue.s0133}}{{ex:yue.s0134}}

Cents: 毫

A 毫 hou4 (also 毫子) is ten cents. So 五毫 is fifty cents and 兩毫 is twenty cents, again with 兩. Prices with cents are rare in everyday shopping, so 蚊 is the word you will use most.

Too expensive, a bit cheaper

平 means cheap and 貴 means expensive. 太 means "too": 太貴 is too expensive. Adding 啲 means "a bit more": 平啲 is "a bit cheaper". Ask with 得唔得 (OK or not OK?). This is fine at street markets, but not in malls or chain stores, where prices are fixed.

Paying

To pay is 畀錢 bei2 cin2, literally "give money". Most people tap an 八達通 baat3 daat6 tung1, the Octopus card that works on buses, trains, ferries and in most shops.

Most common mistake: reading the price tag aloud as written, or using 二 for two dollars. 三十五元 at a market stall → 三十五蚊, and 二蚊 → 兩蚊.
