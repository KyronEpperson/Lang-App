Cantonese is written in characters, not with an alphabet. This course does not ask you to write them by hand: the goal is to recognize about 250 by the end, enough to read menus, signs and family messages.

Pictures

Some characters began as drawings. 人 is a person walking, 口 an open mouth, 日 the sun, 月 the moon, 山 three peaks, 水 flowing water and 木 a tree. 大 daai6 (big) is a person with arms spread wide, and 小 siu2 (small) is three little strokes. 門 mun4 is a door with two swinging halves, and 手 sau2 a hand with its fingers.

Parts that combine

Many characters are built from two parts. Two trees make 林 lam4 (woods). A person leaning on a tree makes 休 jau1 (to rest). The sun and the moon make 明 ming4 (bright). A woman, 女, next to a child, 子, makes 好 hou2 (good).

Strokes and radicals

Each character is written with strokes, the lines, dots and hooks of the pen. A part that turns up in many characters is a radical, and it often hints at the meaning: 亻 is a squeezed 人 (person), as in 休 and 你; 氵 is water; 女 is woman.

Many radicals are picture characters squeezed to one side. Here are the ones you will meet most, in the traditional forms Hong Kong uses. A tile shows the Jyutping of the full character the radical comes from: 扌 is 手 sau2 (hand), 訁 is 言 jin4 (speech), and 忄 is 心 sam1 (heart) standing on the left.

The 口 of spoken Cantonese

Cantonese has everyday words that Mandarin does not, and many of their characters carry 口 (mouth) on the left, as if to say "this is a spoken word": 嘅, 咗, 啲, 嘢, 呢, 嚟, 喺. When you see 口 on the left, think "Cantonese word".

Reading a new character from its parts

Most characters are a radical plus one more part. The radical gives the meaning, and the other part often gives the sound: 媽 maa1 (mom) is 女 (woman) with 馬 maa5 (horse) for the sound. Door signs are a good first test: 推 teoi1 (push) and 拉 laai1 (pull) both carry 扌, the hand. The cards below are common characters; each note names the parts and what each one gives.

Signs, and simplified characters

Hong Kong uses traditional characters; mainland China uses simplified ones, with fewer strokes. Signs, menus and the news are often in standard written Chinese, a formal style that differs from speech: 出口 ceot1 hau2 on a door means "exit". This course teaches what people say, so "he is" is 佢係 keoi5 hai6, even though a newspaper writes 他是.

{{ex:yue.s0046}}
Most common mistake: copying the written style into speech. 他是美國人 → 佢係美國人 keoi5 hai6 mei5 gwok3 jan4, "He is American", the way people say it.
