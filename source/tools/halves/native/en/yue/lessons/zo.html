English changes the verb for the past: eat, ate, have eaten. Cantonese verbs never change. Instead, a small word after the verb shows that the action is done. This lesson also covers the two ways to say "not" about the past: didn't, and not yet.

咗: it's done

Put 咗 zo2 right after the verb (it is an "aspect marker", if you like grammar words). It covers both "I ate" and "I've eaten". It sits between the verb and the thing: 買咗兩本書, never 買兩本書咗. Time and place still go before the verb.

咗 for a change

咗 also says that something has changed and is now true, which is why you hear it so often at the doctor's. Many health phrases work this way: 發燒咗 (got a fever), 好返咗 (got better).

Didn't: 冇 + verb

For something that did not happen, use 冇 mou5 before the verb, the same 冇 as in "not have". Drop the 咗: the two never go together. Compare 唔, which is for habits and plans: 我唔去 means "I'm not going" or "I don't go", while 我冇去 means "I didn't go".

{{ex:yue.s0194}}

Not yet: 未

English "not yet" is 未 mei6 before the verb. To ask "have you … yet?", put 咗 after the verb and 未 at the end, then 呀. You already know one: the greeting 食咗飯未呀？

{{ex:yue.s0195}}{{ex:yue.s0196}}

Answer with the verb and 咗 for yes (食咗), or with 未 for not yet. 未 suggests it may still happen. When the chance is over, use 冇 instead: 我琴日冇食早餐 (I didn't have breakfast yesterday).

No 咗 for habits and states

咗 is not a past tense. A past habit or a past state takes no 咗; the time word alone places it in the past. Adjectives describe states, so they take no 咗 for the past either: "I was tired yesterday" is simply 琴日 + 好攰. (攰咗 is a change: "have gotten tired".)

Most common mistake: mixing 唔 and 咗 for "didn't". For "I didn't go": 我唔去咗 → 我冇去.
