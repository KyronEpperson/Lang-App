English uses pitch for feelings: your voice goes up at the end of a question. In Cantonese, pitch is part of the word itself. Say 買 maai5 and you buy; say 賣 maai6, with another tone, and you sell.

These six words are tone twins: the same letters with a different tone make a different word. Think of three level tones, high (1), middle (3) and low (6); two rising tones, from the middle (2) and from low (5); and one falling tone (4), the lowest of all.

Pairs that are easy to mix up

Tones 3 and 6 are both level, one step apart. Tones 2 and 5 both rise, from different heights; some younger speakers barely separate them, but dictionaries and this course keep them apart. Famous pairs: 四 sei3 (four) and 死 sei2 (to die); 係 hai6 (to be) and 喺 hai2 (at, in). More twins: 九 gau2 (nine), 夠 gau3 (enough) and 舊 gau6 (old); 粉 fan2 (rice noodles) and 瞓 fan3 (to sleep). 馬 maa5 (horse) has the same low rise as 買 maai5.

Syllables ending in p, t or k

A syllable that ends in p, t or k is short and clipped, and it only comes in tone 1, 3 or 6: 七 cat1 (seven), 八 baat3 (eight), 六 luk6 (six). These are the same pitches as the three level tones, just cut short: high (1), middle (3) and low (6).

Changed tones

In some words the last syllable rises. 話 waa6 on its own has tone 6, but in 電話 din6 waa2 (phone) it rises to tone 2. In 媽媽 maa4 maa1 (mom) the second syllable goes up to tone 1. The Jyutping line always writes the tone people actually say. It also keeps the dictionary forms of the lazy sounds from lesson 1: nei5 for 你, even if you hear lei5.

No English question rise

Every syllable keeps its own tone, even at the end of a question. Do not push the last word up the English way: a small word at the end, such as 呀 aa3, does the asking (lesson 6).

Most common mistake: pulling every syllable up to a high, flat tone 1. ngo1 soeng1 maai1 → 我想買 ngo5 soeng2 maai5: low rising, middle rising, low rising.
