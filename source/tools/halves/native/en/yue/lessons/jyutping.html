This course teaches Cantonese as it is spoken in Hong Kong, written in traditional characters the way people write it in messages and ads, with Jyutping to show the sounds.

One character, one syllable

English letters spell sounds. A Cantonese character stands for one syllable with a meaning, so every Cantonese phrase in this course has a Jyutping line under it: one chunk per character, each ending in a number from 1 to 6. The number is the tone, and the next lesson is all about it. 美國人 mei5 gwok3 jan4 (American) is three characters, three syllables, three numbers.

Initials: the consonant at the start

In Cantonese, b, d and g have no puff of air, while p, t and k have a strong one. Hold your hand in front of your mouth to feel it. Several other letters do not mean what English readers expect:

Finals: the rest of the syllable

  • aa is long, as in father; a is short, like the u in but.
  • eo and oe are vowels English lacks: say the u of fur with rounded lips. oe is the longer one.
  • yu is "ee" with rounded lips, never the word you.
  • eu is a rare final that slides from "eh" to "oo"; oi is the oy of boy, and ui runs "oo" into "ee".
  • Final p, t and k are stopped, not released: close the mouth and stop, with no puff and no extra vowel.

The vowel can stand alone, slide into i or u, or end in m, n, ng, p, t or k. The chart shows every final, one vowel family per row. Read across a row and you see the same vowel letter with different endings. A few change sound: in ing and ik the i is closer to the i of sing, in ung and uk the u is closer to the oo of book, and ei and ou sound like the ay of day and the oh of go. eu, em and ep are rare.

Syllables with no vowel

m and ng can be whole syllables. 唔 m4 (not) is a low hum with closed lips, not "um". 五 ng5 (five) is the ng of sing on its own.

Putting a syllable together

Now put the pieces together. A syllable is an initial, a final and a tone number: 香 is h + oeng + tone 1, hoeng1, and 港 is g + ong + tone 2, gong2. Together they make 香港 hoeng1 gong2, Hong Kong. Lazy sounds, and Yale

Many Hong Kong speakers say lei5 for 你 (you), o5 for 我 and m5 for 五. These "lazy sounds" are very common, but the course writes the dictionary forms, which everyone understands: nei5, ngo5, ng5. If you learned the older Yale system: Jyutping j is Yale y, z and c are Yale j and ch, and eo and oe are Yale eu.

The Jyutping line is a crutch: listen to the audio first, and hide the line once the characters feel familiar.

Most common mistake: reading j as in jam. 有 read "jow" → 有 jau5, starting like yes.
