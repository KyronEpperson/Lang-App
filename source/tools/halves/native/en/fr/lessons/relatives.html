English joins a description to a noun with "who", "that", "which" or "where", and often drops the word completely: "the movie I saw". French has three words for this job, qui, que and où, and never drops them. Choosing between qui and que depends ‹on› one thing only: what comes right after.

‹qui›: the next word is a verb

Use qui when it is the subject of the next verb: the person or thing that does the action. It means "who", "that" or "which", for people and things alike. qui never shortens before a vowel. Think of qui as standing in for the noun before it, in the subject slot of the next verb.

{{ex:fr.s0235}}{{ex:fr.s0236}}

The verb after qui agrees with the noun that qui stands for: les amis qui parlent français.

‹que›: the next word is a subject

Use que when the next verb already has its own subject (je, tu, Léa…). It is where English can leave out "that", and it works for people as well as things: when English says "who" or "whom" with a new subject after it, French still says que. Before a vowel, que becomes qu'.

{{ex:fr.s0237}}{{ex:fr.s0238}}

A quick test: look at the word right after the gap. A verb means qui; a subject means que.

‹où›: where, and when

où joins a place, like "where". After a time word such as le jour or l'année, French also uses où where English says "when" or nothing at all. Keep the accent: ou without it means "or".

In the middle of a sentence

The description can also sit between a subject and its verb, exactly as in English. Find the main verb at the end, and the sentence falls into place.

{{ex:fr.s0241}}
Most common mistake: dropping the word, as English does. le film j'ai vu → le film que j'ai vu.
