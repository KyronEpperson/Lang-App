English uses "to", "at" and "in" for places. French often uses one word, à, for all three, and it changes shape with the article that follows.

‹à› + the article: ‹au›, ‹à la›, ‹à l'›, ‹aux›

à and le can't sit side by side: they contract to au. With les they contract to aux. à la and à l' stay as they are. This contraction is not optional.

de (of, from) works the same way: de + le = du, de + les = des.

{{ex:fr.s0128}}

Cities and countries

For a city, just à. For a country, French looks at its gender: en for a feminine country (most end in -e: en France), au for a masculine one, aux for a plural one.

{{ex:fr.s0058}}{{ex:fr.s0129}}{{ex:fr.s0130}}

Where exactly

dans (in, inside), sur (on), sous (under), devant (in front of), derrière (behind), à côté de (next to). chez means "at someone's place": chez Léa, chez moi (at my place, at home).

{{ex:fr.s0131}}{{ex:fr.s0132}}

Getting there

English says "on the train". French says en train, and the same en goes with voiture, bus and avion. On foot is à pied.

{{ex:fr.s0133}}

When

Months take en (en mai), clock times take à (à huit heures). A day on its own needs nothing: lundi is "on Monday". With le, it means every week.

{{ex:fr.s0134}}
Most common mistake: keeping ‹à› and ‹le› apart. Je vais à le cinéma. → Je vais au cinéma. ‹à› + ‹le› always contracts to ‹au›.
