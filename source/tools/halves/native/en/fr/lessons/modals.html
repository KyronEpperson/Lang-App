English has "want to", "can" and "must" or "have to". French has three verbs for these, and they work the same way: conjugate the first verb, and leave the second one in the infinitive.

The three verbs

vouloir (want)pouvoir (can)devoir (must)
jeveuxpeuxdois
tuveuxpeuxdois
il, elle, onveutpeutdoit
nousvoulonspouvonsdevons
vousvoulezpouvezdevez
ils, ellesveulentpeuventdoivent

The je, tu and il forms sound alike; only the spelling changes (-x or -s, then -t).

One conjugated verb, then the infinitive

English sometimes adds "to" (want to leave) and sometimes doesn't (can leave). French never adds a word: the infinitive follows directly. At a pharmacy or a doctor's office, these three verbs carry most of what you need.

Asking for help

To a stranger, Vous pouvez…? is a direct, normal request. You've met the softer Vous pourriez…? in the polite lesson; both are fine.

Saying no

ne … pas goes around the conjugated verb, not the infinitive.

{{ex:fr.s0150}}

Know how to: savoir

English uses "can" for skills too: "I can swim." French uses savoir (to know) for a skill you've learned: je sais, tu sais, il sait, nous savons, vous savez, ils savent. pouvoir means you're able or allowed right now: Je peux nager is "I'm allowed to swim" or "I'm able to swim today".

Most common mistake: conjugating both verbs. Je veux je pars. → Je veux partir. Only the first verb changes; the second stays in the infinitive.
