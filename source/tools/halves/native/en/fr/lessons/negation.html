English makes a verb negative with "do not": I don't speak German. French has no "do" here. Instead, it wraps the verb in two words: ne before it, pas after it.

‹ne … pas› goes around the verb

Put ne right after the subject and pas right after the conjugated verb. Everything else stays where it was. There is nothing to add for "do": the verb keeps its usual ending.

{{ex:fr.s0062}}{{ex:fr.s0063}}

Before a vowel, ne becomes n', just like je becomes j'.

{{ex:fr.s0064}}{{ex:fr.s0065}}

The agreement doesn't change in the negative: contente still matches elle.

‹pas de›: not any

After a negative verb, un, une and des all become de (or d' before a vowel). English says "I don't have a car"; French says "I don't have of car".

YesNo
J'ai une voiture.Je n'ai pas de voiture.
Il a des enfants.Il n'a pas d'enfants.
{{ex:fr.s0066}}{{ex:fr.s0067}}

One exception: after être, the article stays, as you'll see with c'est.

Other negatives

Swap pas for another word to change the meaning. Learn to recognize these three:

FrenchEnglish
ne … jamaisnever
ne … plusnot anymore, no more
ne … riennothing

What you'll hear

In relaxed speech, French people often drop the ne: you'll hear Je sais pas for Je ne sais pas (I don't know). A later lesson covers this. In writing, and in this course's typed answers, keep the ne.

Most common mistake: putting both words before the verb. Je ne pas parle → Je ne parle pas.
