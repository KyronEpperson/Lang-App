Short sentences get you understood; small joining words make you sound natural. Most of them work just like their English partners and sit in the same place in the sentence, so this lesson is mostly about meaning, plus two traps: a sound and an accent.

and, or, but

et is "and", ou is "or", mais is "but". One sound trap: the t of et is never said, not even before a vowel. et un café keeps the two words apart.

‹ou› and ‹où›

They sound exactly the same. ou with no accent is "or"; où with a grave accent is "where". The accent is the only difference, so type it. A quick test: if you could say "or else" in English, you need ou; if the sentence asks about a place, you need où.

so, because

alors and donc both mean "so" and introduce a result. parce que means "because" and gives the reason; it answers pourquoi ("why"). Before a vowel, que becomes qu': parce qu'il pleut. Keep them apart: alors looks forward to the result, parce que looks back at the cause.

{{ex:fr.s0191}}{{ex:fr.s0192}}

first, then

To tell things in order, use d'abord (first), then puis or ensuite (then, next). They work with any tense, including the ‹passé composé›.

{{ex:fr.s0193}}

too, also

aussi means "too" or "also". It goes after the verb, not before it: Je parle aussi espagnol. After a stress pronoun it means "me too", "you too".

{{ex:fr.s0194}}

when

quand is "when", both in questions and to join two sentences. As in English, the part that starts with quand can come first or second: Je fais du vélo quand il fait beau means the same thing. Listen for the linking sound: before a vowel, the d of quand is said as a t.

{{ex:fr.s0195}}
Most common mistake: typing où without its accent when you mean "where". Ou est la gare? → Où est la gare? Without the accent, it says "or".
