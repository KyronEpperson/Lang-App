English has one word for "my", whatever you own: my brother, my sister, my parents. French has three, and the choice depends on the thing you own, not on you.

my, your, his and her

Pick the form by the noun that follows: masculine, feminine or plural.

‹son› and ‹sa›: the noun decides

This is the big surprise. English chooses "his" or "her" by the owner. French doesn't care who the owner is: sa mère is "his mother" or "her mother", because mère is feminine. son père is "his father" or "her father". The context tells you whose.

{{ex:fr.s0090}}{{ex:fr.s0091}}

‹mon› before a vowel

ma amie would put two vowels side by side, and French avoids that. Before a vowel sound, ma, ta, sa become mon, ton, son, even with a feminine noun. You hear a linking n: mon amie. Written down, the noun keeps its feminine -e.

this, that, these, those

English splits "this" and "that". French usually doesn't: one word covers both, and it agrees like the others. ce for a masculine noun, cette for a feminine one, ces for any plural. Before a masculine noun that starts with a vowel or a silent ‹h›, ce becomes cet, and its ‹t› is sounded: cet ami, cet hôtel.
