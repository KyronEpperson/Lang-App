You already know one way to talk about the future: je vais travailler, "I'm going to work". French has a second one, the futur simple, which English translates with "will": je travaillerai, "I'll work". In conversation, je vais + verb is very common for plans. The futur simple is common too, especially for things further ahead, for predictions and promises, and in writing.

Building it

Start from the infinitive and add endings that look like the present of avoir: -ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont. Verbs in -re drop their final e first: prendre → je prendrai. Because the endings go on the whole infinitive, you hear an r before the ending in every form: that r is the sign of the future.

Irregular stems

A few very common verbs change the stem, but the endings stay the same. Learn these six: être → ser-, avoir → aur-, aller → ir-, faire → fer-, pouvoir → pourr-, venir → viendr-. Verbs not on this list follow the regular pattern, so the futur simple has far fewer surprises than the present tense.

Because avoir becomes aur-, "there will be" is il y aura, just as "there was" was il y avait.

quand + future

English says "When I'm in Paris, I'll visit the Louvre", with the present after "when". French is more logical: if the time is in the future, both verbs are in the future. If you forget, people will still understand you, but it is one of the clearest signs of an English speaker.

{{ex:fr.s0217}}

-ai, not -ais

The je form ends in -ai. Don't add an s: je voudrais (I'd like) is a different form, the one you use to order politely. In speech the two can sound alike, so the spelling is what you need to watch.

Most common mistake: the present after quand, as in English. Quand je suis à Paris, je visiterai le Louvre → Quand je serai à Paris, je visiterai le Louvre.
