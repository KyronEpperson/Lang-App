English puts "it", "them" and "her" after the verb: "I'll take it." French puts these small pronouns before the conjugated verb: Je la prends, literally "I it take". This is the main habit to build. The pronouns themselves are short and easy; what takes practice is putting them in the French position without thinking.

it, them: le, la, les

For "it" or "him" or "her" as the object, French uses le or la, and the choice agrees with the gender of the thing or person you mean, not with the English word "it". For "them", use les. Before a vowel, le and la become l'. So to French grammar a shirt is "her": la chemise, je la prends. Getting the gender right matters as much as getting the place right.

le pullJe le prends.
la vesteJe la prends.

to him, to her, to them: lui, leur

Verbs used with à, such as parler à, téléphoner à and donner à, take lui (to him or to her: one word for both) and leur (to them). These two do not show gender, and this leur never takes an -s.

{{ex:fr.s0220}}{{ex:fr.s0221}}

some, of it: en

en replaces a noun with du, de la, des, or a noun after a number. English can just say "I'd like two"; French needs en.

{{ex:fr.s0222}}

there: y

y replaces à + a place. English can leave "there" out ("I'm going"); French cannot. You will hear it all the time in On y va?, "Shall we go?"

{{ex:fr.s0223}}

Infinitives and ne … pas

With a verb + infinitive, the pronoun goes right before the infinitive it belongs to. With ne … pas, it stays glued to the verb, inside the ne.

{{ex:fr.s0224}}{{ex:fr.s0225}}

In the passé composé, the conjugated verb is the helper, so the pronoun goes before avoir or être.
