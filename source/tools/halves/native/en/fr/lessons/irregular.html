Most French verbs follow the -er pattern you know from parler. The most frequent ones don't. These four are worth learning by heart, because you'll use them every day.

The four verbs

aller (go)faire (do, make)venir (come)prendre (take)
jevaisfaisviensprends
tuvasfaisviensprends
il, elle, onvafaitvientprend
nousallonsfaisonsvenonsprenons
vousallezfaitesvenezprenez
ils, ellesvontfontviennentprennent

Look at the ils row: no -ent on vont and font, and a double n in viennent and prennent. The vous form of faire is faites, never "faisez".

aller: going

aller takes à and its contractions: au, à la, aux.

faire: doing, and the weather

English says "it is cold". For the weather, French says "it makes": il fait. Activities also use faire with du, de la: faire du vélo (go biking), faire du sport.

{{ex:fr.s0137}}{{ex:fr.s0138}}{{ex:fr.s0139}}

The same pattern gives il fait chaud (it's hot) and il fait mauvais (the weather's bad). Rain gets its own verb: Il pleut (It's raining).

venir: coming, and where you're from

venir de + a city says where you come from. de becomes d' before a vowel.

{{ex:fr.s0140}}{{ex:fr.s0141}}

prendre: taking

prendre is for transport, food and drink: you "take" the bus, a coffee, a croissant. That's why a server hears Je vais prendre… when you order.

{{ex:fr.s0142}}{{ex:fr.s0143}}
Most common mistake: using être for the weather, as in English. Il est froid. → Il fait froid. Il est froid is for a thing that is cold to the touch, like a dish.
