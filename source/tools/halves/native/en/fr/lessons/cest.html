English uses "it is", "he is" and "she is" freely. French splits them into two patterns, and the choice depends on what comes next.

‹c'est› + a noun with its article

To say what something or someone is, use c'est (it is, this is, that is) with a noun and its article, a name, or a stress pronoun.

{{ex:fr.s0078}}{{ex:fr.s0079}}{{ex:fr.s0041}}

The negative is ce n'est pas, and the article stays: pas de is not used after être.

{{ex:fr.s0080}}

‹il est›, ‹elle est› + an adjective or a job

To describe a person you have already named, use il est or elle est with an adjective. Jobs work the same way, with no article: French says "he is doctor".

{{ex:fr.s0081}}{{ex:fr.s0082}}

If you want to keep the article, switch to c'est: C'est un médecin (he's a doctor) is also correct. What doesn't work is mixing the two: il est + un.

For a general comment on a thing or a situation, French also says c'est + adjective, and the adjective stays masculine: C'est beau! (It's beautiful!)

‹il y a›: there is, there are

English has two forms, "there is" and "there are". French has one: il y a, for one thing or many. It is usually followed by un, une or des.

{{ex:fr.s0083}}{{ex:fr.s0084}}

In the negative, un, une and des become de, as you learned with ne … pas.

{{ex:fr.s0085}}

To ask, put est-ce qu' in front. This is the question to ask a stranger in the street, after bonjour.
