How French spelling works

Under every French phrase in this course there is a pronunciation line in English letters. Each syllable gets the same weight: French has no strong stressed syllable, only the last one of a group a little longer.

French uses the same 26 letters as English, plus a few marks on vowels and on ‹c›. You already read the letters; what changes is the sound. Many sounds are spelled with two or three letters (ou, eau, ch), and many letters at the end of a word are not said at all. So the pieces to learn are the marks, the letter groups and the silent letters. Once you know them you can read almost any word aloud, even one you have never seen.

Accents and marks

An accent is part of the spelling, never decoration. On e it changes the sound: é is always ay, è and ê are eh. On the other vowels it does not: à and â are ah, î is ee, ô is oh, û is ew, and ù appears only in où (where). Two dots (ë, ï) mean: say this vowel on its own, not as part of a pair, so naïf is nah-eef. ç is always s, even before ‹a›, ‹o› and ‹u›, where a plain ‹c› is k. œ is ‹o› and ‹e› joined, said uh. Tap a letter to see how it reads.

Eight vowel sounds

The line always spells a vowel the same way. Two are new for English speakers: ew (say ee, then round your lips as if to whistle) and uh (the a of about, with rounded lips). French ay and oh are pure: no y or w glide at the end.

Nasal vowels

A vowel followed by n or m at the end of a syllable is said through the nose, and the n itself is not pronounced. The line shows this with a capital N: ahN (an, en), ohN (on), aN (in, ain), uhN (un). English speakers tend to merge ahN and ohN; they are different words.

Silent final letters

Most final consonants are silent. Four are usually sounded: c, r, f, l (think of the word careful). But -er and -ez at the end sound ay, and a final e is silent, except in short words such as le and je.

Linking words

Liaison (linking): in a few fixed places, a silent final letter is said before a word that starts with a vowel. The line puts that sound at the start of the next word. A sounded final consonant also runs into a following vowel (il est sounds like ee leh), but the line keeps those words apart. Elision: le and la drop their vowel before a vowel and become l'.

Consonants

French r is made at the back of the throat, close to a light gargle. zh is the s in measure (French j), ny is the ny in canyon (French gn), and gh is the hard g of get.

Before e and i, g and c go soft: ge, gi are zh, ce, ci are s. Before ‹a›, ‹o› and ‹u› they stay hard. qu is just k, th is just t, and h is never said. ill is usually y: fille is feey (but ville is veel). One s between two vowels is z: maison, meh-zohN.

The letter names

The letters have French names. You need them to spell your name or an email address, and a few trip English speakers up: E is uh, I is ee, G is zhay and J is zhee (the reverse of English), H is ahsh, U is ew, W is doo-bluh-vay (double v) and Y is ee-grehk (Greek i). Tap a letter to hear its name.

Read whole words

Now put the pieces together. Read a word left to right in chunks, not letter by letter: find the letter groups, say each as one sound, and drop the silent letters at the end. b + eau + c + ou + p is beaucoup, boh-koo: ‹eau› is oh, ‹ou› is oo, and the final ‹p› is silent. Between words, watch for liaison: les enfants is lay zahN-fahN. Every card below is a common word. Read it aloud before you flip it.

‹le›, ‹la›, ‹les›

Nouns in this course come with a small word in front that means "the": le or la for one thing, les for more than one. Every noun is masculine or feminine (lesson 3 explains), so learn each noun with its word for "the".
