French numbers up to 69 work much like English: a word for the tens, a hyphen, then the unit. Above 69, French counts in twenties, so 70 to 99 need a little arithmetic.

Only the 1 in 21 to 71 takes et; 81 is quatre-vingt-un. Since a spelling reform in 1990, writing every part with hyphens (vingt-et-un) is also correct. In Belgium and Switzerland you'll hear septante (70) and nonante (90).

Telling the time

English says "it's two" or "two o'clock". French always says il est plus the number plus heures (hours), and heure takes an -s from two on. Add et quart (quarter past), et demie (half past) or moins le quart (quarter to).

{{ex:fr.s0100}}{{ex:fr.s0101}}

six and dix change sound. Alone, they end in an s sound. Before a vowel, the ‹x› links as a z: six heures. Before a consonant, it goes silent: six pommes.

{{ex:fr.s0102}}

Schedules, shops and trains use the 24-hour clock: 8 p.m. is vingt heures. Minutes simply follow the hour.

{{ex:fr.s0103}}

Prices

Say the euros, then the cents, with no word for "point": deux euros cinquante is €2.50. The verb agrees with what costs: ça coûte, but les pommes coûtent.

{{ex:fr.s0104}}{{ex:fr.s0105}}

Dates

English puts the month first; French puts the day first, with le: le quatorze juillet. The first of a month is le premier; every other day uses a plain number. Months are lowercase.

{{ex:fr.s0106}}{{ex:fr.s0107}}
Most common mistake: dropping heures the English way. Il est deux. → Il est deux heures. The word for hours is never left out, and it takes an ‹-s› after ‹deux›.
