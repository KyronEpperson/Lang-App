English has "I ate" and "I have eaten". For a finished action, French uses one form for both, the passé composé: j'ai mangé. It has two parts, like "have eaten": a helper verb (also called an auxiliary), conjugated for the subject, and a past participle that carries the meaning.

The past participle

For -er verbs, replace -er with -é: parler → parlé, manger → mangé. It sounds the same as the infinitive, but the accent is not optional: parle is "speak", parlé is "spoken". A few common verbs have their own participle: faire → fait, prendre → pris, voir → vu, avoir → eu, être → été, finir → fini. Think of the participle as the English "-ed" word: "spoken", "eaten", "seen". Once you know it, the rest is a verb you already know.

Most verbs: ‹avoir›

Conjugate avoir in the present and add the participle. The participle does not change.

Coming and going: ‹être›

A small group of verbs, mostly about moving or changing state, use être instead: aller, venir, arriver, partir, entrer, sortir, rester, rentrer, tomber, naître, mourir. Reflexive verbs use être too (je me suis levé). English never says "I am gone to Paris" for "I went", so this has to be learned. A handy way to remember the group: most of these verbs come in pairs of opposites, like going and coming, arriving and leaving, going in and going out, being born and dying.

With ‹être›, the participle agrees

With être, the participle agrees with the subject like an adjective: add -e for a feminine subject, -s for a plural one. You usually can't hear it, so it only shows in writing.

Saying no

Ne … pas goes around the helper verb, not around the participle. English does something similar with "not": "I have not finished". In French, the two halves of the negative wrap around the helper.

Most common mistake: using avoir with a verb of movement. J'ai allé au cinéma → Je suis allé au cinéma (a woman writes allée).
