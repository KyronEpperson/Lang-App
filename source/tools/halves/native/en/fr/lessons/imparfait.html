The passé composé tells what happened: one finished event. French has a second past, the imparfait, for the background: what was going on, what things were like, and what used to happen again and again. English spreads this over "was raining", "used to play" and plain "was".

Building it

Take the nous form of the present, remove -ons, and add the endings below. This works for almost every verb, even irregular ones: nous faisons → je faisais, nous prenons → je prenais. The one exception is être, whose stem is ét-.

parler (nous parlons)être
jeparlaisétais
tuparlaisétais
il, elle, onparlaitétait
nousparlionsétions
vousparliezétiez
ils, ellesparlaientétaient

Four of the six forms sound exactly the same. Only the spelling shows who is talking.

Background and description

Weather, time, feelings and what a place was like go in the imparfait. Three phrases you will use constantly: c'était (it was), il y avait (there was, there were), il faisait (the weather was).

The same goes for the avoir expressions from earlier lessons. English says "I was hungry"; French keeps avoir and puts it in the imparfait.

Habits

Where English says "used to" or "would" for a repeated action, French uses the imparfait. Words like souvent (often), tous les jours (every day) and le samedi (on Saturdays) are good signs that you need it.

The two together

In a story, the imparfait sets the scene and the passé composé moves the action forward. Ask yourself: is it a single event that happened, or the situation around it? A useful picture: the imparfait is the film running in the background, and the passé composé is the moment something happens on screen.

{{ex:fr.s0210}}
Most common mistake: using the passé composé for a description because English says "was". Quand j'ai été petit… → Quand j'étais petit…
