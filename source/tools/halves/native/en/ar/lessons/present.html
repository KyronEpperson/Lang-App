English verbs barely change: I study, she studies. An Arabic present-tense verb always shows who is doing it, with a prefix at the front and, for two persons, an ending. Here is one verb, "study", for every person you know.

WhoVerbEnglish
أَنَاأَدْرُسI study
أَنْتَتَدْرُسyou study (to a man)
أَنْتِتَدْرُسِينyou study (to a woman)
هُوَيَدْرُسhe studies
هِيَتَدْرُسshe studies
نَحْنُنَدْرُسwe study
هُميَدْرُسُونthey study

The prefix says who

أَدْرُس adrus starts with أَ for "I", نَدْرُس nadrus with نَ for "we", and يَدْرُس yadrus with يَ for "he". تَ does two jobs: it means "you" to a man and also "she", so تَدْرُس tadrus can be either. To a woman, "you" also adds -īn: تَدْرُسِين tadrusīn. "They" adds -ūn to the "he" form: يَدْرُسُون yadrusūn.

The pronoun can go

Because the prefix already says who, the pronoun is often left out: أَدْرُس الْعَرَبِيَّة is a complete sentence. Keep هُوَ or هِيَ when you need to make clear who you mean, or to stress it. On its own, تَدْرُس could be "you study" (to a man) or "she studies"; usually the conversation tells you which, and adding هِيَ settles it.

Other verbs work the same way

Change the middle and keep the prefixes: أَسْكُن askun I live, أَعْمَل aʿmal I work, أَشْرَب ashrab I drink, أَتَكَلَّم atakallam I speak. A few verbs, like أُحِبّ uḥibb "I like" and أُرِيد "I want", have u after the prefix letter instead of a, as in يُحِبّ "he likes". The prefix letters themselves are the same.

Things in general take the article

English says "I like coffee" and "I speak English" with no "the". Arabic puts ال on things meant in general and on languages: أُحِبّ الْقَهْوَة, أَتَكَلَّم الْإِنْجِلِيزِيَّة.

Nothing extra at the end

Formal grammar adds a short vowel to the end of these verbs. This course, like everyday speech, leaves the last letter bare.

Most common mistake: using the "he" prefix for a woman. هِيَ يَدْرُس → هِيَ تَدْرُس.
