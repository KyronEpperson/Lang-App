English joins two nouns in two ways: "the student's book" and "the door of the house". Arabic has one way, the iḍāfa: put the two nouns side by side, the owned thing first and the owner second, with an unspoken "of" between them. "The student's book" is "book the-student".

The first word never takes ال

In كِتَاب الطَّالِب kitāb aṭ-ṭālib, the article on the second word makes the whole phrase definite: "the student's book". So the first noun never adds its own ال. Compare an adjective, where both words take it: الْكِتَاب الْجَدِيد al-kitāb al-jadīd, "the new book".

Everyday iḍāfas

Many things English names with one word are iḍāfas in Arabic. A passport is "permit of the travel"; the room key is "key of the room". You have used one since your first greeting: صَبَاح الْخَيْر ṣabāḥ al-khayr, "morning of the good".

{{ex:ar.s0126}}{{ex:ar.s0127}}

ة at the front is said -at

When the first word ends in ة, say a t there: مَحَطَّةْ الْقِطَار maḥaṭṭat al-qiṭār, the train station. Arabic usually writes a plain ة; this course puts a small sukun on it (ةْ) to remind you to say the t. Place names work the same way, and since a name is already definite, it needs no ال: جَامِعَةْ تُولْسَا jāmiʿat tūlsā, مَدِينَةْ نِيُويُورْك madīnat niyūyūrk.

Adding an adjective

An adjective never goes between the two nouns. It comes after the whole iḍāfa, agrees with the noun it describes, and takes ال because the phrase is definite. In the example below, جَدِيدَة is feminine, so it describes the car, not the teacher. Just recognise this for now.

Most common mistake: giving the first word the article, as English "the" seems to ask. الْكِتَاب الطَّالِب → كِتَاب الطَّالِب. And keep Arabic order, owned thing first: الطَّالِب كِتَاب → كِتَاب الطَّالِب.
