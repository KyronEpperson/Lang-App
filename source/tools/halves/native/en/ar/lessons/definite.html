English has two little words, "a" and "the". Arabic has only one: the article ال, "the". For "a", Arabic uses nothing at all. This sounds easy, but it is the mistake English speakers make most often in their first two years, so this lesson takes it slowly.

No word for "a"

A noun on its own is indefinite: غُرْفَة ghurfa is "a room". Add ال and it becomes definite: الْغُرْفَة al-ghurfa, "the room". English speakers often reach for the word for "one", وَاحِد wāḥid, to say "a". Don't: وَاحِد سَيَّارَة is not Arabic. The bare noun already says "a".

Three things make a noun definite

  • The article ال: الْغُرْفَة, the room.
  • A name: سَارَة sāra or شِيكَاغُو shīkāghū never takes ال. A few place names have ال built in, such as الْقَاهِرَة al-qāhira (Cairo); keep it.
  • A possessive ending ("my", "your": lesson 19). The ending already means "the", so it never takes ال as well.
{{ex:ar.s0086}}

Phrase or sentence: one ال decides

An adjective follows its noun and copies its "the". Article on both words: a phrase, "the new apartment". Article on the noun only: a whole sentence, "The apartment is new". Article on neither: "a new apartment". Read carefully: often that one small ال on the adjective is the only thing that tells a phrase from a sentence.

Where Arabic says "the" and English doesn't

When you talk about a thing in general, Arabic uses the article: "I like coffee" is "I like the coffee", أُحِبّ الْقَهْوَة uḥibb al-qahwa. Names of languages take it too: الْعَرَبِيَّة al-ʿarabiyya (Arabic), الْإِنْجِلِيزِيَّة al-injilīziyya (English). So does "at home": فِي الْبَيْت fī al-bayt.

{{ex:ar.s0089}}{{ex:ar.s0090}}
Most common mistake: leaving the article off a general statement, the way English does. أُحِبّ قَهْوَة → أُحِبّ الْقَهْوَة. And for "the new apartment", put ال on both words: الشَّقَّة جَدِيدَة → الشَّقَّة الْجَدِيدَة.
