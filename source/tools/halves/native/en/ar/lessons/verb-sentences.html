English sentences start with the subject: "Sara studies Arabic." Arabic can do that too, but it can also start with the verb: تَدْرُس سَارَة الْعَرَبِيَّة tadrus sāra al-ʿarabiyya, word for word "studies Sara Arabic". A sentence that starts with its verb is called a verb sentence. Both orders are normal statements; news and stories start with the verb very often, and conversation often starts with the person.

Verb first is not a question

In English, putting the verb first turns a sentence into a question: "Does Sara…?", "Is she…?". In Arabic it doesn't. A yes or no question starts with هَل, whatever order follows.

{{ex:ar.s0168}}{{ex:ar.s0169}}

The verb still agrees

The prefix agrees with the subject wherever the subject stands. A woman's name or a feminine noun takes the same verb form as هِيَ; a man's name takes the form of هُوَ. So Maryam gets تَسْكُن taskun, and Omar gets يُحِبّ yuḥibb.

Plural subjects, for recognition

One surprise: when the verb comes first, it stays singular even if the subject is plural. When the subject comes first, the verb is plural, as you learned in the last lesson. You only need to recognize the first pattern for now.

{{ex:ar.s0172}}{{ex:ar.s0173}}

Where the object goes

In a verb sentence the usual order is verb, then who, then what: يَشْرَب أَحْمَد الشَّاي yashrab aḥmad ash-shāy. The object follows the same article rules as everywhere else: ال for languages and for things in general, and ال on both a noun and its adjective, as in الْقَهْوَة الْعَرَبِيَّة.

When you start with the verb, choose its form before you say the name: think "who is it?" first, then pick the prefix.

Most common mistake: making the verb plural before a plural subject, as English would. يَعْمَلُون الطُّلَّاب → يَعْمَل الطُّلَّاب.
