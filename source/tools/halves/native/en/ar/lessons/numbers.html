Arabic numbers are words, as in English, with two surprises: from 21 on, the units come first ("five and twenty"), and much of the Arab world writes its own digits.

11 to 19

Say the unit, then عَشَر ʿashar ("ten"): ثَلَاثَة عَشَر thalātha ʿashar is "three-ten", 13. Eleven and twelve have their own first word: أَحَد عَشَر aḥad ʿashar and اِثْنَا عَشَر ithnā ʿashar.

The tens

Most tens are the unit with -ūn on the end: ثَلَاثَة (3) gives ثَلَاثُون thalāthūn (30). Twenty is the odd one: عِشْرُون ʿishrūn.

21 to 99: units first

English says "twenty-five". Arabic says "five and twenty": the unit, then وَ wa ("and") joined to the ten. Because وَ is written as part of the next word, وَعِشْرُون is one word, and one tile in the exercises.

The digits

Egypt, the Levant, Iraq and the Gulf often write the digits in the table above, though the digits English uses are common there too; Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia use the same digits as English. Inside Arabic text, numbers still run left to right: ٢٥ is 25, not 52. Watch ٧ (7) and ٨ (8), which are the same shape upside down, and ٥, which is 5, not 0. Prices, phone numbers and bus numbers are often written this way, so it pays to learn the digits early.

"One" agrees and comes after

"One" follows the noun and agrees with it: feminine وَاحِدَة wāḥida after a feminine noun. You only need it to stress "one"; the bare noun already means "a".

Prices: the number alone

For prices, use the number by itself, with no "is". (Before a noun, 3 to 10 take the opposite gender: ثَلَاثَةْ كُتُب thalāthat kutub "three books", but ثَلَاث سَيَّارَات thalāth sayyārāt "three cars". You'll meet that later; the number alone is always safe.) If a price goes by too fast, ask for it again or ask to see it written.

{{ex:ar.s0110}}
Most common mistake: saying the tens first, the English way. Twenty-five: عِشْرُون وَخَمْسَة → خَمْسَة وَعِشْرُون.
