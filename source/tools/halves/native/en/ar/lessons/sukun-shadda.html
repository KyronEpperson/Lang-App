Two more vowel marks complete the set you need for reading. Both sit above the letter, and English-language textbooks call them by their Arabic names too: sukun and shadda.

Sukun: no vowel here

Sukun is a small circle above a letter. It means the letter has no vowel after it: the next consonant follows straight on. English speakers like to slip a little vowel between two consonants; sukun says don't. In مَكْتَب maktab "office", ك carries sukun, so the word has two beats, not three: no vowel between k and t.

Shadda: a doubled letter

Shadda is a small w-shaped sign. It doubles the letter: you hold it a moment longer, the way English holds the k in "bookcase". It is one long sound, not two separate ones. The romanisation writes the letter twice.

Gliding vowels: aw and ay

Fatha followed by و or ي with sukun makes a gliding vowel: aw, close to the ow in "how", and ay, close to the i in "bite". Here و and ي are neither plain consonants nor long vowels, and the romanisation shows the glide as two letters.

LettersSound
بَوْbaw, close to "bough"
بَيْbay, close to "buy"

Reading a whole word

You can now read any fully marked word: every letter carries a vowel mark, a sukun or a shadda, except the last one, which is usually bare. Read the Arabic first and check the romanisation after: هٰذَا مَكْتَب جَدِيد.

{{ex:ar.s0038}}
Most common mistake: saying a doubled letter once. دَرَسَ when you mean "he taught" → دَرَّسَ: hold the r, or you have said "he studied".
