English puts the adjective first: "a big house". Arabic puts it after: "house big". And the adjective agrees with its noun in two ways at once, gender and "the". You have seen pieces of this; this lesson puts the whole rule together.

After the noun

The noun comes first, then the adjective: بَيْت كَبِير bayt kabīr. With no ال on either word, the phrase means "a big house".

{{ex:ar.s0007}}

Agreement 1: gender

After a feminine noun, the adjective adds ة: مَدِينَة madīna (city) gives مَدِينَة جَمِيلَة madīna jamīla. People work the same way: a new student is طَالِب جَدِيد ṭālib jadīd for a man, طَالِبَة جَدِيدَة ṭāliba jadīda for a woman.

{{ex:ar.s0119}}

Agreement 2: the article

If the noun is definite, the adjective is definite too: both words take ال. If the noun has none, neither does the adjective.

a … (indefinite)بَيْت كَبِيرمَدِينَة جَمِيلَة
the … (definite)الْبَيْت الْكَبِيرالْمَدِينَة الْجَمِيلَة

A phrase inside a sentence

A whole phrase can be the subject of a sentence with no "is". In الْبَيْت الْكَبِير جَمِيل al-bayt al-kabīr jamīl, the first two words agree (both have ال), and the third has none: it is the predicate, "is beautiful". The same works with a name: مَرْيَم maryam is definite, and the predicate can be a phrase of its own.

Two adjectives

The adjectives you know so far (big, small, new, old, beautiful, cheap, tall, short, easy, difficult, hot, cold) all follow these two rules, with no exceptions in the singular. Line them up after the noun, each one agreeing, often joined by وَ ("and"). To say "very", add جِدًّا jiddan after the adjective: جَمِيل جِدًّا jamīl jiddan, "very beautiful". English puts "very" first; Arabic, as with its adjectives, puts it after.

{{ex:ar.s0123}}
Most common mistake: marking only one word. For "the new car", give the adjective both the ة and the article: السَّيَّارَة جَدِيد → السَّيَّارَة الْجَدِيدَة.
