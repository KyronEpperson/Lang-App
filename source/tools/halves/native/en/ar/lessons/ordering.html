You can count and ask questions; this lesson puts both to work in a café, a restaurant or a shop, where the same few phrases do almost everything.

"I'd like…": أُرِيد

أُرِيد urīd means "I want". With "please" after it, it is polite enough for any café or shop. Put the thing straight after it. It works for anything you want, the check included: أُرِيد الْحِسَاب urīd al-ḥisāb.

One small ending

Words that end in ة or ء, like قَهْوَة qahwa and مَاء māʾ, stay exactly as they are. Other words, when you ask for "a" something, take an extra -an in careful Arabic: أُرِيد شَايًا urīd shāyan "I'd like some tea". Recognise it when you hear it. To keep things simple, order something that ends in ة, or point at the menu and use the article, as in English "I'll have the mint tea".

"Please", to a man or a woman

Say مِن فَضْلِكَ min faḍlika to a man and مِن فَضْلِكِ min faḍliki to a woman. Only the last vowel changes, so look closely at the mark under the ك. To call a waiter over, you will also hear لَوْ سَمَحْتَ law samaḥta, "excuse me", which splits for a man and a woman in the same way.

How much?

بِكَم bikam means "for how much?". Point and say بِكَم هٰذَا؟ for a masculine thing, or بِكَم هٰذِهِ؟ bikam hādhihi? for a feminine one, such as a bag, حَقِيبَة ḥaqība. The answer is a number and a currency. From eleven to ninety-nine, the currency takes -an: عِشْرُون رِيَالًا ʿishrūn riyālan.

Paying, and a compliment

To ask for the check, name it with the article, then say please. And if you liked it, remember that the adjective agrees: قَهْوَة is feminine.
