Arabic is read from right to left, and most letters join to the next one, like handwriting. There are 28 letters. They write the consonants and long vowels; short vowels are small marks, taught in lesson 3.

Learn the letters in shape families

Many letters share one shape and differ only in their dots: how many, and above or below.

Under every Arabic word in this course there is a romanisation line. Its th (as in "think"), dh (as in "this"), kh, gh and sh are each one sound, written with two English letters. A dot under a letter, as in ḥ ṣ ḍ ṭ ẓ, means the throat or heavy version of a sound; lesson 2 is about those. The letter ج is said as the j of "jam" here, as on the news; in Egypt people say it as a hard g.

Your first words

The dots decide the word: بَيْت bayt is "house" and بِنْت bint is "girl". Read each word right to left, then check the romanisation.

Two signs you'll see everywhere

ة tāʾ marbūṭa at the end of a word usually means the word is feminine. It is said -a: سَيَّارَة sayyāra "car". A describing word comes after its noun, and it takes ة too when the noun is feminine. Even "this" has two forms: هٰذَا hādhā for masculine words, هٰذِهِ hādhihi for feminine ones.

The second sign is ال at the start of a word. It means "the": الْبَيْت al-bayt is "the house". Lesson 7 is all about it.

Which Arabic?

You are learning the Arabic everyone reads and everyone understands: Modern Standard Arabic (MSA), the language of books, signs, news and speeches in every Arab country. People chat in their own dialect; you'll hear different words in Cairo and in Amman. The Spoken Arabic deck shows you the most common ones. The Qurʾan uses the same script, but this course teaches modern Arabic, not Qurʾanic recitation.

Most common mistake: ignoring the dots. Reading بِنْت "girl" as بَيْت "house" → look at the middle letter: one dot above is ن, so the word is بِنْت; two dots below would be ي.
