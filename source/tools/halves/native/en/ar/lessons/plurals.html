English makes almost every plural with -s. Arabic has three ways, and which one a noun uses belongs to the word, so learn each plural together with its singular.

Groups of men: add -ūn

Many words for people simply add -ūn: مُدَرِّس mudarris "teacher" becomes مُدَرِّسُون mudarrisūn "teachers". The word stays whole and only an ending is added, so this is called a sound plural. A group of men, or of men and women together, takes -ūn. In everyday speech you will often hear -īn on the same words; this course writes -ūn.

Women, and many ة words: add -āt

For a group of women, drop the taa marbuta and add -āt: مُدَرِّسَة becomes مُدَرِّسَات mudarrisāt. Many things that end in ة do the same: سَيَّارَة sayyāra "car" becomes سَيَّارَات sayyārāt "cars".

{{ex:ar.s0149}}

Broken plurals: the inside changes

Many everyday nouns change shape inside, the way English says man and men, or mouse and mice. This is the broken plural. The root consonants keep their order, and the vowels around them change. English has only a handful of these; Arabic has hundreds, and they include many of the most common words. You can't predict the new shape yet, so learn it with the word.

A plural of things is "she"

This one surprises everybody. A plural of things, not people, behaves like one feminine noun: its adjective takes ة, "these" is هٰذِهِ hādhihi, and "they" is هِيَ. Plurals of people keep the plural: الطُّلَّاب, "they" = هُم.

Exactly two: for recognition

Arabic also has a form for exactly two things, the dual: كِتَابَان kitābān "two books". You only need to recognize its -ān ending for now.

Most common mistake: giving a plural of things a plural adjective, as English does. الْكُتُب كَبِيرُون → الْكُتُب كَبِيرَة.
