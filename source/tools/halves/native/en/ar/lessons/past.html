English marks the past with -ed or a new form: studied, went, ate. Arabic changes the verb too, but in its own way. The present-tense verb shows who with a prefix at the front; the past-tense verb shows who with an ending at the back, and it has no prefix at all.

One verb, every person

The basic past form is the "he" form, دَرَسَ darasa "he studied". Dictionaries and teachers use it as the name of the verb. The other persons add an ending to it.

WhoVerbEnglish
أَنَادَرَسْتُI studied
أَنْتَدَرَسْتَyou studied (to a man)
أَنْتِدَرَسْتِyou studied (to a woman)
هُوَدَرَسَhe studied
هِيَدَرَسَتshe studied
نَحْنُدَرَسْنَاwe studied
هُمدَرَسُواthey studied

Look closely at the first three: "I", "you" to a man and "you" to a woman are spelled with the same letters, and only the last vowel mark differs: -tu, -ta, -ti. "She" is دَرَسَت darasat, with -at. "They" ends in a long ū; the ا written after it is silent.

Other verbs follow the same pattern

Learn each verb by its "he" form, then add the same endings: ذَهَبَ dhahaba went, سَكَنَ sakana lived, عَمِلَ ʿamila worked, أَكَلَ akala ate, شَرِبَ shariba drank.

Word order and agreement

Everything from the lesson on verb sentences still holds: the verb can come first or after the subject, and it agrees with the subject. A woman's name takes the "she" ending, before or after the name, and a man's name takes the bare "he" form.

Time words for the past

أَمْس ams "yesterday" and الْأُسْبُوع الْمَاضِي al-usbūʿ al-māḍī "last week" usually go at the end or at the start.

"Didn't", for recognition

To say "didn't", Arabic uses a different little word with a present-tense verb. You'll meet it later; just recognize it when you see it.
