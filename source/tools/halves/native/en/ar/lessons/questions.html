English questions move words around: "The school is near" becomes "Is the school near?" and "Where is the school?". Arabic moves nothing. Put the question word at the front, keep the rest of the sentence as it is, and raise your voice at the end.

Yes or no: هَل

Put هَل hal in front of any sentence. The sentence itself does not change: still no "is", still no ال on the predicate. هَل has no meaning of its own; it works like a spoken question mark. In speech you can also skip it and just let your voice rise. Answer with نَعَم naʿam (yes) or لَا lā (no).

As with every sentence so far, there is no "is": "Where is the school?" is just "where the school?". The same frame works for anything you can name: a person, a place or a thing.

Two words for "what"

مَا mā goes before a noun or a pronoun: مَا اِسْمُكَ؟ mā ismuka? "What's your name?", مَا هٰذَا؟ mā hādhā? "What's this?". مَاذَا mādhā goes before a verb. Verbs come later in the course, so for now learn two whole questions with it.

{{ex:ar.s0102}}{{ex:ar.s0103}}

كَم

كَم kam asks "how many" or "how much". You already know كَم عُمْرُكَ؟ kam ʿumruka?, "How old are you?" (to a man), literally "how much is your age?". For prices, lesson 16 adds بِ in front: بِكَم.

What you'll hear in the street

Question words are where the dialects differ most. These MSA words are understood everywhere, but in Cairo or Amman you'll hear others. The Spoken Arabic deck gives them in its notes, for recognition. If someone answers you in dialect, don't worry: reply in the Arabic you know, and you will be understood.

Most common mistake: using مَاذَا before a noun, because both mean "what". مَاذَا اِسْمُكَ؟ → مَا اِسْمُكَ؟
