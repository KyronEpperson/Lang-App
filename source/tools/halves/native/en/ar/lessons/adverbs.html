Time words work much as in English: they usually go at the start or at the end of the sentence, and putting one first gives it a little more weight. Most of them you learn as fixed words.

Arabic has no special form for "I'm doing it right now". The present-tense verb covers both "I drink" and "I'm drinking", and the time word tells you which: أَشْرَب الشَّاي الْآن is "I'm drinking tea now", while أَشْرَب الشَّاي كُلّ يَوْم is "I drink tea every day".

Today is "the day"

الْيَوْم al-yawm is literally "the day", and it means "today". Without the article, يَوْم yawm is just "day", as in كُلّ يَوْم kull yawm "every day". Careful: كُلّ الْيَوْم with the article means "all day".

Words with -an

Many time words end in -an, the doubled mark with its extra ا: غَدًا ghadan, دَائِمًا dāʾiman, صَبَاحًا ṣabāḥan. "In the evening" is usually said another way: فِي الْمَسَاء fī al-masāʾ, with "in" and the article.

The days of the week

Each day is "day of the…": يَوْم plus a word with the article, an iḍāfa. Several are old number words: Sunday is "day of the first", Monday "day of the second". In conversation people often drop يَوْم. Notice that the weekend in most Arab countries falls on Friday and Saturday, so Sunday is a normal working day, the first of the week.

"Very" and "a lot" come after

English puts "very" before the adjective. Arabic puts جِدًّا jiddan after it: كَبِير جِدًّا, "very big". The adjective still agrees with its noun, and جِدًّا itself never changes: جَمِيلَة جِدًّا, "very beautiful" for a feminine noun. كَثِيرًا kathīran "a lot" goes after the verb and its object.
