English has three small words for this job: the, a and some. Spanish has the same three, but each one has four forms, because it agrees with the noun in gender and number.

the: el, la, los, las

These are the definite articles, and you already know them from the last lesson: el and los for masculine nouns, la and las for feminine ones.

a and some: un, una, unos, unas

These are the indefinite articles. English a never changes. Spanish un goes with masculine nouns and una with feminine ones. In the plural, unos and unas mean "some" or "a few".

MasculineFeminine
the (one)el tacola tortilla
the (more)los tacoslas tortillas
aun tacouna tortilla
someunos tacosunas tortillas

Note that "a" is un, not uno, in front of a noun. Uno is the number you count with.

el agua: a feminine word with el

Agua (water) is feminine, but it starts with a stressed a sound, and la agua would run two a sounds together. So in the singular it takes el: el agua. It is still feminine, as you will see when adjectives come along.

Things in general keep the article

English drops the article when it talks about something in general: "I like coffee", "Tacos are great". Spanish keeps it. To say you like something, use the set phrase me gusta plus the noun with its article. A later lesson explains how gustar works.

{{ex:es.s0032}}

Ordering

In a café or taquería, a noun with its article plus por favor already makes a polite order. Ask for the check the same way.

{{ex:es.s0033}}
Most common mistake: leaving the article out, as English does. Me gusta café → Me gusta el café.
