English builds most questions with do: Do you speak Spanish? Where do you live? Spanish has no do. You keep the normal verb and either raise your voice or start with a question word.

Yes/no questions: raise your voice

Take a statement and let your voice rise at the end. In writing, open with ¿ and close with ?. The verb does not change, and its ending still matches the subject. Exclamations work the same way, with ¡ at the start and ! at the end: ¡Qué bien! (How nice!). The upside-down marks tell you early how to read the sentence.

The question words

SpanishEnglish
¿qué?what?
¿dónde?where?
¿cuándo?when?
¿cómo?how?
¿quién? ¿quiénes?who?
¿cuánto? ¿cuánta? ¿cuántos? ¿cuántas?how much? how many?
¿por qué?why?

Every question word carries an accent mark. Without it, several become other words: que means "that", como means "I eat". In speech the question word is stressed, so the accent mark also shows you where your voice goes.

The verb comes right after the question word

English says "Where does Ana work?" with the subject in the middle. Spanish puts the verb straight after the question word, and the subject, if you say it, after the verb.

{{ex:es.s0081}}{{ex:es.s0082}}

quién and cuánto change their form

English "who" and "how many" never change. Spanish uses quiénes when you expect more than one person. Cuánto agrees with the noun after it, like an adjective: cuántos libros (masculine plural), cuántas horas (feminine plural).

{{ex:es.s0083}}{{ex:es.s0084}}

¿Por qué? and porque

¿Por qué?, two words with an accent mark, asks "why?". The answer often starts with porque, one word and no accent mark, which means "because". You will practice porque in a later lesson.

{{ex:es.s0085}}
English speakers keep the English word order. ¿Dónde usted vive? → ¿Dónde vive usted? In most of Latin America it is question word, then verb, then subject. (Caribbean speech often puts a pronoun first; you will hear that in the last lesson.)
