Everything you have learned works in the Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico and Cuba. What changes most is the sound: Caribbean Spanish is fast, and it softens or drops sounds at the end of syllables. This lesson trains your ear. Keep speaking and writing the standard forms you know; you will be understood everywhere.

The disappearing s

At the end of a syllable, s often turns into a soft breath, like English "h", or disappears. Los niños can sound like "lo niño", and ¿Cómo estás? like "¿Cómo 'tá?". The s is still there in writing and in the grammar, so use the article and the verb to tell that something is plural.

-ado becomes -ao

In relaxed speech, the d in -ado softens until it is gone: cansado sounds like "cansao". You will hear this in other countries too, but it is very strong in the Caribbean.

{{ex:es.s0203}}

r at the end of a syllable

Regional (Dominican): in Santo Domingo, an r at the end of a syllable often sounds like l, so porque sounds like "polque". In the Cibao region in the north, it can become an "ee" sound: "poique".

{{ex:es.s0204}}

tú before the verb in questions

Regional (Dominican): textbook Spanish says ¿Cómo estás? or ¿Cómo estás tú?, with any pronoun after the verb. Dominicans often put tú before the verb. The verb still agrees with tú, so it's written quieres with an s, even when you don't hear it.

When you speak, standard Spanish is always right. If you use a local word, a Dominican friend will smile and understand.

Don't write what you hear. In writing and in typed answers, use the standard spelling: Estoy cansao → Estoy cansado.
