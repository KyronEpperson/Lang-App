English adjectives never change and always come first: "the red shirt", "the red shoes". Spanish adjectives do two things differently. They usually come after the noun, and they change their ending to agree with it.

After the noun

Say the thing first, then describe it: la camisa roja, literally "the shirt red". Putting the color first, as in English, sounds wrong to a Spanish speaker.

Four forms

An adjective ending in -o has four forms. It takes -o or -a to match the gender of the noun, and adds -s when the noun is plural. Check the noun first, then pick the ending that matches it. Usually the adjective copies the ending of the noun, but follow the gender, not the letters: el mapa is masculine, so it is el mapa rojo.

Two forms

Adjectives ending in -e or a consonant are the same for masculine and feminine. They only change for the plural: -s after -e, -es after a consonant.

After ‹ser›

The adjective agrees even when it comes after the verb. It looks back at the subject: one woman is alta, two men are altos. A mixed group takes the masculine plural, just like nouns do.

Colors

Colors are adjectives too, so they follow every rule above: rojo (red), blanco (white), negro (black), amarillo (yellow), verde (green), azul (blue), gris (gray). In a store you will describe clothes all the time, so practice saying the noun and its color together as one unit. Other handy words for shopping: caro (expensive) and barato (cheap).

{{ex:es.s0048}}
Most common mistake: keeping the adjective in its masculine form. la camisa rojo → la camisa roja, and never la roja camisa.
