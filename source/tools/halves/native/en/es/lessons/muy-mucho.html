English uses "very" before describing words, and "a lot (of)" or "much" with nouns and verbs. Spanish splits the same way, with muy and mucho. The trouble is that some things English says with "very" are nouns in Spanish.

‹muy›: very, before a describing word

muy goes right before an adjective, or before a word like bien or cerca. It never changes form.

‹mucho›: a lot of, before a noun

Before a noun, mucho means "a lot of", "much" or "many". Here it works like an adjective: it agrees with the noun in gender and number, so it has four forms.

NounA lot of…
el trabajomucho trabajo
la gentemucha gente
los amigosmuchos amigos
las clasesmuchas clases

The trap: very cold is ‹mucho frío›

English says "I'm very hungry" and "It's very cold". Spanish says these with tener and hacer plus a noun: tengo hambre is literally "I have hunger", and hace frío is "it makes cold". A noun can't take muy, so you use mucho, and it agrees with the noun. Hambre and sed are feminine, so you say mucha hambre and mucha sed. Frío, calor and miedo are masculine, so you say mucho frío.

‹mucho› after a verb: a lot

After a verb, mucho means "a lot" and never changes, because it describes the action, not a noun. It can also stand alone as a short answer, where English says "a lot" or "very much". Muy can never stand alone.

A quick test

Look at the word that comes next. If it's a describing word (grande, cansado, bien), use muy. If it's a noun (frío, hambre, gente), use mucho in the form that agrees with it. If it's a verb, or nothing at all, use plain mucho.

The classic mistake: Tengo muy hambre → Tengo mucha hambre. Watch for the reverse too: Es mucho grande → Es muy grande.
