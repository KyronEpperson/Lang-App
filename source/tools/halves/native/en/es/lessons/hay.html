English has two forms: "there is" for one thing and "there are" for more. Spanish has one word for both: hay. It sounds just like English "I", and it never changes. You will use it all the time to describe a home, a street or a menu.

{{ex:es.s0063}}{{ex:es.s0064}}

What comes after hay

After hay comes something new: a noun with un, una, unos or unas, a number like dos (two) or tres (three), or a noun with no article at all. The article still agrees with its noun, even though hay does not.

SpanishEnglish
Hay una mesa.There's a table.
Hay unas sillas.There are some chairs.
Hay tres camas.There are three beds.
Hay agua.There's water.
{{ex:es.s0065}}

Questions and no

To ask, just raise your voice: ¿Hay…? To say there isn't any, put no in front: No hay…. English adds "any"; Spanish needs nothing extra.

hay or estar?

Both can answer a question about places, but they do different jobs. Hay says that something exists somewhere: you are introducing it. Estar says where a particular thing is, one you have already mentioned or both know about. That is why hay goes with un, una or a number, and estar goes with el or la.

Introducing it: hayLocating it: estar
Hay un banco cerca.El banco está cerca.
There's a bank nearby.The bank is nearby.
{{ex:es.s0068}}

A typical exchange uses both: first ¿Hay una farmacia cerca?, then, once you know there is one, ¿Dónde está la farmacia?

Most common mistake: putting el or la after hay, as English does after "there's the…". Hay el banco cerca → Hay un banco cerca, or El banco está cerca.
