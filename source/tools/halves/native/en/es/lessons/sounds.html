Spanish spelling is very regular, so a few rules let you read almost anything. Under every Spanish phrase in this course there is a pronunciation line in English letters. The syllable in CAPITALS is the stressed one.

The alphabet has 27 letters: the 26 you know, plus ñ. These ten sound much as in English (k and w turn up only in borrowed words). The rest of this lesson is about the others. Tap a letter to hear it.

Five short, pure vowels

English vowels slide: say no slowly and your lips end on a w. Spanish vowels stay put. Each is short and keeps one sound, stressed or not. The pronunciation line always spells them ah, eh, ee, oh, oo, with no glide.

Where the stress goes

Spanish stress follows one rule. A word ending in a vowel, n or s is stressed on the next-to-last syllable. Any other ending: the last syllable. A written accent mark (á é í ó ú) overrides both. Careful with words that look English: hospital and animal are stressed at the end.

The mark changes only the stress, never the sound: á is still "ah". A few short words use it to tell two words apart: sí (yes) and si (if), tú (you) and tu (your). Two dots on ü, after ‹g›, mean the ‹u› is said: pingüino. Questions and exclamations also open with a mark, upside down: ¿Cómo estás?, ¡Hola!

Tap and trill

A single r between vowels is a tap: one quick flick of the tongue, like the tt in American butter. Double rr, and r at the start of a word, is a trill (a rolled r). It changes the word: pero means "but", perro means "dog". The pronunciation line writes the tap as r and the trill as ‹rr›.

Read whole words

Now put it together. Read a word in two steps. First give each letter or spelling its Spanish sound: every vowel short and clear, h silent, j a breathy h. Then place the stress: an accent mark shows it; with no mark, a word ending in a vowel, n or s is stressed on the next-to-last syllable, and any other ending on the last. So trabajo is trah-BAH-hoh, and feliz is feh-LEES. Each card's note names the spelling and the stress rule. Say the word aloud before you flip it.

{{ex:es.s0011}}

‹el› and ‹la›

Nouns here come with a small word in front: el perro, la llave. Both mean "the". Every noun is masculine or feminine (lesson 3, on gender, explains why), so learn each one with its el or la. For more than one: los perros, las llaves.

Use the crutch, then drop it

The line cannot show everything: d between vowels is soft, close to the th in this, and p, t, k have no puff of air. Listen to the audio first, and hide the line once the spelling feels natural.

{{ex:es.s0012}}
Most common mistake: saying the h the English way. hola with a breathy h → hola starting straight on the vowel. The breathy sound belongs to j, never to h.
