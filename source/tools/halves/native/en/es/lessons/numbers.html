Spanish numbers work much like English ones, with a few twists: some are written as one word, some agree with their noun, and the time uses la and las.

From 0 to 100

NumberSpanish
0 to 10cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez
11 to 15once, doce, trece, catorce, quince
16 to 29dieciséis, diecisiete… veinte, veintiuno, veintidós… veintinueve
30 to 90treinta, cuarenta, cincuenta, sesenta, setenta, ochenta, noventa
100cien

From 16 to 29 the number is one word: veinticinco. From 31 on, you join the tens and the units with y (and): treinta y dos. Before a noun, uno becomes un or una: un libro, una persona.

cien, ciento and the hundreds

Exactly 100 is cien. Before another number it becomes ciento: ciento veinte (120). From 200 to 900 the hundreds agree with the noun, so they have a masculine and a feminine form: doscientos pesos, doscientas personas. Watch quinientos (500). 1,000 is mil.

Telling the time

English says "It's three o'clock". Spanish says "they are the three": Son las tres. The word horas (hours) is understood, so the article is feminine las, and the verb is plural son. Only one o'clock is singular: Es la una. Add y media for half past and y cuarto for a quarter past. For "at" a time, use a las.

Prices

To ask a price, say ¿Cuánto cuesta? for one thing and ¿Cuánto cuestan? for more than one: the verb agrees with the thing. In Mexico prices are in pesos; in the US, in dólares.
