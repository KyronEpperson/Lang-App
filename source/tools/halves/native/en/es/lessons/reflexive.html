English says "I get up", "I get dressed", "I shower", with nothing extra. Spanish describes many daily actions as things you do to yourself: me levanto is literally "I raise myself". These are reflexive verbs, and the small word in front is a reflexive pronoun.

The pronoun matches the subject

The infinitive of a reflexive verb ends in -se: levantarse (to get up). When you conjugate it, the se moves to the front and changes with the person, just like the verb ending.

‹levantarse›
yome levanto
túte levantas
él, ella, ustedse levanta
nosotrosnos levantamos
ellos, ellas, ustedesse levantan

The pronoun goes right before the conjugated verb, never after it.

A routine in Spanish

Common reflexive verbs for the day: despertarse (to wake up), levantarse, bañarse (to shower, in Latin America), vestirse (to get dressed), lavarse (to wash), acostarse (to go to bed). Some are also stem-changing verbs: me despierto, me visto, me acuesto.

Your name is a reflexive verb

You already know one: me llamo means "I call myself". That is why the question is ¿Cómo te llamas? to a friend and ¿Cómo se llama usted? to a stranger.

{{ex:es.s0131}}

Myself or something else?

The pronoun shows that the action comes back to the subject. If you wash something else, drop it: Lavo el carro (I wash the car), but Me lavo las manos (I wash my hands). With body parts, Spanish uses los or las, not "my".

{{ex:es.s0132}}
English has no pronoun here, so it is easy to drop. Levanto a las siete → Me levanto a las siete. ¿Cómo llamas? → ¿Cómo te llamas?
