Spanish has a second verb for "to be": estar. Its two main jobs are saying where someone or something is, and how someone is right now. When you ask for the bathroom or say you are tired, you need estar, not ser.

The forms

Subject‹estar›English
yoestoyI am
túestásyou are (a friend)
él, ella, ustedestáhe is, she is, you are (a stranger)
nosotros, nosotrasestamoswe are
ellos, ellas, ustedesestánthey are, you all are

Watch the accent marks. Está (is) and esta (this) are different words, and the accent shows the stress on the last syllable.

Where: location

English uses "is" for location; Spanish uses estar. To ask where something is, say ¿Dónde está…? For more than one thing, ¿Dónde están…? Short answers you will hear: aquí (here), allí (there), cerca (close) and lejos (far).

This holds even when the place never moves. A city stays where it is, and it still takes estar.

How: the way someone is right now

You already know ¿Cómo estás? The answer uses estar too: Estoy bien, "I'm fine", or Estoy mal, "I'm not well". With an adjective, the adjective agrees with the person, as in the last lesson: a man says Estoy cansado, a woman says Estoy cansada. The same goes for health: Estoy enfermo or Estoy enferma, "I'm sick".

Asking a stranger

To a stranger, start with Disculpe and use the usted form when you talk about them: ¿Está usted bien? When you ask about a place, the verb agrees with the place, not with the person you are talking to.

{{ex:es.s0055}}
Most common mistake: using ser because English uses "is". ¿Dónde es el baño? → ¿Dónde está el baño?
