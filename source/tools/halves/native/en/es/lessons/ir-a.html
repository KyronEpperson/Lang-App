English talks about plans with "going to": "I'm going to travel." Spanish has the same tool, built the same way: a form of ir, then a, then an infinitive. You already know the forms of ir.

Who‹ir a› + infinitive
yovoy a viajar
túvas a viajar
él, ella, ustedva a viajar
nosotrosvamos a viajar
ellos, ellas, ustedesvan a viajar

Three parts, every time

Only ir changes: it agrees with the subject. The infinitive at the end never changes. And the little a is never dropped, even in fast speech, where English shrinks "going to" into "gonna".

A place or a plan

You have seen ir a before, with a place: Voy a Cancún means "I'm going to Cancún", right now or soon. Put an infinitive after a instead and it becomes a plan. The two can even meet in one sentence, and a + el still becomes al, as always.

{{ex:es.s0172}}

When: time words

Plans need a time. Useful ones: mañana (tomorrow), esta noche (tonight), el sábado (on Saturday, with no word for "on") and la próxima semana (next week). próximo agrees with its noun: la próxima semana, but el próximo año.

{{ex:es.s0173}}

Questions and ‹no›

To ask, just raise your voice at the end. To say no, put no before the form of ir. Question words such as qué, cuándo and dónde come first, as always, and the rest of the sentence keeps its usual order.

{{ex:es.s0174}}{{ex:es.s0175}}

‹Vamos a›: let's

Vamos a + infinitive also means "let's…". The situation tells you which meaning is meant.

{{ex:es.s0176}}
The most common mistake is dropping the a: Voy comer → Voy a comer. The second is using one form of ‹ir› for everyone: Nosotros va a viajar → Nosotros vamos a viajar.
