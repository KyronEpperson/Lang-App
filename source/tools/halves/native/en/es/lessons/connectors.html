A few small words turn short sentences into real conversation. Four of them do most of the work: y (and), o (or), pero (but) and porque (because).

y and o, and how they change

y means "and" and o means "or", just as in English. When y joins two people, the verb takes the ellos form, because together they are "they". In a list, y goes only before the last item, as in English: tacos, arroz y frijoles.

{{ex:es.s0151}}{{ex:es.s0152}}

One twist English doesn't have: before a word that starts with an "ee" sound (spelled i- or hi-), y becomes e. Before a word that starts with an "oh" sound (o- or ho-), o becomes u. This keeps the two sounds from running together.

{{ex:es.s0153}}{{ex:es.s0154}}

pero: but

pero works like English "but". It joins two sentences, or two describing words that agree with the same noun: cansada pero contenta. Say it with a quick tap, not a trill: perro is "dog".

{{ex:es.s0155}}

You will also hear sino, another "but", after a no: No es mexicano, sino colombiano (He isn't Mexican, he's Colombian). Just recognize it for now.

porque and ¿por qué?

English has two different words, "why" and "because". Spanish uses almost the same word for both. ¿Por qué?, two words with an accent mark and the stress on qué, asks "why?". porque, one word with no accent and the stress on por, answers "because".

{{ex:es.s0156}}

también and tampoco

también means "also" or "too" and agrees with a yes. tampoco means "not either" or "neither" and agrees with a no. Never say también no: tampoco already carries the "not".

{{ex:es.s0157}}{{ex:es.s0158}}
The most common slip is in writing: ¿Porque estudias español? → ¿Por qué estudias español? In speech, watch for A mí también no → A mí tampoco.
