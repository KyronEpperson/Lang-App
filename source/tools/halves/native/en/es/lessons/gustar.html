In English, the person who likes is the subject: I like tacos. Spanish turns the sentence around: Me gustan los tacos means "tacos are pleasing to me". The thing you like is the subject, so the verb agrees with the thing, not with you.

‹gusta› or ‹gustan›

Use gusta for one thing or for an activity (an infinitive). Use gustan for more than one thing. Keep the article: things in general take el, la, los or las.

Who likes it: ‹me›, ‹te›, ‹le›, ‹nos›, ‹les›

The person who likes is shown by a small word before the verb, an indirect object pronoun. It means "to me", "to you" and so on.

Who likesSpanish
I likeme gusta, me gustan
you like (a friend)te gusta, te gustan
he, she likes; you like (‹usted›)le gusta, le gustan
we likenos gusta, nos gustan
they like; you all likeles gusta, les gustan

To name the person, add a before the name and keep the pronoun: A Ana le gusta… You can also add a mí, a ti for emphasis: A mí me gusta.

Me too, me neither

To agree with someone's like, say A mí también (me too). To agree with a dislike, a sentence with no, say A mí tampoco (me neither).

{{ex:es.s0123}}

‹encantar› and ‹doler› work the same way

Me encanta means "I love" (a thing or an activity). Me duele means "it hurts me". Both agree with the thing: me duelen los pies. With body parts, Spanish uses the article, not "my": la cabeza, los pies.

Two classic mistakes: Yo gusto la pizza → Me gusta la pizza, and Me gusta los tacos → Me gustan los tacos. Look at the thing: one or more?
