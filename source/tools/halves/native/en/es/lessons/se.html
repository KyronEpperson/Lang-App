English often uses "you", "they" or "people" for no one in particular: "How do you say this?", "They sell tortillas here", "You can't come in." Spanish usually says this with se and the él form of the verb, and no person is named. This is the impersonal se. It feels strange at first, but the idea is simple: something is done, and who does it doesn't matter.

se + verb: no one in particular

Put se right before the verb. With no, the order is no se + verb.

One thing or several: the verb agrees

When the thing after the verb is plural, the verb goes plural too: one house is se vende, several houses are se venden. You will see this on signs everywhere, from markets to apartment buildings.

OneSeveral
Se vende una casa.Se venden casas.
Se renta un cuarto.Se rentan cuartos.

Don't translate "you" and "they" word for word

When English "you" or "they" means people in general, don't reach for usted or ellos. ¿Cómo dice usted…? asks how one particular person says it, and ellos points at a particular group. In everyday speech you'll also hear a general tú, as in English, but se is the form to learn first, and the one on every sign.

{{ex:es.s0200}}

At work and at school

The same pattern explains how things are done: Aquí se trabaja mucho (People work hard here), ¿Cómo se escribe tu nombre? (How do you spell your name?). At a new job or in a class you will hear questions like these all day, so learn to spot se right before a verb. The verb stays in the él form unless a plural noun follows.

The common mistake is keeping the verb singular with a plural noun: Se vende casas → Se venden casas. You will see the first on handmade signs, but standard written Spanish wants the plural.
