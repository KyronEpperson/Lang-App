English has one set of numbers. Taigi has two: a colloquial set for counting things and saying amounts, and an older, literary set for reading out digits one by one, as in a phone number. The characters are the same; only the reading changes. This lesson teaches the counting set first, then the digits.

Bigger numbers

Bigger numbers need no new words below a hundred. Build them the way you read them: 十二 tsa̍p-jī is "ten two", 三十 sann-tsa̍p is "three ten". The big units are 百 pah hundred, 千 tshing thousand and 萬 bān ten thousand. In Tâi-lô a whole number is one word, joined by hyphens.

Two of something: 兩

When you count people or things, the number is followed by a classifier, a small word that comes before the noun (lesson 15 has the full set; 个 ê is the general one). Before a classifier, "two" is always 兩 nn̄g, never 二. 二 is for numbers such as 十二 and for digits.

Age

幾 kuí asks "how many" when the answer is a small number, and 歲 huè is "years old". Like every question word, 幾 sits where the number will go in the answer: 你幾歲？ is answered 我三十歲。 There is no verb "to be" in either sentence.

Phone numbers: the literary digits

Read a phone number digit by digit, in the literary readings. 空 khòng is zero. The number below starts khòng-kiú-pat-sam. Compare the counting readings of the same digits: 九 káu, 八 peh, 三 sann. Every digit of a phone number takes the literary reading, and each group is written as one word in Tâi-lô.

Use the counting set for amounts, ages and prices; use the literary digits only when you read out a string of digits.

Most common mistake: 二 before a classifier. 二个人 → 兩个人.
