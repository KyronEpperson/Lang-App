At a night market or a morning market you will ask prices all day. You need one question, one money word, and a few words for cheap and dear.

How much is it?

English says "How much is it?". Taigi says 偌濟錢？ Guā-tsē tsînn?, literally "how-much money?". Name the thing first, then ask. To get the stall owner's attention, start with 頭家 thâu-ke, "boss".

Notice the classifiers: a bowl of noodles is 一碗麵, and bananas are sold by weight, 一斤 (a catty, about 600 grams).

Dollars: 箍

In speech, a Taiwan dollar is 箍 khoo. It comes after the number, like a classifier. Use the counting numbers from lesson 12, never the literary digits.

{{ex:nan.s0117}}{{ex:nan.s0118}}

Cheap and expensive: no 是

貴 kuì is expensive and 俗 sio̍k is cheap. English says "it is expensive". Taigi adjectives need no 是: put a word like 真 (very) or 遮 (so) in front instead, or nothing at all when you compare with 較 (more).

{{ex:nan.s0119}}{{ex:nan.s0120}}

Too much: 傷

傷 siunn means "too", and it goes right before the adjective. You will hear it about prices and about food.

{{ex:nan.s0121}}{{ex:nan.s0122}}

莫 in 莫買啦 means "don't!"; it is one of the four negatives of lesson 26. 啦 at the end makes the advice friendlier.

Cheaper, dearer: 較

較 khah before an adjective means "more": 較俗 is "cheaper" and 較貴 is "more expensive". English adds "-er" or "more" and usually "than"; in Taigi, when it is clear what you compare with, 較 + adjective is the whole sentence, as in the fruit-stall example above. Comparing two things with 比 comes in lesson 24.

Putting it together

A typical exchange: you point and ask 偌濟錢？, the owner answers with a number and 箍, and you answer 好 (fine) or 傷貴. Numbers before 箍 are ordinary counting numbers: 三十五箍, 三百箍, 一百箍.

Most common mistake: putting 是 before an adjective. 這是貴 → 這真貴.
