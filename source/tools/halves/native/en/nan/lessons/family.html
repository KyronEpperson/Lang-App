Many learners come to Taigi to talk with their grandparents, so these are words you will use every day. Most of them start with the friendly prefix 阿, which you will also see in front of names, as in 阿美仔. Family words are also how you address people: you call your grandmother 阿媽, not by her name, and you call your older brother 阿兄.

Parents and grandparents

TaigiTâi-lôEnglish
阿爸a-pahdad
阿母a-búmom
阿公a-konggrandpa
阿媽a-mágrandma
外公guā-konggrandpa on your mother's side
外媽guā-mágrandma on your mother's side

阿公 and 阿媽 work for both sides of the family; 外公 and 外媽 make clear you mean your mother's parents. Watch 阿母 (mom) and 阿媽 (grandma): one character apart.

Brothers, sisters and children

English says "brother" and "sister" and adds "older" or "younger" only when it matters. Taigi always says which: 阿兄 a-hiann is an older brother, 阿姊 a-tsí an older sister, 小弟 sió-tī a younger brother. Your child is 囝 kiánn, and a son is 後生 hāu-senn.

Uncles and aunts, and older people

阿伯 a-peh is your father's older brother and 阿姨 a-î your mother's sister. Both are also the polite way to address an older man or woman you do not know, at the market or next door.

Husband and wife

翁某 ang-bóo is "husband and wife", a couple. 牽手 khan-tshiú, literally "hold hands", is a Taiwanese word for "wife" (Wiktionary).

My, your, his: 阮, 恁, 𪜶

With family words, Taigi often uses the plural pronouns where English says "my", "your" and "his": 阮 guán (we, not you), 恁 lín (you all) and 𪜶 in (they). So "my big brother" is 阮大兄, and "your little brother" is 恁小弟. You can also say 我的 (my), with 的 linking the owner to the person.

Counting people in the family

People take the classifier 个, and two is 兩, as in lesson 12. The same pattern works after 𪜶: 𪜶兩个 is "the two of them", as in the example above.
