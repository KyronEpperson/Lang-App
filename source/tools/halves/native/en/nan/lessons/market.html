The traditional morning market, 菜市仔 tshài-tshī-á, sells food by the piece and by weight, and the stall owners speak Taigi. This lesson gives you the food words and the price question.

What is on the stalls

菜 tshài is vegetables, 肉 bah is meat, 魚 hî is fish and 水果 tsuí-kó is fruit; 弓蕉 king-tsio is a banana. An egg is 卵 nn̄g, and a chicken egg 雞卵 ke-nn̄g.

A sound trap: 卵 (egg) and 兩 (two) are both nn̄g, with the same tone. Only the context tells them apart.

{{ex:nan.s0161}}

Eggs are counted with 粒 lia̍p, a classifier for round things (Bite-size Taiwanese: "measure word for round objects"). Buy is 買 bé. Like every counted noun, eggs need the number, then the classifier, then the noun, and two is 兩, never 二. So when you hear nn̄g lia̍p ke-nn̄g, the first nn̄g is the number and the last one is the egg.

By the catty

Taiwanese markets weigh in 斤 kin, a catty (a Taiwan catty is 600 grams). 斤 works like a classifier: number + 斤 + food.

{{ex:nan.s0162}}

How much?

You met 偌濟錢 guā-tsē tsînn (how much money) in the prices lesson. At the market the food comes first, then the amount, then the question. English asks "How much is a catty of bananas?"; Taigi says "bananas, one catty, how much money?".

{{ex:nan.s0163}}

Cheap and expensive

俗 sio̍k is cheap and 貴 kuì is expensive. Both are adjectives, so there is no 是 in front. 較 khah in front means "more": 較俗, cheaper. If it is clear what you compare with, you do not need a word for "than".

{{ex:nan.s0120}}{{ex:nan.s0164}}

彼擔 hit tànn is "that stall": 彼 (that) needs its classifier, like 這 and like numbers. And 莫 tells someone not to do something.

{{ex:nan.s0165}}
Most common mistake: putting 是 before cheap or expensive. 雞卵是俗 → 雞卵真俗. Use a word for "very", or 較, never 是.
