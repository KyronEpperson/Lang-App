English uses one set of numbers for everything. Tagalog uses two: Spanish numbers for the clock, native Tagalog numbers for counting how long.

The clock: alas- + Spanish number

To ask the time, say Anong oras na? (literally "What hour already?"). The answer is alas- plus the Spanish number: alas-dos, alas-tres, up to alas-dose. One o'clock is the exception: ala-una, with no s. Half past is y medya.

Add the part of the day with ng: ng umaga (in the morning), ng hapon (in the afternoon), ng gabi (in the evening).

How long: native number + linker

For a length of time, count with native numbers, linked to the noun as always: tatlong oras (three hours), limang minuto (five minutes), dalawang araw (two days). So alas-tres is a time on the clock, and tatlong oras is three hours long.

Days of the week

The days come from Spanish and are written with a capital: Lunes, Martes, Miyerkoles, Huwebes, Biyernes, Sabado, Linggo. "On Monday" is sa Lunes, with the sa marker, the same one you use for places.

{{ex:tl.s0174}}

Yesterday, today, tomorrow

kahapon (yesterday), kanina (earlier today), ngayon (now, today), mamaya (later today), bukas (tomorrow). The verb's aspect has to agree: completed with kahapon and kanina, future with mamaya and bukas. Careful: mamaya looks like "maybe", but it means "later today".

Most common mistake: using a Spanish number for a length of time, or a native one on the clock. tres oras → tatlong oras (three hours); alas-tatlo → alas-tres (three o'clock).
