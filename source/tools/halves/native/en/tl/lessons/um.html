English verbs mostly change at the end: "eat, ate". Tagalog verbs are built from a root word plus a piece added to it. The first kind you learn adds -um-, an infix: a piece put inside the word.

Making an -um- verb

Put -um- right before the first vowel of the root. If the root starts with a vowel, um- simply goes in front.

Root-um- verbEnglish
kainkumainate
bilibumilibought
puntapumuntawent
sayawsumayawdanced
inomuminomdrank

In this lesson the verb means it already happened: kumain is "ate". The next lessons add "is eating" and "will eat".

The verb comes first

As with describing words, the verb opens the sentence, and the doer follows it.

Who did it: actor focus

An -um- verb puts the spotlight on the doer. That is called actor focus: the doer is the topic, so it takes ang. A pronoun comes in the ang form (ako, siya, kami), and a name takes si. English speakers often reach for ko here, because it looks like "I" after a verb; with an -um- verb it is always ako.

{{ex:tl.s0132}}

What they ate or bought: ng

The thing (what was eaten, drunk or bought) is not the topic, so it takes ng. Only one noun per sentence gets ang, and here it is the doer.

{{ex:tl.s0008}}{{ex:tl.s0133}}

Where they went: sa

A place takes sa, as always.

{{ex:tl.s0134}}

To say it did not happen, put hindi first; the pronoun moves up after it.

{{ex:tl.s0135}}
Most common mistake: giving the doer the ng form, or giving the thing ang as well. Kumain ko ng mangga → Kumain ako ng mangga; Kumain ako ang mangga → Kumain ako ng mangga.
