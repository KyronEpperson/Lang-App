Food is at the center of Filipino visits, and the first questions you hear are about eating, not about how you are.

Eating together

English asks "How are you?"; Filipinos often ask "Have you eaten?" It is a friendly greeting, not an invitation you must accept. When someone starts eating, they say Kain tayo! (Let's eat!) to anyone nearby. When you can't eat more, say Busog na ako.

Rice and ulam

A meal is kanin (cooked rice) plus ulam, the dish you eat with it: adobo, sinigang, fish, vegetables. English has no word for ulam; "What's for dinner?" is often Ano ang ulam?

Ordering: number + linker + dish + po

At a carinderia (a small eatery) or a restaurant, say the number, the linker, the dish, and po to the staff. nga softens the order, like "please". "One more" is isa pa, and before a noun the linker moves onto pa: isa pang kanin.

At the sari-sari store

The sari-sari store is the little neighborhood shop. Call out Pabili po! ("I'd like to buy, please!"), then say what you want with ng.

{{ex:tl.s0167}}

Prices

Ask Magkano po? or, for the whole bill, Magkano po lahat? Up to about a hundred, prices are usually said with Spanish numbers (bente pesos, singkwenta pesos); native numbers (dalawampung piso) are correct too. From a hundred up, native numbers are normal: isang daan, limang daan.
