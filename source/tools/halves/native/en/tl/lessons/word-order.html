English almost always starts with the person or thing the sentence is about: "I am a teacher", "Ana is tired". Everyday Tagalog starts with the new information, the part that says something (the predicate), and puts the topic after it.

Predicate first

"I am a teacher" becomes "Teacher I": Guro ako. "Ana is tired" becomes Pagod si Ana. Notice there is no word for "am" or "is": Tagalog does not need one.

ay: topic first, more formal

You will also see the topic first, followed by ay: Ako ay guro. It means exactly the same. This order is common in writing, speeches and when you contrast two people, but in conversation it sounds stiff if you use it all the time. ay is not "is": it only marks that the topic came first. After a vowel it is often shortened: Ako'y guro.

{{ex:tl.s0125}}

The little words go second

Tagalog has short words that cannot stand at the start or at the end of a sentence where English would put them. They go right after the first word, in a fixed order:

  • first the one-syllable pronouns ka, ko, mo
  • then na (already) or pa (still, yet)
  • then po
  • then ba
  • then the longer pronouns ako, siya, kayo, sila

hindi pulls the pronoun forward

When a sentence starts with hindi, the pronoun moves up right after it: Hindi ko alam, Hindi siya pagod.

{{ex:tl.s0129}}
Most common mistake: keeping the English order and putting the pronoun or ba where English puts it. Kumain na ba ikaw? → Kumain ka na ba?; Hindi gusto ko → Hindi ko gusto.
