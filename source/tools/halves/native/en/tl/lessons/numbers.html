Filipinos use two sets of numbers every day: native Tagalog numbers and numbers borrowed from Spanish. English has one set, so the trick is knowing which set goes with which job.

Native numbers 1 to 10

12345
isadalawatatloapatlima
678910
animpitowalosiyamsampu

Use these for counting things and people, for age and for how long something takes.

A number needs the linker

English puts the number straight before the noun: "two dogs". Tagalog joins them with the linker, exactly as with a describing word. After a vowel, add -ng to the number; after a consonant, put na in between.

{{ex:tl.s0089}}{{ex:tl.s0090}}{{ex:tl.s0064}}{{ex:tl.s0091}}

The noun itself does not change: there is no plural ending like English -s. The number already says "more than one".

Bigger numbers

11 to 19 start with labing- or labin-: labing-isa (11), labindalawa (12). The tens end in -pu: dalawampu (20), tatlumpu (30). You will hear these less often than the Spanish ones in shops, but you will see them written. Hundreds are daan and thousands are libo, with a linked number in front.

{{ex:tl.s0092}}{{ex:tl.s0093}}{{ex:tl.s0094}}

Spanish numbers for prices and the clock

For small prices, people usually say the Spanish number with pesos: bente (20), trenta (30), singkwenta (50). The native form is correct too, just less common in a shop. Clock time always uses Spanish numbers (alas-tres, three o'clock), which comes in a later lesson. So tatlong oras is "three hours", but tres oras is not Tagalog for it.

{{ex:tl.s0095}}{{ex:tl.s0096}}{{ex:tl.s0097}}

Phone numbers and years are usually said in English, so you only need to recognize them.

Most common mistake: leaving out the linker after a number. dalawa bahay → dalawang bahay; apat aso → apat na aso.
