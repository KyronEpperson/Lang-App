The second big family of Tagalog verbs adds mag- to the front of the root instead of putting -um- inside. Many everyday actions at home, at work and at school are mag- verbs: cooking, playing, working, studying and cleaning.

mag- for the action, nag- for "did it"

English adds -ed to show the action is finished: "cook, cooked". Tagalog changes the m of mag- to n: magluto, nagluto. Learn new verbs of this kind in pairs, the mag- form and the nag- form together.

The hyphen before a vowel

When the root starts with a vowel, write a hyphen: mag-aral, nag-aral. The hyphen stands for the catch, the small break before aral. Without it, the letters would be read "ma-ga-ral". The hyphen is part of the spelling, so type it in your answers too: you will meet it every time mag- or nag- comes before a vowel.

Actor focus again

mag- verbs work exactly like -um- verbs: actor focus. The doer is the topic, so it takes ang (ako, si Lola, ang mga bata); the thing takes ng; a place takes sa.

Learn each verb with its own piece

Each root has its usual piece: kumain (not nagkain), nagluto (not lumuto). A few roots take both pieces, sometimes with a small difference in meaning, so learn every verb whole, as this course teaches it.

Most common mistake: dropping the hyphen or giving the doer the ng form. nagaral → nag-aral; Nagluto ko ng adobo → Nagluto ako ng adobo.
