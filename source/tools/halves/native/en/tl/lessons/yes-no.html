English uses "no" and "not" for almost everything. Tagalog has three different words, and each one has its own job: hindi, wala and huwag. Picking the right one is the real trap, so this lesson takes them one at a time.

Yes: oo and opo

To a friend, a sibling or a child, "yes" is oo. To anyone who gets po (an older person, a stranger, a shop assistant), say opo. Answering Lola with a plain oo sounds rude.

hindi: not

hindi means "not". It goes first, in front of a describing word, a noun or a verb, and a short pronoun such as ako, ka or siya moves up right after it. English says "I am not tired"; Tagalog says "not I tired". On its own, Hindi. is "No." To someone who gets po, say Hindi po.

{{ex:tl.s0081}}{{ex:tl.s0005}}

Words like alam (know) keep their ng pronoun after hindi: Hindi ko alam, never Hindi ako alam.

{{ex:tl.s0071}}

wala: there's none, I don't have

When the answer is "there isn't any" or "I don't have one", Tagalog does not use hindi. It uses wala. Someone asks May tubig ba? ("Is there water?") and there is none: the answer is Wala. To say who has none, add the pronoun with the linker -ng: wala + ako = Wala akong…

{{ex:tl.s0082}}{{ex:tl.s0083}}{{ex:tl.s0084}}

huwag: don't

For "don't" in an instruction, use huwag, never hindi. The person you are talking to comes next, joined by the linker: huwag kang… to a friend, huwag po kayong… to someone who gets po. Huwag! alone means "Don't!"

{{ex:tl.s0085}}{{ex:tl.s0086}}

Not yet: Hindi pa

The little word pa means "yet". When someone asks Kumain ka na ba? ("Have you eaten?"), a very common Filipino greeting, the answer may be Hindi pa. ("Not yet.").

{{ex:tl.s0087}}

Answering a "not" question

A question like "Aren't you hungry?" is easy to misread in any language, and a bare oo or hindi can leave people unsure what you mean. Play it safe: give the whole answer.

{{ex:tl.s0088}}
Most common mistake: using hindi for "there isn't any". May tubig ba? Hindi. → May tubig ba? Wala. And for "don't": Hindi ka umalis! → Huwag kang umalis!
