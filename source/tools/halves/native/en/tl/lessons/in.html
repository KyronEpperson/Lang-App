English changes the little word: "I ate a mango", "I ate the mango". Tagalog changes the verb. So far your verbs (-um-, mag-) made the doer the topic, marked by ang. That is actor focus. With an -in verb, the thing becomes the topic. That is object focus.

Two ways to say "I ate"

{{ex:tl.s0008}}{{ex:tl.s0156}}

In the first, ako (the ang form) is the topic and the mango takes ng: some mango, any mango. In the second, the mango takes ang, and the doer moves to the ng form: ko. Only one noun per sentence gets ang.

How the -in verb is built

The completed form puts -in- after the first consonant of the root: kain → kinain, bili → binili. A root that starts with l takes ni- in front instead: luto → niluto. A root that starts with a vowel takes in-: inom → ininom.

The ongoing and future forms repeat the first syllable of the root, as you learned in the lesson on aspect.

Who did it: the ng form

The doer of an -in verb takes the ng form: ko, mo, niya, or ni before a name. The thing takes ang.

Which one to choose

Look at the English. A particular thing ("the mango", "this fish", "Ana's bread") makes object focus natural: use the -in verb. "A mango", "some fish" or no thing at all: use the -um- or mag- verb.

{{ex:tl.s0161}}
Most common mistake: mixing the two patterns. Kumain ko ang mangga → Kinain ko ang mangga (or Kumain ako ng mangga for "a mango").
