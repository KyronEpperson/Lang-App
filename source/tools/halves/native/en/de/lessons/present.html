English verbs barely change: I live, you live, she lives. German verbs take a different ending for almost every person. The good news: most verbs follow one pattern.

Stem plus ending

Take the infinitive, the form in a word list (wohnen, to live), and drop -en. What is left is the stem: wohn-. Then add the ending that matches the subject.

SubjectEnding‹wohnen›
ich-ewohne
du-stwohnst
er, sie, es-twohnt
wir-enwohnen
ihr-twohnt
sie, Sie-enwohnen
{{ex:de.s0042}}{{ex:de.s0043}}

For Sie (formal you) and sie (they), the verb is simply the infinitive: Sie wohnen. A name works like er or sie: Lena wohnt. Two or more people work like sie (they): Lena und Max wohnen.

{{ex:de.s0044}}

You, to a group of friends

ihr is "you" for two or more people you would each call du: friends, family, classmates. Its ending is -t, the same as the ‹er› form. For a group of strangers, use Sie, as for one stranger.

{{ex:de.s0045}}

Two small adjustments

If the stem ends in -t or -d, an e slips in so you can say the ending: du arbeitest, er arbeitet, ihr arbeitet. Without it, arbeitst would be almost impossible to say, and er arbeitt would sound the same as the stem. If it ends in an s sound (s, ß, z), the ‹du› form adds only -t: du heißt, du tanzt.

{{ex:de.s0046}}

One tense, three English meanings

German has no "-ing" form. Ich lerne Deutsch means "I learn German", "I'm learning German", and, with a time word, "I'll learn German". Never build it with sein.

{{ex:de.s0047}}{{ex:de.s0048}}
Most common mistake: one verb form for every person. Er wohnen in Hamburg → Er wohnt in Hamburg. And no "am": Ich bin lerne Deutsch → Ich lerne Deutsch.
