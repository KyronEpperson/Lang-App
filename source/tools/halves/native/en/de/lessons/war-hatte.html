The Perfekt is the everyday past for most verbs. Two verbs are different: sein and haben. In speech, Germans usually use their simple past, war (was) and hatte (had), because it is shorter.

‹war›: was, were

English has two forms, was and were. German has a form for each person, but the endings are the ones you know. The ‹ich› form and the ‹er› form are the same, with no ending.

‹hatte›: had

hatte works the same way. Use it for anything you had: time, a cold, a meeting, a good day.

{{ex:de.s0197}}{{ex:de.s0198}}

One hatte is enough. Ich hatte Zeit means "I had time". Adding gehabt makes it "I had had time", which is a different tense.

The modal verbs

Modal verbs have a simple past too, and you will hear it often. It has no umlaut: konnte (could), musste (had to), wollte (wanted to). The infinitive still goes to the end.

{{ex:de.s0199}}{{ex:de.s0200}}

For other verbs, keep using the Perfekt in conversation: Ich war im Kino, but Ich habe einen Film gesehen. You can also say Ich bin müde gewesen. It is correct, just longer, so most people say Ich war müde.

In books and the news you will also see the simple past of many other verbs (ging, kam). In conversation people use the Perfekt for those, so you can leave them for later.

Telling a short story

A story about yesterday usually mixes both: war and hatte for how things were, the Perfekt for what happened. Questions work as usual: the verb comes first in a yes/no question and second after a question word.

{{ex:de.s0201}}

‹war› or ‹wahr›?

war (was) and wahr (true) sound the same. The ‹h› is only in the spelling: Das ist wahr means "That's true".

Most common mistake: doubling the past, as if ‹hatte› needed a participle. Ich hatte keine Zeit gehabt → Ich hatte keine Zeit.
