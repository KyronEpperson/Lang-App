English uses two words for place: in for where something is, and into for where something goes. German uses the same preposition for both and changes the case of the article instead.

Where? The dative

Nine prepositions work this way: in, an, auf, neben, vor, hinter, über, unter, zwischen (in, at, on, next to, in front of, behind, above, under, between). They are called two-way prepositions. When something simply is somewhere, they take the dative.

Where to? The accusative

When someone or something moves to a place, the same prepositions take the accusative. That is why you are im Kino but you go ins Kino.

{{ex:de.s0169}}{{ex:de.s0170}}

Going to a place in town

For "to" a shop, an office or a person, use zu, which always takes the dative: zum (‹zu dem›) for masculine and neuter places, zur (‹zu der›) for feminine ones. nach is only for cities, countries and nach Hause.

{{ex:de.s0171}}

Time: ‹am›, ‹im›, ‹um›

English says on Monday, in July, at eight. German says am for days and parts of the day, im for months and seasons, and um for clock times.

{{ex:de.s0172}}

‹seit› and ‹vor›

For something that started in the past and is still going on, German uses seit with the present tense, where English says "have been" or "have lived".

{{ex:de.s0173}}

vor with a time means "ago": vor einer Woche, a week ago. You will use it with the past tenses in the next lessons.

Waiting for something

Some verbs come with their own preposition. You wait auf something, not für it.

{{ex:de.s0174}}
Most common mistake: translating "for" word for word. Ich warte für den Bus → Ich warte auf den Bus.
