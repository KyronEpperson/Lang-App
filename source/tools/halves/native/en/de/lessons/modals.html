English puts the second verb right after can, must or want to: I can speak German. German modal verbs work the same way, with one big difference: the second verb goes to the very end.

The infinitive goes to the end

The modal verb is the conjugated verb, so it takes second place. The other verb stays in the infinitive and goes last, with everything else in between. This frame is the sentence bracket (‹Satzklammer›), and you'll use it again and again. Every modal works the same way, so once you know the pattern for one, you know it for all of them.

The ‹ich› and ‹er› rows are the same. kann, muss, will have no ending there at all (never er kannt), and möchte ends in ‹-e› in both. In the singular, können, müssen and wollen also change their vowel.

{{ex:de.s0125}}

‹will› means "want"

German will looks like English "will", but it means "want". For the future, German usually uses the present with a time word: Morgen arbeite ich. German does have a future with werden (Ich werde…), but you won't need it yet. When you ask for something, Ich will can sound blunt; Ich möchte is the polite choice in a shop, a café or an office.

{{ex:de.s0126}}{{ex:de.s0127}}

‹muss nicht›: "don't have to"

Ich muss nicht arbeiten means "I don't have to work", not "I must not work". "Must not" is darf nicht, from dürfen (be allowed to). For now you only need to recognize dürfen and sollen (be supposed to).
