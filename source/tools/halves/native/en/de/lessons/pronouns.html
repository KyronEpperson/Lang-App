English has two forms for most pronouns: I and me, he and him. German has three, one for each case you know: the nominative for the subject, the accusative for the direct object, and the dative for the indirect object and after dative prepositions.

The three forms

NominativeAccusativeDativeEnglish
ichmichmirI, me
dudichdiryou (a friend)

siesieihrshe, her

wirunsunswe, us

sie / Siesie / Sieihnen / Ihnenthey, them / you (formal)

Two forms need care. sie can mean she or they, and with a capital, Sie is the formal you; the verb ending tells you which. And ihr is both "you" to friends and "to her" in the dative: Ich helfe ihr means "I'm helping her".

Accusative: the direct object, and after für

Use the accusative form for the person the verb acts on, and after für (for) and ohne (without).

{{ex:de.s0157}}{{ex:de.s0158}}

Dative: after mit, and with a few verbs

After mit, bei, zu, von, aus and nach, use the dative form, just as with articles. A few common verbs also take the dative where English uses a plain object. The most useful is helfen (to help).

{{ex:de.s0159}}{{ex:de.s0160}}

When you give or show something to someone, the person is the indirect object, so it is dative too.

{{ex:de.s0161}}

How you feel: mir and dir

English says "I am cold". German says "to me it is cold": Mir ist kalt. The same pattern asks how someone is: literally, "how does it go for you?"

{{ex:de.s0162}}{{ex:de.s0163}}{{ex:de.s0164}}

Liking with gefallen

Das gefällt mir means "I like that", but word for word it says "that pleases me". The thing you like is the subject, and you are in the dative.

{{ex:de.s0165}}
Most common mistake: copying English "I am cold". Ich bin kalt → Mir ist kalt. Ich bin kalt describes your personality, not how you feel.
