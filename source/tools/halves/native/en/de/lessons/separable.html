English has verbs with a small extra word: get up, call up, come along. German has them too, but writes them as one word in the dictionary: aufstehen (get up), anrufen (call), mitkommen (come along), einkaufen (shop for groceries), fernsehen (watch TV).

The prefix goes to the end

In a statement or a question, the verb part takes its normal place (second, or first in a yes/no question), and the prefix goes to the very end. It's the sentence bracket again, with the prefix closing it.

{{ex:de.s0130}}{{ex:de.s0131}}{{ex:de.s0132}}{{ex:de.s0133}}{{ex:de.s0134}}

However long the sentence gets, the prefix waits until the very end. A dictionary lists the whole infinitive, so when you hear rufe … an, look it up under anrufen.

{{ex:de.s0135}}

The verb part keeps its usual endings and stem changes: fernsehen gives Lena sieht fern.

{{ex:de.s0136}}

Together after a modal

When a modal verb takes second place, the separable verb is the infinitive at the end, and it stays in one piece.

{{ex:de.s0137}}

Questions work the same way: the modal moves forward, and the whole infinitive still waits at the end.

{{ex:de.s0138}}

Hear the prefix

Separable prefixes are always stressed: aufstehen sounds like AUF-stehen, anrufen like AN-rufen. The common ones are an, auf, aus, ein, mit, zu, zurück and fern. In the pronunciation line, the prefix is the part in capitals.

Prefixes that never separate

be-, ver-, er-, ent- and ge- stay glued to the verb and are never stressed: besuchen, verstehen, bekommen. If the stress falls after the prefix, don't split the verb.

{{ex:de.s0139}}{{ex:de.s0140}}
Most common mistake: keeping the verb in one piece. Ich aufstehe um sieben → Ich stehe um sieben auf.
