English says with the bus, to the station, from the doctor, and "the" never changes. German has a third case, the dative (Dativ), and some small words always take it.

Prepositions that always take the dative

mit (with, by), bei (at someone's place), zu (to), von (from), aus (out of, from), nach (to, after) and seit (since, for). After them, the article changes:

NominativeDative
masculineder, ein, meindem, einem, meinem
femininedie, eine, meineder, einer, meiner
neuterdas, ein, meindem, einem, meinem
pluraldie, meineden, meinen + -n
{{ex:de.s0149}}{{ex:de.s0150}}{{ex:de.s0151}}

Watch two things. Feminine die becomes der. And in the plural the noun adds -n too, unless it already ends in -n or -s: mit den Kindern, but mit den Eltern.

zum, zur, beim, vom

Some pairs merge, and the short forms are what people normally say: zu dem → zum, zu der → zur, bei dem → beim, von dem → vom.

{{ex:de.s0152}}{{ex:de.s0153}}

Three ways to say "to"

  • nach + a city or a country with no article: nach Berlin; and nach Hause (home).
  • zu + a person, a shop or a building: zu Anna, zum Arzt, zur Bank.
  • zu Hause means "at home", not "to home".

So never zu Berlin: for a city, "to" is always nach.

{{ex:de.s0154}}

seit: "for" with the present

English says "I have lived here for two years". German uses the present and seit.

{{ex:de.s0155}}

The person you give something to

Verbs like geben (give), zeigen (show) and schenken (give as a present) can have two objects. The thing is the direct object; the person is the indirect object, in the dative. For now you only need to recognize it.

Most common mistake: keeping the nominative after mit. Ich fahre mit der Bus → Ich fahre mit dem Bus.
