English has my, your, his, her, its, our and their. German has a matching set, and like every word before a noun, they show gender and case.

euer drops an ‹e› when it gets an ending: eure Eltern.

Endings like ‹ein›

Possessives take the same endings as ein and kein (textbooks call all three "‹ein›-words"): no ending for a masculine or neuter subject, -e for feminine and plural, and -en for a masculine direct object. The ending comes from the noun that follows, never from the speaker: a woman says mein Bruder, and a man says meine Schwester.

‹sein› or ‹ihr›? Look at the owner

As in English, the owner decides between sein (his) and ihr (her). Then the thing owned decides the ending. So "his sister" is seine Schwester: the ‹-e› comes from ‹Schwester›, not from the man.

{{ex:de.s0108}}{{ex:de.s0109}}

For "their", use ihr as well, with the ending the thing owned needs. So you always ask two questions: who owns it, and what gender and case is the thing owned?

{{ex:de.s0110}}

‹ihr›, ‹ihr›, ‹Ihr›

ihr means "her" and also "their". With a capital ‹I›, Ihr means "your" for someone you call Sie. And ihr on its own is also "you" to several friends. The sentence usually makes it clear; in writing, watch the capital. One trap for English speakers: ihr Bruder is "her brother" or "their brother", never "your brother". To friends, "your brother" is euer Bruder; to one friend, dein Bruder.

Most common mistake: forgetting the accusative ending. Ich besuche mein Bruder → Ich besuche meinen Bruder.
