You have now met two kinds of verbs that come in two parts: a modal with an infinitive (möchte … fahren) and a separable verb (fährt … ab). German treats them the same way: the two verb parts frame the middle of the sentence like a pair of brackets.

One pattern, four slots

First elementVerb (second place)MiddleEnd
Ichmöchtenach Berlinfahren.
Der Zugfährtum achtab.
Morgenmussich frühaufstehen.
Wannkommtder Zugan?

The conjugated verb has the ending that matches the subject; the part at the end never changes. Questions with a question word use the same frame. In a yes/no question the verb moves to first place, and the end stays the end.

Close the bracket last

English keeps the two verb parts together: I'd like to go to Berlin. German puts everything else between them, so in a simple sentence the second part is the last word.

{{ex:de.s0145}}

A long middle

The middle can hold several things: the subject when something else comes first, the direct object, time words and places. A useful tendency is time before place: morgen nach Hamburg sounds more natural than nach Hamburg morgen. It's a tendency, not a law, so don't worry if you hear it the other way round.

The bigger risk is English order inside the bracket. English says I have to buy a ticket today, with the verb early and the rest after it. In German the infinitive waits at the end, and the ticket and the time word fill the middle.

The Perfekt (the past you use in conversation), coming soon, uses this same frame, so it's worth getting comfortable with now.

Most common mistake: closing the bracket too early. Ich möchte fahren nach Berlin → Ich möchte nach Berlin fahren.
