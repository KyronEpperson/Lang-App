English has several past forms: "I ate", "I have eaten", "I did eat". In conversation German mostly uses one, the Perfekt (the past you use in conversation): haben or sein as a helping verb, plus a past participle.

A sentence bracket

The helping verb is the conjugated verb, so it stays in second place. The past participle goes to the very end, the same bracket you know from modal verbs.

{{ex:de.s0184}}{{ex:de.s0185}}

If you start with a time word, the helping verb is still the second element, and the participle still waits at the end. Everything else, however long, goes in between: the time, the people, the place.

{{ex:de.s0186}}

Making the past participle

Regular verbs take ge- and -t: machen → gemacht, kaufen → gekauft, arbeiten → gearbeitet. Irregular verbs (also called strong verbs) take ge- and -en, often with a new vowel. Learn them one by one.

InfinitivePast participle
essengegessen
lesengelesen
trinkengetrunken
fahrengefahren

Two groups take no ge-: verbs with an inseparable prefix (be-, ver-, er-, ent-, ge-) and verbs ending in -ieren. Separable verbs put ge between the prefix and the stem.

The participle never changes with the person: ich habe gemacht, wir haben gemacht. Only the helping verb takes the ending, so get haben or sein right and the rest follows.

‹haben› or ‹sein›?

Most verbs take haben. Take sein when the verb means moving from one place to another (gehen, fahren, kommen, fliegen), a change of state (aufstehen, einschlafen), and with bleiben. When in doubt, ask: did someone go from one place to another, or turn into something new? If so, use sein.

{{ex:de.s0190}}{{ex:de.s0191}}{{ex:de.s0192}}

One Perfekt covers all the English forms: Ich habe gegessen is "I ate", "I have eaten" and "I did eat". To ask a yes/no question, put the helping verb first; the participle still goes to the end.

{{ex:de.s0193}}
Most common mistake: using ‹haben› for every verb. Ich habe nach Berlin gefahren → Ich bin nach Berlin gefahren.
