English keeps the subject before the verb, whatever comes first: Today I work. German has a different rule, and it is the most important rule of German word order.

The verb is the second element

In a German statement, the verb with the ending always stands in second place. The first place can hold the subject, a time, a place or something else. Whatever stands first, the verb comes next.

{{ex:de.s0049}}{{ex:de.s0050}}

Both sentences mean the same; German simply lets you choose what to put first, often the thing you want to stress or the thing that links to what came before. In the second one, heute takes the first place, so the subject ich moves behind the verb. It cannot stay in front: Heute ich arbeite is the classic English speaker's mistake.

Element, not word

"Second" means the second building block, not the second word. Am Montag (on Monday) is one block, and so is die Kinder.

{{ex:de.s0051}}{{ex:de.s0052}}

Time words that often come first

heute (today), morgen (tomorrow), am Wochenende (on the weekend), and the days with am: am Montag, am Dienstag, am Samstag, am Sonntag. Starting with one of them is very natural, and the subject then follows the verb.

{{ex:de.s0053}}

The weather

Weather sentences need es (it), just like English: Es regnet (it's raining). Put a time word first and es moves behind the verb.

{{ex:de.s0054}}{{ex:de.s0055}}

‹und› and ‹aber› do not count

und (and) and aber (but) join two sentences without taking a place. After them, count again from the next block: the first block after aber is the first element, and the verb follows it.

{{ex:de.s0056}}
Most common mistake: keeping English order after a time word. Heute ich arbeite → Heute arbeite ich.
