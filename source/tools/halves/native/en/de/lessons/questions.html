English builds most questions with "do": Where do you live? Do you live here? German has no "do" in questions. It simply moves the verb, and the verb keeps the same ending it has in a statement.

Question words

GermanEnglish
werwho
waswhat
wowhere

wohinwhere to
wannwhen
wiehow
wie vielhow much
warumwhy

Careful with two look-alikes: wer means "who", not "where" (that is wo), and wo is never "who".

W-questions: question word, verb, subject

The question word comes first, the verb second, and then the subject. It is the verb-second rule again. The subject can be a pronoun, a name or a noun with its article: Wo wohnt Anna?, Wann kommt der Zug?

For prices, Was kostet das? is just as common as Wie viel kostet das?

Where, where from, where to

English often adds "from" or "to" at the end. German puts it into the question word: wo asks where something is, woher where someone comes from, wohin where someone is going. So the answer to Woher? usually starts with aus, and the answer to Wo? often with in.

{{ex:de.s0062}}

Yes/no questions: the verb comes first

For a question that expects ‹ja› (yes) or ‹nein› (no), put the verb at the very start, then the subject. No "do" is needed.

{{ex:de.s0063}}{{ex:de.s0064}}{{ex:de.s0065}}

‹ja›, ‹nein›, ‹doch›

ja is yes and nein is no. You will also hear doch: it means "yes, actually" and contradicts a question or statement that has a "not" in it.

The verb keeps its normal ending in a question: du wohnst becomes Wohnst du?, and Sie kommen becomes Kommen Sie?

Most common mistake: keeping the statement order after a question word. Wo du wohnst? → Wo wohnst du?
