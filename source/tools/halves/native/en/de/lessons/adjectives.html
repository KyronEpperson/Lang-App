English adjectives never change: a red jacket, the jacket is red. German adjectives change in one place only: in front of a noun. After sein they stay bare.

After sein: no ending

When the adjective comes after ist or sind, use it exactly as it is in the dictionary.

{{ex:de.s0085}}{{ex:de.s0202}}

Before a noun: learn chunks

In front of a noun the adjective takes an ending that depends on the gender, the case and the article. The full system can wait. For now, learn a few useful chunks whole, with the article and the ending together.

ChunkEnglish
die rote Jackethe red jacket
das blaue Kleidthe blue dress
ein weißes Hemda white shirt
ein neues Handya new cell phone
einen schwarzen Pullovera black sweater (direct object)
{{ex:de.s0203}}{{ex:de.s0204}}

Colors work like any other adjective. After sein they stay bare (Die Jacke ist rot); in front of a noun they take the ending from the chunk. To ask for a color, say Welche Farbe? (Which color?).

Comparing: -er and als

English says "cheaper than" or "more expensive than". German always adds -er, even to long words, and "than" is als. Do not build it with mehr. In everyday speech in some regions you will hear wie instead of als, but standard German uses als.

{{ex:de.s0205}}

Many short words also add an umlaut: alt → älter, groß → größer, warm → wärmer.

{{ex:de.s0206}}

The comparative after sein takes no ending either: Der Mantel ist wärmer. The thing after als is usually in the nominative, like the subject it is compared with: als ich, als der Mantel.

In a clothing store, a comparative asks for the next size: literally, "Do you have that one number bigger?"

{{ex:de.s0207}}

The most: am -sten

-eram -sten
billigbilligeram billigsten
teuerteureram teuersten
gutbesseram besten
gernlieberam liebsten

gut and gern are irregular, just like English good, better, best.

{{ex:de.s0208}}
Most common mistake: comparing with "more" as in English. mehr teuer → teurer, and mehr billig → billiger.
