English says "not" for almost everything: not a car, not expensive, not today. German has two words. kein goes before a noun that would have ein or no article, and nicht does everything else.

‹kein›: "no", "not a", "not any"

Use kein where a noun would otherwise have ein or no article at all. It is the negative article, and it works exactly like ein: keine before a feminine noun, and keinen for a masculine direct object. Unlike ein, it also has a plural form: keine.

masculinekein Terminkeinen Termin
femininekeine Zeitkeine Zeit
neuterkein Autokein Auto
pluralkeine Kinderkeine Kinder

English "I don't drink coffee" becomes Ich trinke keinen Kaffee, literally "I drink no coffee".

{{ex:de.s0097}}

‹nicht›: everything else

nicht never changes. Where it goes depends on what it negates:

  • A whole simple sentence: nicht comes after the verb and after time words, often at the very end.
  • An adjective or a place: nicht goes right before it: nicht teuer, nicht in Berlin.
  • A noun with der, die or das: use nicht, not kein.
{{ex:de.s0098}}{{ex:de.s0099}}{{ex:de.s0100}}{{ex:de.s0101}}

With a noun that has der, die or das, a simple sentence usually puts nicht at the end: Ich kaufe den Pullover nicht. With ein or no article, it's kein: Ich kaufe keinen Pullover.

{{ex:de.s0102}}

A preview: when a sentence ends in a second verb, nicht goes before it: Ich kann heute nicht kommen (I can't come today). You'll meet kann in the lesson on modal verbs.

Two phrases you'll hear every day

Kein Problem means "no problem". Das geht nicht means "that's not possible" or "that won't work", and you'll hear it at offices and counters.

{{ex:de.s0103}}
Most common mistake: using ‹nicht› in front of ‹ein›. Ich habe nicht ein Auto → Ich habe kein Auto. And keep the verb second: Ich nicht verstehe → Ich verstehe nicht.
