How German spelling works

German uses the same 26 letters as English, plus ä, ö, ü and ß. Its spelling is far more regular than English: once you know a handful of rules, you can read almost any word. Under every German phrase in this course there is a pronunciation line in English letters. The syllable in CAPITALS is the stressed one.

Most letters sound as they do in English, so this lesson teaches only what is different, in small groups: letters with a new sound, vowel pairs, umlauts, ‹ch›, word endings, and long and short vowels. Each group has tiles: tap one to hear it and see how the line writes it. Here is the whole alphabet with the German names of the letters. You need them to spell your name.

ß is used after a long vowel or after ei, au, eu, as in Straße and heißen. Swiss German writes ss instead.

‹ei› and ‹ie›

ei sounds like English eye (the line writes ai), and ie sounds like ee. Read the second letter and say its English name: Wein (wine), Wien (Vienna). eu and äu sound like oy, and au like ow. A long ‹e›, as in Tee (tea), is written ay: say day without the glide at the end.

{{tiles:de.tl003}}{{ex:de.s0003}}{{ex:de.s0004}}

Umlauts: ‹ä›, ‹ö›, ‹ü›

The two dots change the sound. ä is close to the e in bet. For ü, say ee with your lips rounded; the pronunciation line writes it ew. For ö, say ay with rounded lips; the line writes ur, but there is no r sound in it.

{{tiles:de.tl004}}{{ex:de.s0005}}{{ex:de.s0006}}

Two ‹ch› sounds

After a, o, u and au, ch is throaty, like Scottish loch: the line writes kh. Everywhere else it is soft, like a strong h in huge: the line writes hh. Neither one is a k or a sh. sch is one sound, always sh, as in schön. In the tiles, the first row is throaty and the second soft.

Word endings

At the end of a word, b, d, g sound like p, t, k: Hund sounds like hoont, and Tag (day) like tahk. A final e is never silent, so Bitte has two syllables. The line writes this weak ‹e›, and the weak endings -en and -er, with uh: BIT-uh. One ending breaks the rule: -ig sounds like ihh, as in zwanzig (TSVAHN-tsihh).

Long and short vowels

A German vowel can be long or short, and the spelling usually tells you which. An h after a vowel is silent and makes it long: Bahn, Uhr. A doubled vowel is long too: Tee, Boot. Before a doubled consonant, or before two consonants, a vowel is usually short: Wasser, Sonne, Hund. The line shows the difference for ‹e›, ‹i› and ‹o›: long ay, ee, oh, short eh, i, aw. So Tee is tay but Bett (bed) is beht, and Sohn (son) is zohn but Sonne is ZAWN-uh. For ‹a› and ‹u› the line writes ah and oo either way.

Stress, ‹r› and capital letters

Most German words stress their first syllable, but be-, ge-, ver-, er- and ent- are never stressed, and many words borrowed from other languages stress the last syllable: Hotel, Büro. The German r is made in the throat, and after a vowel it often softens to an uh; listen to the audio. Every noun starts with a capital letter, and the small words der, die, das in front of nouns all mean "the" (lesson 3 explains them).

{{ex:de.s0013}}
Most common mistake: reading ‹w› the English way. Wasser with an English w → Wasser starting with a v sound.

Read whole words

Now put the pieces together. Read a word left to right, one spelling at a time: sch, ei and ie each count as one sound. Most words stress their first syllable; the notes point out the ones that do not. German builds long words by joining short ones. Read a compound part by part, with the stress on the first part: Fahr + Rad is Fahrrad (bicycle), FAHR-raht. Each part keeps its own ending, so the ‹d› at the end of Hand still sounds t in Handschuh (glove). Sound out every card below before you flip it.
