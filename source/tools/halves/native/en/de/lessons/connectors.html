English joins sentences without changing the word order: "I'm tired, but I'm coming". German has two kinds of joining words. One kind leaves the order alone. The other sends the verb to the end.

und, oder, aber, denn: no change

These four do not count as the first element. After them, the sentence starts again as usual: subject, then the verb in second place.

{{ex:de.s0209}}{{ex:de.s0210}}{{ex:de.s0211}}{{ex:de.s0212}}

As in English, you can drop a repeated subject after und, oder and aber: Ich bin müde und gehe nach Hause. After denn, always say the subject again. In speech, many people prefer weil to denn; you will meet it below.

wenn: the verb goes to the end

wenn means "if" and also "when" for things that happen again and again. After wenn, the conjugated verb goes to the very end of its part of the sentence.

{{ex:de.s0213}}

The verb that moves is the one with the ending. A separable verb joins up again at the end: wenn ich früh aufstehe, and a modal goes after its infinitive: wenn ich arbeiten muss.

A wenn part at the start

A whole wenn part can come first. Then it counts as the first element, so the main verb comes right after the comma, and the subject follows it. English says "If I have time, I'll come"; German says "If I time have, come I".

weil and dass

You will often hear weil, another word for "because", and dass, "that". Both send the verb to the end, like wenn. For now, just recognize them.

{{ex:de.s0215}}{{ex:de.s0216}}

German writes a comma before aber, denn, wenn, weil and dass, but not before und and oder.

Most common mistake: keeping English order after wenn. wenn ich habe Zeit → wenn ich Zeit habe, and Wenn ich Zeit habe, ich komme → Wenn ich Zeit habe, komme ich.
