English marks the object only on a few pronouns: he sees, but someone sees him. German marks it on the article too. The subject (who does the verb) is in the nominative, the form you already know. The direct object (the thing or person the verb acts on) is in the accusative (Akkusativ).

Only the masculine changes

Good news: in the accusative, only masculine nouns change. der becomes den, and ein becomes einen. Feminine, neuter and plural nouns look exactly as before.

haben and es gibt

Many everyday verbs take a direct object: haben, kaufen, suchen, brauchen, nehmen, sehen. So Bruder, a masculine noun, becomes einen Bruder after habe. The phrase es gibt (there is, there are) takes the accusative too.

{{ex:de.s0089}}{{ex:de.s0090}}

Read the article, not the position

In English, the first noun is almost always the subject. In German, the verb must be the second element, but the subject does not have to come first. Any element can, including the direct object. Den Rock kauft Anna means "Anna is buying the skirt", not the other way round; it just puts the skirt in the spotlight.

{{ex:de.s0091}}{{ex:de.s0092}}

Compare Der Mann sucht den Hund with Den Hund sucht der Mann. Both mean "The man is looking for the dog". The nouns swap places, but the articles keep their jobs: der is the subject, den is the direct object.

{{ex:de.s0093}}

So don't trust the order: look at the article. den or einen marks a masculine direct object wherever it stands. With die and das the article gives no clue, and the meaning decides.

Most common mistake: forgetting that a masculine direct object changes. Ich habe ein Bruder → Ich habe einen Bruder. Ich sehe der Mann → Ich sehe den Mann.
