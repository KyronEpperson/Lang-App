German numbers are built from a small set of parts. Two things surprise English speakers: the ones come before the tens, and "half three" means 2:30.

0 to 20

null, eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf, sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn, elf, zwölf. From 13, add -zehn: dreizehn, vierzehn. Two spellings drop letters: sechzehn (no s) and siebzehn (no en). Then zwanzig.

{{ex:de.s0066}}{{ex:de.s0067}}

Tens, and ones before tens

The tens end in -zig: zwanzig, vierzig, fünfzig, with one exception, dreißig (30). From 21 on, German says the ones first: "one-and-twenty", written as one word.

{{ex:de.s0068}}{{ex:de.s0069}}

hundert is 100 and tausend 1,000. When you count, one is eins; before a noun, and in einundzwanzig, it is ein.

Prices

Say the euros, the word Euro, then the cents. Euro never takes an -s after a number. To ask a price: Was kostet das? or Wie viel kostet das?

{{ex:de.s0070}}{{ex:de.s0071}}

For one thing, the verb is kostet; for several, it is kosten.

Telling the time

Ask Wie spät ist es? (what time is it?). Full hours use Uhr: Es ist drei Uhr. Viertel nach drei is 3:15 and Viertel vor vier is 3:45. And halb looks ahead to the next hour: halb vier is "half way to four", so 3:30.

{{ex:de.s0072}}{{ex:de.s0073}}

For "at", use um: um acht Uhr. Timetables, shops and offices use the 24-hour clock: um zwanzig Uhr is 8 p.m.

{{ex:de.s0074}}

A time at the front is the first element, so the verb comes next and the subject follows.

Most common mistake: reading halb as half past. halb vier for 4:30 → halb fünf. halb always points to the next hour.
