An English word can pile up consonants: strengths has three at the start and four at the end. A Vietnamese syllable is much tidier. It has at most one first consonant, one vowel or vowel pair, one last consonant, and always a tone.

Every syllable needs a vowel and a tone. The consonants at either end are optional, and there is never a cluster like the str of street.

One sound, two spellings

Before i, e and ê, three sounds change their spelling: the k sound is written k, g becomes gh, and ng becomes ngh. Everywhere else they are c, g and ng. The k sound before a w glide is written qu.

ng at the start

English has the ng sound only at the end of a syllable, as in sing. Vietnamese also starts syllables with it. Say singer, then drop the si: what is left starts like ngủ. The family name Nguyễn is a single syllable that starts the same way.

Last consonants

A syllable can end in m, n, ng, nh, p, t, c or ch. The last p, t and c are not released: your mouth gets into position and stops, with no puff. In Hanoi, -anh sounds like "aing" and -ach like "aik", so bánh is never the English word bank.

Stop endings

Syllables that end in p, t, c or ch are cut short, so they can carry only two tones: rising or heavy. That makes them easier to spell, since only two marks are possible. In the pronunciation line, these chunks always end in / or a dot, never in any other tone symbol.

{{ex:vi.s0026}}{{ex:vi.s0027}}
Most common mistake: reading -anh the English way. bánh mì said "bank me" → bánh mì said as its line shows, baing/ mee_, with no k sound.
