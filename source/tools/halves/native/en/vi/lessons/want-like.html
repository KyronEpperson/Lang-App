English puts "to" between want and the next verb: I want to eat. Vietnamese just puts the two verbs side by side: Tôi muốn ăn. There is no word for that "to".

muốn: want

muốn takes a verb or a noun after it.

{{ex:vi.s0149}}{{ex:vi.s0150}}

thích: like

thích works the same way. rất thích is "really like". Food or drink in general takes no classifier.

{{ex:vi.s0151}}{{ex:vi.s0152}}

thích or muốn?

English "I'd like" is a polite "I want", but Vietnamese thích only means "like, enjoy" in general. Tôi thích phở is "I like pho"; for what you want right now, use muốn.

To offer something, wrap the verb in có … không, as in any yes/no question.

{{ex:vi.s0153}}

cần: need

cần also takes a noun or a verb, with no "to".

{{ex:vi.s0154}}

Not: không before the verb

{{ex:vi.s0155}}

Ordering: Cho tôi …

At a stall or café, people rarely say "I want". They say Cho tôi ("give me") + number + container + food. The container word works like a classifier: bát (bowl; South: tô), cốc or ly (glass), đĩa (plate). The describing word still comes after the noun: phở bò, beef pho.

{{ex:vi.s0156}}

The order never changes: number, container, then the food or drink with its describing words after it: hai cốc cà phê sữa đá, two iced coffees with milk.

To a stall-holder older than you, use your self-word em instead of tôi. tôi is not wrong, but it sounds distant. Add ạ to be extra polite.

{{ex:vi.s0157}}
Most common mistake: translating "to" in "want to". Tôi muốn để ăn. → Tôi muốn ăn.
