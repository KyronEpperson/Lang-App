English turns a sentence into a question by moving words around ("You are…" becomes "Are you…?") or just by raising the voice. Vietnamese does neither. It wraps the sentence in question words, and every syllable keeps its tone.

có … không

Put có before the verb and không at the very end. The rest stays in normal order. có is often dropped in speech, but không never is.

{{ex:vi.s0053}}{{ex:vi.s0054}}

It works the same with words like "busy" or "well": bận means "busy".

{{ex:vi.s0055}}

You already know one question like this: Anh khỏe không? is a có … không question with có dropped. The usual answer repeats the key word, then thanks the person.

{{ex:vi.s0056}}

Answering: no single word for yes

English says "yes" or "no" to everything. Vietnamese repeats the key word. For yes, say có or repeat the verb; for no, say không.

{{ex:vi.s0057}}{{ex:vi.s0058}}

Vâng is a polite yes in the North, good for agreeing with someone older. You will also hear dạ, used in both regions and especially in the South.

phải không: checking

To check something you think is true, add phải không? ("right?") at the end, a little like English "isn't it?". It is the usual way to ask about là sentences. The answer is phải (or vâng) for yes, không phải for no.

{{ex:vi.s0059}}{{ex:vi.s0060}}

You can also put the check in the middle: Bạn có phải là sinh viên không? means the same.

Don't lean on your voice

Anh uống cà phê with an English rising voice may be understood in context, but it sounds like surprise, not a real question. Add the question words, and say không with its level tone. In writing, too, a question mark alone is not enough: the question words must be there.

Most common mistake: leaving out the final không. Em có thích phở? → Em có thích phở không?
