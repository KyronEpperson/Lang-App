Clock times, days and dates are built from numbers you already know. The traps are the order of the parts and a few tone marks.

What time is it?

giờ means hour or o'clock, and mấy asks "how many" for small numbers, so mấy giờ is "what time". For minutes, add the number after giờ (you can add phút, minute). Half past is rưỡi, with the broken tone. For the minutes before the hour, Hanoi uses kém (less): bốn giờ kém mười lăm is a quarter to four.

To say when something happens, put lúc (at) before the time. Timetables may use the 24-hour clock, but in conversation people say the hour and add the part of the day.

{{ex:vi.s0202}}

Morning, afternoon, evening

English says "8 a.m."; Vietnamese adds the part of the day after the time: sáng (morning), chiều (afternoon), tối (evening). Watch the tone on tối: it is rising. With the level tone, tôi means "I".

{{ex:vi.s0203}}

Days: numbered from Sunday

Chủ nhật is Sunday. The other days are numbered with thứ (order), starting from two, so Monday is thứ hai, literally "the second". Wednesday uses tư, not bốn, for four. Days work with the time words you know: thứ hai tuần sau is next Monday.

Dates: day, then month

Months are numbered too: tháng một is January, tháng chín is September (April is tháng tư). A date goes from small to big: ngày (day) and its number, then tháng (month) and its number. American English puts the month first; Vietnamese never does. The year, if you need it, comes last, after năm (year): day, month, year, the same order you will see on Vietnamese forms and tickets. Careful: năm is also the number five, so tháng năm is May.

Most common mistake: the month first, as in American English. tháng chín ngày mười lăm → ngày mười lăm tháng chín.
