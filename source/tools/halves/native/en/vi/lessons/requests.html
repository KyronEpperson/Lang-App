English makes a request polite with "please" and "could you". Vietnamese does it mostly with the right pronouns and a few small words at the start and the end of the sentence.

Cho …: give me, let me

cho means "give" or "let", and Cho tôi… is the basic way to order. To someone older than you, such as a stall-holder, call yourself em and end with ạ, which shows respect. Adding xin (ask for) makes it softer still. Choose the self-word by the listener: em to someone a little older, cháu to someone your parents' or grandparents' age. A grandchild thanks a grandmother with Cháu cảm ơn bà ạ.

The thing you order keeps its classifier and describing words in place: number, then cốc or ly (glass) or bát (bowl), then the noun, then what kind: hai cốc trà đá, một bát phở bò.

Cho em hỏi (let me ask) is the polite way to start a question to a stranger.

{{ex:vi.s0217}}

mời: please, as an invitation

mời means "invite". Use it when you offer something, as a host does: please sit, please come in, please eat. It is not for asking a favor.

{{ex:vi.s0218}}

làm ơn: please, to a stranger

làm ơn is a formal "please", used with strangers. Said in every sentence, it sounds stiff, so save it for when you really need it.

{{ex:vi.s0219}}

nhé, ạ, dạ, vâng

nhé at the end softens a suggestion, like "OK?" in English. ạ at the end shows respect to someone older; it would sound odd to a friend your own age. When someone older speaks to you, answer Dạ, and agree with Vâng ạ (in the South, Dạ).

Most common mistake: a bare Cho tôi… to an older stall-holder. It isn't wrong, but it sounds curt. Cho tôi một cốc trà đá. → Cho em một cốc trà đá ạ.
