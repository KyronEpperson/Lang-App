English joins a reason and a result with one word: "because" or "so", never both. Vietnamese likes to mark both halves.

vì … nên: because … so

vì introduces the reason and nên introduces the result. When the reason comes first, Vietnamese often uses both, which sounds odd in English ("because it rains, so I stay home") but is normal here.

{{ex:vi.s0208}}

You can also drop vì and keep nên, like English "so". When the result comes first, use vì alone, like English "because".

{{ex:vi.s0209}}{{ex:vi.s0210}}

In speech you will also hear tại vì and bởi vì: both mean "because".

Why? Tại sao?

Tại sao (why) usually goes at the start of the question. The answer starts with vì, never with nên. In relaxed speech, people often shorten it to sao: Sao chị không đến? (Why didn't you come?)

{{ex:vi.s0211}}{{ex:vi.s0212}}

bị: when something bad happens to you

English says "I have a headache" or "I got lost". Vietnamese often puts bị before the problem. It marks something unpleasant that happens to you, so it goes with illness, pain, losing things and getting lost, not with good news. For something good that comes your way, Vietnamese uses được instead: Tôi được nghỉ is "I got the day off". Losing works the same way as illness: Tôi bị mất ví (I lost my wallet). At a pharmacy or a doctor's, start with Tôi bị… and name the problem.

I'm sick.Tôi bị ốm.
I have a fever.Tôi bị sốt.

ốm (sick) is the Hanoi word, with the rising tone. With the level tone, ôm means "hug". In the South, people say bệnh for sick, and ốm means thin.

{{ex:vi.s0214}}
Most common mistake: mixing up the two halves. Tại sao anh ở nhà? Nên trời mưa. → Vì trời mưa. vì goes with the reason, nên with the result.
