English puts the describing word first: "an American person", "the English language". Vietnamese puts the noun first and the country after it: "person America", "language England". This order runs through the whole language, so it is worth getting right from the start.

Nationality: người + country

người means "person". Add a country after it and you have a nationality. Mỹ is America, Việt (or Việt Nam) is Vietnam, Anh is England, Úc is Australia, Pháp is France.

{{ex:vi.s0061}}{{ex:vi.s0062}}

You will often be asked Anh là người nước nào? ("Which country are you from?", to a man a little older). nước means "country". Answer in a full sentence with người and the country: Tôi là người Mỹ.

{{ex:vi.s0063}}

Yes, Anh (England) is spelled like anh (an older man, "you"). Context always makes it clear, and a country gets a capital letter.

Languages: tiếng + country

tiếng means "language" or "sound". Put the country after it: tiếng Việt, tiếng Anh. nói means "to speak" and học "to study, to learn".

{{ex:vi.s0027}}{{ex:vi.s0064}}

Keep the two apart: người Việt is a Vietnamese person, tiếng Việt is the Vietnamese language.

Jobs

Jobs are nouns, so they take là: Tôi là giáo viên. To ask what someone does, say làm nghề gì?, literally "do which trade?". Keep the question word at the end, where the answer will go. The same question works for anyone: just change the first word.

Careful with students: sinh viên is only for college or university. A child or teenager at school is a học sinh.

We

Chúng tôi is "we" when the listener is not included, as when you tell a stranger about you and your friends. With these frames you can already introduce yourself in four short sentences: your name, your nationality, your language and your job.
