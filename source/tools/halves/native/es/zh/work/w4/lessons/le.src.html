English marks time on the verb: go, went, gone. Chinese verbs never change. 我去北京 can be yesterday, today or tomorrow, and a time word tells you which. So 了 le is not a past tense. It does two other jobs.

Job 1: the action is done

了 right after a verb says the action happened and is complete. It sounds most natural when the object has a number.

{{ex:zh.s0172}}{{ex:zh.s0173}}

When the object has no number, put 了 at the end of the sentence instead. Verb-object words such as 吃饭 chīfàn (sometimes called separable verbs) do the same: 我吃饭了, "I've eaten".

{{ex:zh.s0174}}

Job 2: something has changed

了 at the end of a sentence can also mean "now it's so, and before it wasn't". English often says "now", "has gotten" or "already".

{{ex:zh.s0175}}{{ex:zh.s0176}}{{ex:zh.s0177}}

When there is no 了

  • Habits in the past. "I used to take the subway a lot" is 我以前常常坐地铁, with no 了.
  • States in the past. "I was busy yesterday" is 我昨天很忙. The time word already says when.
  • After 没. 没 says the action did not happen, so there is nothing to complete: 我昨天没去.
{{ex:zh.s0178}}{{ex:zh.s0179}}{{ex:zh.s0093}}

Asking about it

To ask if something is done, add 吗 after 了. Answer yes with the verb and 了, and no with 没 and the verb, without 了.

{{ex:zh.s0180}}

A quick test

Before you add 了, ask: did one action get done, or did something change? If you are only describing how things were, leave it out.

Most common mistake: adding 了 to everything in the past, the way English adds -ed. 我昨天很忙了 → 我昨天很忙. 我昨天没去了 → 我昨天没去.
