English links two actions with "to": "I'm going to the supermarket to buy some things." Chinese has no word for that "to". It simply puts the two verb phrases one after the other, in the order they happen. Grammar books call this a serial verb pattern.

去 or 来 + place + what you do there

First you go (去 qù) or come (来 lái) somewhere, then you do something there. The sentence follows the same order.

{{ex:zh.s0186}}{{ex:zh.s0187}}

Use 来 when the place is where you or the listener are, and 去 when it is somewhere else. Many learners in China say 我来中国学中文, "I came to China to study Chinese."

{{ex:zh.s0188}}

How you go + 去 or 来

The way you travel comes first, too, because you get on before you arrive. 坐 zuò (literally "sit") is for anything you ride inside: the subway, a bus, a taxi, a train, a plane. 骑 qí is for things you sit astride, like a bike. 走路 zǒulù is to walk.

All together

You can chain all of it, still in the usual line-up: who, when, how, go + place, what to do there.

{{ex:zh.s0192}}
WhoWhenHowGo + whereWhat
我明天坐高铁去西安看兵马俑

Saying no

不 or 没 goes before the first verb, and it covers the whole chain.

{{ex:zh.s0193}}

Leave out the "to"

Do not look for a Chinese word for "to" or "in order to". The order already says it: the first verb gets you there, the second says why.

Most common mistake: putting the place at the end, as in English. 我去买东西在超市 → 我去超市买东西. 我去学校坐地铁 → 我坐地铁去学校.
