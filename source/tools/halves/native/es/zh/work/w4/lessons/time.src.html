Telling time and dates in Chinese is mostly numbers you already know. The two things English speakers trip on are the order (big before small) and where the time goes in a sentence.

Clock time

点 diǎn is "o'clock", 分 fēn is "minutes", and 半 bàn is "half". Like a measure word, 点 takes 两 for two: 两点, never 二点. One o'clock is 一点 yī diǎn: in clock times 一 keeps its first tone. To ask the time, use 几.

Days of the week

English names each day. Chinese numbers them: 星期 xīngqī (week) + one to six, starting from Monday. Sunday is 星期天 xīngqītiān (also written 星期日).

{{ex:zh.s0144}}

今天 jīntiān is today, 明天 míngtiān tomorrow and 昨天 zuótiān yesterday. Notice that "today is Monday" needs no verb; 今天是星期一 is also fine.

Dates: big to small

Months are numbered too: 一月 yīyuè is January, 十二月 shí'èryuè December. The day of the month is the number + 号 hào in speech (日 in writing). English can say "the 3rd of December"; Chinese always goes from the bigger unit to the smaller one: month, then day.

{{ex:zh.s0145}}{{ex:zh.s0146}}

Time words go before the verb

English usually puts the time at the end: "I'm going to Beijing tomorrow." Chinese puts it after the person and before the verb, or at the very start of the sentence. Never at the end.
