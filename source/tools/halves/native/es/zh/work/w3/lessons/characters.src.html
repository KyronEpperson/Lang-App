English has 26 letters that spell sounds. Chinese has no alphabet: each character is one syllable with a meaning. That sounds like a lot to learn, but characters are built from a small set of parts, and many parts come back again and again. This course aims at recognizing about 300 common characters by the end. You will read them, not write them by hand.

Strokes

Characters are made of strokes: lines, dots and hooks. There are only a few basic strokes, and every character is built from them. Knowing them helps you see the parts and look characters up. This course does not drill stroke order.

Pictures

The oldest characters began as drawings. 人 rén is a person walking, 口 kǒu an open mouth, 木 mù a tree with roots, 山 shān three peaks, 日 rì the sun and 月 yuè the moon. More pictures: 女 nǚ is a woman, 子 zǐ a child with its arms out, 门 mén a door, 雨 yǔ rain falling, 火 huǒ a flame and 水 shuǐ a stream. 上 shàng (up) and 下 xià (down) are a mark above and below a line.

Radicals: hints, not spellings

A radical is the part of a character that hints at its meaning. Many radicals are picture characters, squeezed to one side. Three dots on the left (氵) mean water, as in 汤 tāng (soup). A small 口 on the left often marks a mouth word or a sound. 亻, a narrow form of 人, means a person, as in 你 nǐ (you). 女 means a woman.

Another part often hints at the sound. 妈 mā (mom) is 女 (woman) for the meaning and 马 mǎ (horse) for the sound. The hint is rough: you cannot sound out a character letter by letter, so learn each one with its pinyin.

Two parts, one meaning

Many characters join two parts whose meanings add up. Two trees make 林 lín (woods). A person leaning on a tree, 休 xiū, is to rest. The sun and the moon make 明 míng, bright. A woman with her child makes 好 hǎo, good.

{{ex:zh.s0031}}{{ex:zh.s0032}}{{ex:zh.s0033}}

Most characters are built from two or three parts you already know. Sometimes every part gives the meaning, as in 好. More often the radical gives the meaning and the other part gives the sound: 请 qǐng (please), 清 qīng (clear) and 晴 qíng (sunny) all take their sound from 青 qīng, and 讠 (speech), 氵 (water) and 日 (sun) tell them apart. Simplified and traditional

Mainland China uses simplified characters, which this course teaches. Taiwan and Hong Kong use traditional ones, with more strokes: 门 is written 門 there.

Most common mistake: trying to sound out a character from its parts. 妈 read as "woman-horse", or as mǎ → 妈 mā. The parts are hints for memory; the pinyin tells you the sound.
