Chinese numbers are built like a calculator: once you know one to ten, you can say every number up to 99 with no new words.

Eleven to ninety-nine

English has special words like "twelve" and "twenty". Chinese just says the parts: twelve is "ten two", twenty is "two ten", twenty-five is "two ten five".

Hundreds, and the zero

A hundred is 一百 yìbǎi, "one hundred": the one is never dropped. When a place in the middle is empty, Chinese says 零 líng (zero) where English says "and".

{{ex:zh.s0112}}

Careful: 一百五 is short for 一百五十, so it means 150, not 105.

{{ex:zh.s0113}}

二 or 两?

Chinese has two words for "two". Use 二 èr when counting and inside bigger numbers (十二, 二十). Use 两 liǎng when two of something are counted, before a measure word such as 个.

{{ex:zh.s0114}}

Money

The Chinese currency is the yuan. Price tags write 元 yuán, but people say 块 kuài. Like a measure word, it comes right after the number. A tenth of a yuan is a 毛 máo, and in speech the last 毛 is often dropped.

{{ex:zh.s0115}}{{ex:zh.s0116}}{{ex:zh.s0117}}

To ask "how much" or "how many", use 多少 duōshao for any number, and 几 jǐ when you expect a small one, under about ten.

Most common mistake: using the counting two before a measure word. 二个人 → 两个人. Count with 二, but say two of something with 两.
