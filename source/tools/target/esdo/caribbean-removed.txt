<p>Everything you have learned works in the Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico and Cuba. What changes most is the sound: Caribbean Spanish is fast, and it softens or drops sounds at the end of syllables. This lesson trains your ear. Keep speaking and writing the standard forms you know; you will be understood everywhere.</p>
<h3>The disappearing s</h3>
<p>At the end of a syllable, <b>s</b> often turns into a soft breath, like English "h", or disappears. <b>Los niños</b> can sound like "lo niño", and <b>¿Cómo estás?</b> like "¿Cómo 'tá?". The <b>s</b> is still there in writing and in the grammar, so use the article and the verb to tell that something is plural.</p>
{{ex:Los niños están en la casa.|lohs NEE-nyohs ehs-TAHN ehn lah KAH-sah|The kids are in the house.}}
{{ex:¿Cómo estás?|KOH-moh ehs-TAHS|How are you?}}
<h3>-ado becomes -ao</h3>
<p>In relaxed speech, the <b>d</b> in <b>-ado</b> softens until it is gone: <b>cansado</b> sounds like "cansao". You will hear this in other countries too, but it is very strong in the Caribbean.</p>
{{ex:Estoy muy cansado.|ehs-TOY mwee kahn-SAH-doh|I'm very tired.}}
<h3>r at the end of a syllable</h3>
<p>Regional (Dominican): in Santo Domingo, an <b>r</b> at the end of a syllable often sounds like <b>l</b>, so <b>porque</b> sounds like "polque". In the Cibao region in the north, it can become an "ee" sound: "poique".</p>
{{ex:Voy a comer porque tengo hambre.|boy ah koh-MEHR POHR-keh TEHN-goh AHM-breh|I'm going to eat because I'm hungry.}}
<h3>tú before the verb in questions</h3>
<p>Regional (Dominican): textbook Spanish says <b>¿Cómo estás?</b> or <b>¿Cómo estás tú?</b>, with any pronoun after the verb. Dominicans often put <b>tú</b> before the verb. The verb still agrees with <b>tú</b>, so it's written <b>quieres</b> with an <b>s</b>, even when you don't hear it.</p>
{{ex:¿Cómo tú estás?|KOH-moh too ehs-TAHS|How are you?}}
{{ex:¿Qué tú quieres?|keh too KYEH-rehs|What do you want?}}
