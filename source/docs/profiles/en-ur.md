# English speakers learning Urdu: course profile

Course `en-ur`: Urdu for English speakers. Every explanation, prompt, `why`, `hint`, meaning and card note is in English. Answers, examples and card targets are in Urdu, in the Perso-Arabic script, right to left, **without vowel marks** (real Urdu), except in the script lessons and on a short list of homographs (§2.8). Machine-readable twin: `tools/target/ur/profile.json`, written by a script from the same data as §8 and §9, so the two agree. Pronunciation generator: `tools/target/ur/respell.js`, a **lexicon** tool (§6): writers never type a pron.

(The course folder keeps its `HOLD` file until the lead releases it. No other file was written.)

**Evidence tags.**
- **[research-backed]**: an empirical study opened for this profile, named where it is used.
- **[curriculum convention]**: what syllabuses, textbooks, standards bodies and courses do.
- **[practitioner opinion]**: a teacher's, school's, blog's or app's stated view, with no data behind it.
- **[inference]**: this profile's own reasoning, **including the author's own knowledge of Urdu**. Every claim of that kind that writers will copy is in the native-check list (§11).
- **(as cited in en-hi)**: taken from `docs/profiles/en-hi.md` and not reopened here.

Untagged rules in §6 to §10 are design decisions, not findings.

## 1. Summary

- **Variant and register:** standard Pakistani Urdu (educated Karachi / Lahore speech), Perso-Arabic script, `ur-PK`; everyday words over heavy Persian and Arabic ones; آپ by default, تم for friends, تو recognition only; Pakistani greetings (السلام علیکم, شکریہ, اللہ حافظ with خدا حافظ accepted). The course says plainly that spoken Urdu and Hindi are mutually understood (§2).
- **Voice:** Azure `ur-PK-UzmaNeural` / `AsadNeural` for recorded clips; Android's Google TTS has Urdu on some devices; **iOS has no Urdu voice**; `voiceMatch` keeps any other language's voice from reading Urdu (§2.2).
- **Script and fonts:** unvowelled Urdu everywhere except the script lessons and marked homographs (§2.8); correct in Naskh, Nastaliq where the device has it. The brief says no web font can load; **the code's CSP already allows Google Fonts**, so Noto Nastaliq Urdu is a lead decision (§10.6).
- **Pron:** the **en-hi key** (ā ī ū, ṭ ḍ ṛ, aspirates, tilde nasals) plus q ḵh ġ z f zh and izafat -e-, **generated by a lexicon tool** (about 850 entries, 2,207 forms) that throws on unlisted words and undecidable homographs; 3 differences from the Hindi generator on 133 shared words, all real Urdu/Hindi differences (§6).
- **Standing rule:** (a) gender and number agreement, (b) postpositions and the oblique; a sound strand in the five script lessons; keywords tested on 56 Urdu `why` lines, 0 failures (§7).
- **Answers:** tiles by default; typing in Urdu script or romanisation. Today Urdu script is matched exactly (the Arabic folds are `isAr()`-only), ۔ is not stripped, and Roman Urdu like *theek, hoon, chahiye, wo* fails: requests 1–4 must be built before writers start (§10.5, §10.8).
- **Lessons and decks:** 28 lessons (all 27 roadmap items, roadmap order, plus `connectors`), 18 decks in 5 groups including five ordinary script decks and "Traps for English speakers" (§8, §9).
- **Biggest gaps:** no native or dictionary check of the lexicon; no Urdu learner-error study (Hindi data used); device behaviour of Urdu tiles, fonts and voices untested (§11).

## 2. Variant, register and voice

### 2.1 Variant: standard Urdu of Pakistan

- The roadmap names "Standard Urdu of Pakistan, written in Nastaliq script" and `ur-PK`, on the McGill Introductory Urdu-Hindi outline built on Farooqui & Pien's *Beginning Urdu* [curriculum convention]. This profile keeps it. `ttsLang: "ur-PK"`.
- **Norm:** the educated, neutral speech of Karachi and Lahore as heard on Pakistani TV news and talk shows; not the Lucknow / Delhi literary register, not Punjabi-coloured street Urdu. Regional forms (Punjabi *tusī̃*, Karachi slang) appear only in notes [inference].
- **Say it plainly to users** (course blurb and `letters-1`): "This course teaches everyday standard Urdu as spoken in Pakistan, written in the Urdu script. Spoken Urdu and Hindi are so close that their speakers understand each other in everyday conversation; they are written in different scripts, and formal and literary Urdu uses many more Persian and Arabic words." [curriculum convention: CAL's Urdu heritage profile (Rubab Qureshi, UPenn): Urdu and Hindi have "identical syntax and grammar", speakers "can talk with each other informally without much difficulty", while formal and literary registers diverge "to the point of being mutually incomprehensible" ([CAL](https://cal.org/heritagevoicecollectionurdu))].
- **Where Urdu and Hindi differ in everyday words, the course uses the Urdu word:** شکریہ (not دھنیواد), السلام علیکم (not نمستے), اللہ حافظ / خدا حافظ (not "phir milenge" as the goodbye), جی ہاں, مہربانی, ضرور, شاید, مشکل, آسان, خوبصورت, وقت (time), ملک (country), شہر, دوست, زبان. Hindi-only Sanskrit words (प्रश्न, उत्तर, समय for "time" in formal use) are never used [inference].
- **Everyday over heavy:** use the word an educated Karachi speaker uses in polite speech. English loans that are normal in Pakistan are written in Urdu script and used: اسٹیشن, ٹکٹ, ڈاکٹر, موبائل, فون, ہوٹل, بس, ٹرین, کلاس, میٹنگ. Heavy literary words are avoided: not براہ کرم in speech (signs only; requests use کیجیے / ذرا), not دریافت کرنا for "ask" (پوچھنا), not شب بخیر as a greeting in dialogues (it is the late-night `greet` slot only). Card notes may name the formal word once [inference].
- **Never Latin letters in Urdu strings.** The generator throws on them.

### 2.2 Voice

- **Azure** (recorded clips): `ur-PK-UzmaNeural` (female) and `ur-PK-AsadNeural` (male) were announced as generally available ([Microsoft Tech Community, "Eleven more languages…"](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/azure-ai-services-blog/eleven-more-languages-are-generally-available-for-azure-neural/ba-p/2236871)); a voice catalogue lists Uzma as Urdu (Pakistan), female, and Gul as Urdu (India) ([json2video](https://json2video.com/ai-voices/azure/voices/ur-pk-uzmaneural/)). The Microsoft language-support page, as fetched, showed only `ur-IN` for speech to text and did not render its voice table, so the current voice list should be confirmed with the REST voice list before recording [inference].
- **Android:** Google's TalkBack language list includes **"Urdu (Pakistan)"** as a Google Text-to-Speech language, with the caveat that "some devices may not support all languages in all regions" ([Google, TalkBack languages](https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/11101402?hl=en)).
- **iOS:** Apple's iOS 26 feature-availability page lists the VoiceOver / Live Speech / Read & Speak languages; the list goes from Ukrainian to Valencian with **no Urdu**, and Urdu dictation is not offered ([Apple](https://www.apple.com/ios/feature-availability/); the fetch read the first 100,000 of 160,000 characters, which included that list). iOS 26 does add Urdu as an interface language, but not Siri or dictation ([TechJuice](https://www.techjuice.pk/ios-26-brings-full-urdu-support-for-iphone-users-in-pakistan/amp)). So **iPhone users have no device voice for Urdu**.
- **What happens with no voice:** without `voiceMatch` the engine falls back to "any voice whose tag starts with ur-" and then to **nothing**, and still calls `speechSynthesis` with `lang: ur-PK`, so a browser may read Urdu with a default (English) voice or stay silent (`src/app.html`, `pickVoice` / `speak`). `profile.json` sets `voiceMatch: "^ur(?:[-_]|$)"`: an `ur-IN` voice is accepted as a fallback (same language, Indian accent), any other voice is refused, and the engine shows its existing toast "No voice for this language on this device." [design].
- **Decision:** (1) record Azure clips (Uzma and Asad alternating by speaker gender in dialogues, Uzma for cards) for every card target and example row; the engine already plays clips from `audio/<t>/index.json` before falling back to the device voice; (2) `voiceMatch` as above; (3) the `pron` line stays on by default for the whole course (§6.5), because many learners will have no voice. Engine request 3 (§10.8).

### 2.3 آپ, تم and تو

| Form | Verb with it | Used for | In the course |
|---|---|---|---|
| آپ āp | plural forms: آپ کہاں رہتے ہیں؟ / رہتی ہیں؟ Requests: بیٹھیے or بیٹھیں | strangers, older people, shopkeepers and customers, teachers, colleagues, in-laws; the safe default | **default** in every prompt, example and dialogue from lesson 6 |
| تم tum | -تے ہو: تم کہاں رہتے ہو؟ Command: بیٹھو | friends, children, younger siblings and cousins | taught in `greetings`, used in friend and family dialogues, practised in `imperative` |
| تو tū | singular: تو کہاں رہتا ہے؟ Command = stem: بیٹھ | very close friends, small children, God in prayer; rude otherwise | **recognition only**: one Traps card (marked تُو, §2.8) and one note in `imperative` |

- [practitioner opinion] UrduPod101's tips: "When in doubt, speak formally"; never put جی after your own name ([UrduPod101, *Top five tips*](https://www.urdupod101.com/lesson/all-about-14-the-top-five-tips-for-avoiding-common-mistakes-in-urdu?lp=10)).
- **آپ is grammatically plural** even for one person: آپ کیسے ہیں؟ (to a man), آپ کیسی ہیں؟ (to a woman: -ī, still ہیں). Respected third persons take the plural too: میرے ابو ڈاکٹر ہیں, not میرا ابو ڈاکٹر ہے [inference, standard teaching].
- **Polite requests:** Pakistani Urdu uses two polite forms: **-یے / -ئیے** (بیٹھیے, آئیے, irregular کیجیے, دیجیے, لیجیے, پیجیے) and the **-یں form** (بیٹھیں, آئیں, کریں), the latter very common in Pakistan and slightly less formal [inference]. Both are taught in `imperative` and both are accepted where a polite request is the answer.

**Rules for writers**
- A prompt whose answer has "you" says who is addressed when it matters: "(to a friend)" → تم; no cue or "(politely)" → آپ only.
- **Speaker and addressee gender** change verbs (میں جاتا ہوں / میں جاتی ہوں; آپ کیسے ہیں؟ / آپ کیسی ہیں؟). Every first- and second-person prompt whose answer changes with gender carries a cue: "(a man speaking)", "(a woman speaking)", "(to a woman)", or a named persona (§9.1). Only the cued form is accepted; the other is the distractor.
- Dialogue roles: customer and shopkeeper both use آپ, and the customer may call the shopkeeper بھائی; student and teacher: آپ; new acquaintances: آپ, switching to تم only when the dialogue says they are friends.

| Urdu | `pron` | Use | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|

| وعلیکم السلام | va-alaikum assalām | the reply | `greetings` |
| اللہ حافظ | allāh hāfiz | goodbye; the usual form in Pakistan today | `greetings` |
| خدا حافظ | ḵhudā hāfiz | goodbye; the older form, still heard and always understood; accepted wherever "goodbye" is the answer | `greetings` |
| آداب | ādāb | a courteous greeting, older and more literary; recognition (note) | `greetings` |
| شکریہ / بہت شکریہ | shukriyā / bahut shukriyā | thank you / thank you very much | `greetings` |
| جی | jī | after a name for respect (علی صاحب, امی جی); alone = polite "yes" or "pardon?"; جی ہاں / جی نہیں = polite yes / no | `greetings` |
| معاف کیجیے | māf kījie | excuse me; sorry. سوری is common (note) | `greetings` |
| کوئی بات نہیں | koī bāt nahī̃ | no problem; you're welcome (to thanks) | `greetings` |
| خوش آمدید | ḵhush āmadīd | welcome (to a place); never the answer to thanks (Traps) | `greetings` |
| ان شاء اللہ / ماشاء اللہ / الحمد للہ | in shā allāh / māshā allāh / alhamdu lillāh | "God willing" (plans), admiring something, "fine, thank God" (after آپ کیسے ہیں؟); everyday courtesy in Pakistan, taught neutrally in notes | `greetings`, notes |

- **اللہ حافظ vs خدا حافظ.** Dawn (2009, an opinion column) describes how اللہ حافظ spread in the early 2000s after a 2002 campaign in Karachi and says it had become "the standard farewell", with خدا حافظ drawing puzzled reactions in some circles ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/833553)) [practitioner opinion; the column is argumentative, and 17 years old]. **Decision:** teach اللہ حافظ as the active goodbye, teach خدا حافظ alongside it in the same lesson as an equally correct form, accept both everywhere, and say nothing about religion beyond "both are used; اللہ حافظ is more common today" [inference, §11].
- **Not used:** نمستے and other Hindi greetings; "phir milenge" alone as a goodbye (it is a friendly add-on: پھر ملیں گے).
- Thanking: Urdu speakers thank less than English speakers for small things in the family [inference, note only].

### 2.5 Politeness basics an A1 learner needs

1. آپ + plural verb with anyone you do not know (§2.3).
2. جی after names and titles (علی صاحب, باجی); جی ہاں / جی نہیں.
3. Requests with کیجیے-type forms or -یں forms, softened with ذرا: ذرا پانی دیجیے. Bare commands (دو!) only to friends and children. پلیز is common in casual urban speech (note).
4. Respected people take plural verbs: میرے ابو آئے ہیں.
5. Address terms: بھائی / بھائی صاحب (shopkeepers, drivers, a man your age or older), باجی / آپی (older sister; a polite way to address a woman), انکل / آنٹی (friends' parents, older neighbours), صاحب after a name (خان صاحب) [inference].

### 2.6 Voice of the English text

- Plain, friendly US English, US spelling. A `why` is one sentence.
- Urdu inside English text: in lesson bodies `<b>` around the Urdu, then the romanisation in `<i>` at first mention in that lesson: `<b>شکریہ</b> <i>shukriyā</i>`. In plain-text fields (`why`, `hint`, notes) write the Urdu bare; add the romanisation only when the point is the sound ("ٹ (ṭ, tongue curled back)").
- The engine wraps every Urdu run in English text in a right-to-left isolate (`bidiPass`, built for Arabic, keyed on `RTL_LANGS`, which includes `ur`). Writers still avoid putting an Urdu run directly before a digit or the romanisation in brackets, which reads oddly even when isolated.
- Never add English endings to Urdu words ("two postpositions", not "two کوs").

### 2.7 Urdu text conventions (every file)

- **Urdu letters only**: ی (U+06CC) not Arabic ي / ى; ک (U+06A9) not ك; ہ (U+06C1) for the round h and ھ (U+06BE) only for aspiration (کھ, پھ, ٹھ...); ے (U+06D2) for final e / ai; ۃ only where standard spelling has it (rare). The generator throws on Arabic ي ى ك ه ة and on Arabic-Indic digits ٠–٩.
- **NFC.** Hamza forms are stored composed (ئ ؤ ۂ ۓ). A keyboard that types ی + hamza above (U+06CC U+0654) does not compose under NFC; the generator and (request 1) the matcher turn it into ئ. r12a recommends decomposed hamza forms for Urdu input ([r12a, *Urdu orthography notes*](https://r12a.github.io/scripts/arabic/ur)); they are canonically equivalent, so NFC storage is safe.
- **No ZWNJ, ZWJ, RLM or tatweel** in stored text (the validator rejects U+200B–200F; tatweel only in lesson tables that show joined letter forms).
- **Spaces:** postpositions are separate words after nouns and most pronouns (گھر میں, آپ کا, آپ کو, اُس کا); the fused pronoun forms are one word (مجھے, ہمیں, تمہیں, اسے, انہیں). The future گا / گی / گے is a separate word (کروں گا). Compound verbs are separate words (کھول دیجیے).
- **One spelling per word** (the lint compares whole strings): چاہیے (not چاہئے), لیے (not لئے), کیجیے / دیجیے / لیجیے / بیٹھیے with یے after a consonant, آئیے / جائیے with ئیے after a vowel, گئی / گئے, نئی / نئے, اسٹیشن, سکول (not اسکول), امریکہ, کہہ for the stem of کہنا (کہ alone is *ki*, "that"), یہ for both "this" and "these" [inference: Pakistani spelling varies, §11].
- **Punctuation:** ۔ (U+06D4) ends a statement in card targets, examples and dialogues; ؟ and ، are the Urdu question mark and comma; ! as in English. The generator throws on ASCII . , ? inside Urdu. **No ۔ in accepted answers, tiles or distractors** until request 1 (today's `pracNorm` strips ؟ and ، but not ۔).
- **Numbers as words** in every Urdu string (پچاس روپے, ساڑھے تین بجے), so the voice, the pron and the tiles agree. Western digits appear only in English prompts, notes and lesson tables (prices as shops write them: Rs 250); Urdu digits ۰–۹ are taught once, as a recognition table in `numbers` (§10.7). `targetScript` excludes digits, so a digit-only card would fail the validator anyway.
- **Vowel marks:** none, except (1) in the five script lessons (zabar, zer, pesh, shadda, jazm shown and explained), and (2) the homograph marks of §2.8.

### 2.8 Homographs: the marks the course does write

Unvowelled Urdu has a few very frequent words with two readings. The generator decides most from context and **throws** where it cannot; the writer then adds one mark, which also helps the learner:

| Spelling | Readings | Rule (generator) | Mark when needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| میں | maĩ "I" / mẽ "in" | "I" at the start of a clause (after only connectors and time words: کل, آج, لیکن, کیونکہ, جب, اگر, کیا, یہاں …) or before نے; "in" after a noun or pronoun; it **throws** when the word before is a connector, adverb or postposition (رات کو میں), or when the clause has a first-person verb (ہوں, -وں گا) and no clause-initial میں | مَیں (I), مِیں (in) |
| کیا | kyā "what", question word / kiyā "did" | after نے: "did" only at the end of the clause or before ہے / تھا / گا …, otherwise "what" (آپ نے کیا کھایا؟ *kyā*); "did" before گیا / جاتا (passive); in کیا کیا the first is *kyā*; clause-final کیا with no نے **throws** (کام کیا؟) | کِیا |
| اس, ان, اسے, انہیں, انہوں, ادھر | is / us, in / un, ise / use … ("this" / "that") | always throws unmarked (meaning, not grammar, decides); اس لیے is a fixed entry | اِس / اُس, اِن / اُن … |
| اسی | assī "eighty" / isī, usī | eighty, with a warning | اِسی, اُسی |
| تو | to "then, so" / tū "you" | to | تُو |
| سو | sau "hundred" / so "sleep" | so before رہا / جا… / گیا / سکتا | — |
| ہوا | huā "happened, was" / havā "air" | huā | ہَوا |
| بہتر | behtar "better" / bahattar "seventy-two" | behtar, with a warning | بہتّر (shadda) for 72 |
| پیر | pīr "Monday" / pair "foot" | pīr | پَیر |
| کہ / کہہ | ki "that" / kah stem of کہنا | throws on کہ before رہا, دیا, سکتا…: write کہہ | — |

UrduPod101 tells learners that میں "I" and "in" differ by a dot ([UrduPod101](https://www.urdupod101.com/lesson/all-about-14-the-top-five-tips-for-avoiding-common-mistakes-in-urdu?lp=10)); in standard Urdu they are **the same spelling** (میں), and the course must not repeat that tip [inference, §11]. The marks are accepted in answers but never required (request 1).

## 4. Error zones of English speakers, ranked

**Ranking** [inference]: items in nearly every sentence that tiles and typing can check come first; items with learner data are ranked up; then lesson-local, sound and script traps. Learner data on English speakers learning **Urdu** was not found; the one study with numbers is on English-speaking learners of **Hindi**, whose grammar is the same (Montrul et al. 2019, whose PMC full text was fetched for this profile). Most of this section is [inference] and in the native-check list.

1. **Gender and number agreement** (verbs, adjectives, کا / کی / کے, the speaker's own gender)
   - میرا کتاب → میری کتاب; وہ لڑکی بہت اچھا ہے۔ → وہ لڑکی بہت اچھی ہے۔; میں اردو سیکھتا ہوں۔ (a woman) → میں اردو سیکھتی ہوں۔; بڑا کمرے → بڑے کمرے; پانی ٹھنڈی ہے۔ → پانی ٹھنڈا ہے۔
   - [practitioner opinion] UrduPod101 tip 3, "Watch your gender": learn each noun's gender; male learners copying a female teacher sound feminine.
   - Script point: the -ī / -e endings are ی / ے, two different letters, so agreement is **visible in unvowelled text and in tiles**. Design: strand (a) of §7.

2. **Postpositions after the noun, and the oblique before them**
   - میں گھر → گھر میں; لڑکا کو → لڑکے کو; میرا گھر میں → میرے گھر میں; کمرہ میں → کمرے میں; میں کو → مجھے; لڑکیاں کے لیے → لڑکیوں کے لیے
   - [research-backed] Montrul, Bhatia, Bhatt & Puri (2019), *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:461: native-English university learners of Hindi in Illinois (1–7 years of study) showed "similarly lower command" of case marking than Hindi-dominant speakers ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6433818)). Hindi learners, not Urdu; the grammar is identical [inference].
   - [curriculum convention] McGill teaches "postpositions with oblique forms", possessive *ka* and oblique adjectives in the first term. Design: strand (b) of §7.

3. **نے in the completed past**
   - میں کھانا کھایا۔ → میں نے کھانا کھایا۔; اُس نے کتاب پڑھا۔ → اُس نے کتاب پڑھی۔; میں نے لاہور گیا۔ → میں لاہور گیا۔
   - [research-backed] Montrul et al. (2019): in elicited production the L2 learners used ergative -ne correctly with transitive perfective verbs **76.5%** of the time; they **omitted** it 19.4% of the time, and put it on imperfective verbs in 62.5% of the (only 18) relevant cases; in judgments they accepted omitted and overused -ne about equally. Design: `past` comes late; its agreement half is in strand (a), its pronoun forms (اُس نے, میں نے) in strand (b).

4. **ہے / ہیں / ہوں, and the plural with آپ and respected people**: آپ کہاں رہتا ہے؟ → آپ کہاں رہتے ہیں؟; میرے ابو ڈاکٹر ہے۔ → … ہیں۔ Strand (a).

5. **مجھے-constructions** (like, need, have to, feel, know a language): میں پانی چاہیے۔ → مجھے پانی چاہیے۔; میں چائے پسند ہوں۔ → مجھے چائے پسند ہے۔; میں ٹھنڈا ہوں for "I'm cold" → مجھے سردی لگ رہی ہے۔; "have": things میرے پاس, relatives میرا ایک بھائی ہے, illness مجھے بخار ہے [curriculum convention: McGill: possession constructions, likes and needs, contracted *ko* forms, expressions of compulsion]. Strand (b) when the hinge is مجھے / آپ کو.

6. **The verb at the end, and question words where the answer goes**: میں کھاتا ہوں کھانا → میں کھانا کھاتا ہوں; آپ رہتے ہیں کہاں؟ → آپ کہاں رہتے ہیں؟; کیا at the start for yes-no questions. Not a keyword (easy in short sentences; every `or` item drills it).

7. **Sounds English does not separate**
   - ت / ٹ, د / ڈ (dental / retroflex), ر / ڑ (tap / flap); aspiration: پل *pal* "moment" vs پھل *phal* "fruit"; کھ is aspirated k, not خ; پھ is not f; long and short vowels (the script shows long ones only); nasal vowels (ہاں, ہیں, میں).
   - [research-backed] English-speaking adults discriminate the Hindi dental / retroflex contrast poorly (Werker et al. 1981, as reported in Werker & Tees 1984) (as cited in en-hi).
   - Script point: Urdu marks retroflexion with the small ط and aspiration with ھ, so these contrasts are visible and tile-testable; short vowels are not.

8. **Reading the script**
   - Letters that sound the same: س ص ث (s), ز ذ ض ظ (z), ت ط (t), ہ ح (h): spelling must be learned word by word (no sound strand on these).
   - Non-joining letters read as word breaks (لڑکا is one word); و and ی read as consonants or vowels (و = v, o, ū, au); final ہ read as h (کمرہ is *kamrā*); missing short vowels (بہن *bahan*, سمجھنا *samajhnā*): the pron line carries them.
   - [curriculum convention] Google's fonts team: most Urdu books and newspapers are in Nastaliq, and Urdu readers have long had to use Naskh fonts "which can distort Urdu characters" ([Google Fonts blog 2023](https://fonts.googleblog.com/2023/03/why-are-there-so-few-digital-nastaliq.html)); learners who learn in Naskh need one lesson note that print and signs use Nastaliq (§10.6).

9. **Negation and commands**: میں جانتا نہیں (marked) → میں نہیں جانتا; نہیں کرو → مت کرو (to a friend) / نہ کریں (polite).

10. **Politeness slips**: تم to a shopkeeper → آپ; میرا نام سارہ جی ہے → میرا نام سارہ ہے; دو! to a stranger → دیجیے / ذرا دیجیے [practitioner opinion: UrduPod101 tips 1–2].

11. **False friends and calques** (the Traps deck): خوش آمدید for "you're welcome" → کوئی بات نہیں; میں ٹھنڈا ہوں → مجھے سردی لگ رہی ہے; میں پچیس سال ہوں → میری عمر پچیس سال ہے / میں پچیس سال کا ہوں; کل = yesterday and tomorrow; آم = mango and "common"; تو as a casual "you" (rude); معاف کیجیے to call a waiter → بھائی / سنیے; "I am agree" calque from English-Urdu mixing → میں متفق ہوں / ٹھیک ہے (note) [inference; UrduPod101 gives آم / آم].

12. **Numbers and time**: بیس پانچ for 25 → پچیس (every number to 100 is its own word); تین اور آدھا for 3:30 → ساڑھے تین; 1:30 ڈیڑھ, 2:30 ڈھائی; لاکھ and کروڑ for big sums (recognition).

**Who**

Of those aged 5 and older, **68% speak Urdu at home** and 19% only English; Panjabi 4%, Pashto 2% ([Pew Research Center, *Pakistanis in the U.S.*](https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/fact-sheet/asian-americans-pakistanis-in-the-u-s/markdown)). Many Indian Muslim and Hyderabadi families also use Urdu [inference].
- [curriculum convention] Urdu is taught in few US universities (CAL names UPenn and Johns Hopkins SAIS; Wisconsin's SASLI runs a summer course with *Let's Study Urdu Script* and *Beginning Urdu*) ([CAL](https://cal.org/heritagevoicecollectionurdu); [WISLI Elementary Urdu 2025](https://wisli.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/239/2025/11/Elementary-Urdu-SASLI-2025-.pdf)). Self-study is the main route [inference].
- [inference] Groups, probably in this order: **heritage learners** (Pakistani-American and Indian Muslim families) who understand speech but cannot read the script; **partners and in-laws**; **Hindi speakers and Hindi learners** who want the script and the Urdu words; **people drawn by poetry, qawwali, ghazals, Pakistani dramas and music**; **career learners** (government, NGO, journalism).

**Why** — no survey of US adult Urdu self-learners' motives was found (§11). [inference] family and identity, religion-adjacent culture (without teaching religion), poetry and music, work.

**What they need first** [inference]
1. **Reading the script** well enough to use the course: five script lessons first, as the roadmap plans (McGill interleaves script lessons with units; an app with no teacher must front-load them).
2. **Survival politeness:** السلام علیکم, آپ, جی, شکریہ, اللہ حافظ, معاف کیجیے, introducing yourself.
3. **Family words** with the respect plural (heritage learners and partners): the roadmap's `possessive` carries family.
4. **Numbers, prices and time**; **food, shopping, getting around**.
5. **The مجھے-constructions** early (like, need, have to).

**Check of the roadmap's order** [curriculum convention]: McGill's 2023–24 outline runs meeting people → questions → yes-no questions → greetings → personal information → "there is" → noun types and forms, کوئی / کچھ → adjectives, plurality and respect → infinitive, imperative → postpositions with oblique forms, possessive کا, possessive pronouns → siblings, oblique adjectives; then having, comparatives, superlatives → routine, clock time, present continuous, conjunct verbs → likes and needs, *vala*, price constructions → past habitual, past continuous, *saknā*; then compulsion ([McGill outline](https://www.mcgill.ca/islamicstudies/files/islamicstudies/intro_urdu-hindi_2023-2024_course_outline_1.pdf)). ## 6. Pronunciation aid (`pron`)

**Decision.** `pron: "required"`. The pron is the **en-hi learner romanisation** (§6.2), generated by `tools/target/ur/respell.js` from a built-in **lexicon**; writers never type a pron. `node tools/target/ur/respell.js "<text>"` prints `<text> -> <pron>`; the module exports `respell`, `pattern`, `warnings`, `normalise`, `lookup`.

### 6.1 Why a lexicon, not rules, and not hand-typed prons

- Urdu is normally written **without short vowels**; و and ی are each a consonant or one of several vowels; final ہ is usually the vowel -ā (کمرہ *kamrā*) but in a few words h (the numbers گیارہ to اٹھارہ, written *gyārah*, *bārah* … as en-hi writes them); four letters each for s, z and two for t and h. So the vowels and many consonant choices **cannot be read from the spelling** [curriculum convention: r12a: Urdu text is normally unvowelled and "each letter can represent several vowel sounds" ([r12a](https://r12a.github.io/scripts/arabic/ur))]. A rule generator like hi's or ar's (which reads vowel marks) is impossible on unvowelled text.
- **Hand-typed prons under a strict `pronPattern`** were rejected: the pattern can police letters and format but not whether *bahan* or *behen*, *samajhnā* or *samjhnā* was typed, and five writers would type five versions; the lint would then fail on every shared string.
- **Lexicon tool** (chosen, as the brief prefers; the model is `tools/target/yue/respell.js`): every word the course uses is listed once with its romanisation; inflected forms are **generated** from one entry per noun, adjective and verb (§6.1.1), so the list stays short; longest match over up to four words handles fixed phrases (السلام علیکم, اس لیے, بادشاہی مسجد). An optional `tools/target/ur/lexicon.txt` (owned by the lead; not created) is read after the built-in list and overrides it.
- **It throws** («…», exit 1) on: a word not in the list; an undecidable homograph (§2.8); Arabic code points (ي ى ك ه ة, ٠–٩); Latin letters; ASCII . , ? inside Urdu; digits inside a sentence (a lone number 0–100 is read as its word); invisible characters. **It warns** on generated forms that collide (today: کھیلوں *khelõ* "games" vs *khelū̃* "I may play"; تیرے / تیری "your" vs "swim"), and on اسی read as *assī*. `repron.js` swallows throws, so an unlisted word simply gets no pron until the lead adds it: writers must run every new string and report «…» lines.

#### 6.1.1 Entry format (for the lead)

`tag: اردو = roman` or `اردو = roman`, separated by `;` or new lines. Tags: `m:` masculine noun in -ā (ا or ہ) → ے / وں; `f:` feminine noun → اں / وں after ی, یں / وں otherwise; `M:` unchanging masculine → وں; `a:` adjective in -ā → ے / ی; `v:` verb stem → infinitive, habitual, familiar and two polite command forms, subjunctive, regular completed past, the stem itself (for رہا). `x/y` gives the roman before a consonant / before a vowel: `v: سمجھ = samajh/samjh` → *samajhnā, samajhtā, samjhā, samjho, samjhie*. A written entry always beats a generated form (کہ *ki* beats the stem of کہنا; irregular کیا, گیا, دیا, لیا, پیا are written entries).

### 6.2 The key (exact format; identical letters to en-hi §6.2)

| Urdu | pron | Note for learners |
|---|---|---|
| unwritten short vowels (zabar, zer, pesh) | a, i, u | a like the u of "but" |
| ا / آ (as a vowel) | ā | "father", held long |
| ی | ī, or y, or (in ے-like words) e / ai | the lexicon decides |
| ے | e or ai | final "ay" (without the glide) or the e of "bet" held long (ہے *hai*) |
| و | ū, o, au, or v | v is between English v and w; never written w |
| ب پ ت ٹ ج چ د ڈ ر ڑ س ش ک گ ل م ن ہ ی | b p t ṭ j ch d ḍ r ṛ s sh k g l m n h y | dot below = retroflex (tongue curled back); plain t d = dental |
| ث ص | s | sound like س |
| ذ ز ض ظ | z | four letters, one sound |
| ط | t | sounds like ت (dental) |
| ح | h | sounds like ہ |
| خ غ ق ف ژ | ḵh ġ q f zh | Perso-Arabic sounds; many speakers say k for ق |
| ع | not written | the vowel is written as said: عید *īd*, علیکم *alaikum* |
| ء, ئ between vowels | not written | گئے *gae*, آئیے *āie* |
| letter + ھ | kh gh chh jh ṭh ḍh th dh ph bh ṛh | "with a puff of air"; ph is never f, th never as in "think" |
| doubled consonant (shadda, usually unwritten) | written twice; چھ doubled = cchh | بچہ *bacchā*, اچھا *acchhā* |
| ں, and ن before a consonant in nasal-vowel words | tilde on the vowel: ã ā̃ ĩ ī̃ ũ ū̃ ẽ õ, ai → aĩ, au → aũ | ہاں *hā̃*, ہیں *haĩ*, میں *maĩ* / *mẽ* |
| ن before ڈ / ٹ, ب / پ | ṇ / m where Hindi has them | ٹھنڈا *ṭhaṇḍā* (as en-hi) |
| izafat zer at the end of a word | -e- joining the two words | وزیرِ اعظم *vazīr-e-āzam* |
| ۔ ؟ ، ؛ ! : | . ? , ; ! : attached to the word before | |

**Format rules** (as en-hi): one pron word per Urdu word except fixed entries; **all lowercase**, names included; single spaces; hyphens only inside a word (izafat, و in fixed phrases: *va-alaikum*); NFC (ā̃ ī̃ ū̃ are macron letter + U+0303).

**Where Urdu prons differ from Hindi ones for the same word** (by design, and in the native check):
- **Spelling-driven differences:** the future is two words (کروں گا *karū̃ gā*; Hindi करूँगा *karū̃gā*); میں نے is *maĩ ne* (Hindi मैंने *maĩne*); اس لیے *is lie* (Hindi इसलिए *islie*).
- **Real word differences:** چھ *chhe* (Hindi छह *chhah*), نوے *navve* (Hindi नब्बे *nabbe*), ننانوے *ninnānve* (निन्यानवे *ninyānve*), ڈاکٹر *ḍākṭar* (Urdu spelling has ا; Hindi डॉक्टर *ḍŏkṭar*), اسٹیشن *isṭeshan* (Hindi स्टेशन *sṭeshan*), سر *sar* "head" (Hindi सिर *sir*).
- **Spoken forms where the script gives no vowel:** یہ *yeh*, وہ *voh* (Hindi's pron writes the spelling *yah*, *vah* and teaches "yeh / voh" as a rule; Urdu spelling has no vowel to follow, so the lexicon writes what is said). For a beside h, the lexicon follows en-hi (بہن *bahan*, شہر *shahar*, کہنا *kahnā*, مہنگا *mahãgā*), because Urdu dictionaries vowel these with zabar too [inference, §11].

### 6.3 `pronPattern`

The en-hi pattern, built by `respell.js`'s `pattern()` (so one checker serves both courses):

```
^(?!.*(?:aa|ii|uu|ee|c(?!c?h)|[^āīū]̃|^̃))[abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvyzāīūṭḍṇṛṅñġḵŏãĩũẽõ̃]+(?:-[abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvyzāīūṭḍṇṛṅñġḵŏãĩũẽõ̃]+)*[.,?!:;]?(?: [abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvyzāīūṭḍṇṛṅñġḵŏãĩũẽõ̃]+(?:-[abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvyzāīūṭḍṇṛṅñġḵŏãĩũẽõ̃]+)*[.,?!:;]?)*$
```

It enforces the key's letters, lowercase, no w / x / digits / Urdu script, c only in ch / cch, the combining tilde only after ā ī ū, no doubled vowels, single spaces, punctuation right after a word. It cannot see a wrong vowel: the lexicon is the guarantee. Tested with and without the `u` flag (validate.js compiles with `u`, repron.js without). Every pron in this profile was generated and passes it both ways.

**Collision accepted:** ژ is *zh*, and z + h (مذہب *mazhab*) also gives *zh*; ژ is rare at A1 (ٹیلی ویژن is the only likely word), so no separator was added [inference].

### 6.5 How reliable (self-measured; no native check yet, §11)

- **Against the Hindi generator** (133 words that Urdu and Hindi share: pronouns, postpositions, numbers, family, food, adjectives, verb forms, given in both scripts; `tools/target/hi/respell.js` run on the Devanagari): **3 differences**, all intended (چھ, نوے, ننانوے, §6.2). The run also found **1 bug** (the stem آ was treated as consonant-final, so آئیے was missing), fixed before the count above.
- **Held-out set** (40 everyday sentences written before looking at the lexicon; scratch file, not shipped): first run **7 throws** (4 unlisted words: خط, براہ کرم, تیرنا, ریلوے; 1 missing verb, کہنا; 2 homograph throws: کیا میں اندر آ سکتا ہوں؟ and an unmarked اس), and no wrong output among the 33 that ran, by the author's reading. Fixing کہنا exposed a bug (a verb stem overrode the written entry کہ *ki*); fixed, and the کہ / کہہ rule added.
- **A review of the rules then found two silent errors the held-out set had not hit**: the first میں rule read "I" as *mẽ* whenever a connector or time word came first (کل میں لاہور گیا, لیکن میں نہیں جانتا, کیونکہ میں بیمار تھا, جب میں چھوٹا تھا), and the first کیا rule read every کیا after نے as *kiyā* (آپ نے کیا کھایا؟). Both rules were rewritten (§2.8); those sentences now come out right, and the shapes the rules cannot see now throw. **So the held-out result is weak evidence**: 40 sentences, and two whole classes of error lay outside them.
- **Every Urdu string in this profile** (726 runs in §1–§10) was then run through the generator: every pron printed here matches it; the only unlisted strings are single letters and fragments, intended non-word distractors (توپی, تھیک, ہان, داک), Hindi or variant spellings the course does not use (نمستے, چاہئے, اسکول), and دریافت (named as a word to avoid). Every output containing میں or کیا was read by eye.
- **Sample cards** in §9 (90 rows) and the `greet` strings: 0 throws, 0 pattern failures.
- **Silent-error classes that remain:** (1) a wrong romanisation **in the list itself** (the author's knowledge; the native check of the list is item 1 of §11); (2) homographs the tool does not know (for example عمر *umar* the name vs *umr* "age": only "age" is listed; writers avoid the name), and the میں / کیا rules in sentence shapes not tried; (3) ہوں read as *hū̃* where it is the plural subjunctive *hõ* (شاید وہ گھر پر ہوں), not flagged; (4) generated junk forms for irregular verbs (کرا, جایا) exist in the table but are not words, so they only matter if a writer misspells.
- **No dictionary check was done**: no Urdu dictionary site was fetched in this session (§11).

### 6.6 Card front, vowel marks, and hiding the aid

- **Card front:** the unvowelled Urdu, large, right to left. Under it the pron line, **shown by default for the whole course** (the script hides vowels, and many learners have no voice, §2.2).
- **Hideable:** the romanisation display setting requested by en-hi (engine request 7); suggested prompt after `possessive` (lesson 15). Heritage readers may hide it at once.
- **Practice items carry no pron** (PRACTICE-SPEC), except in the five script lessons, where English prompts may name a sound or word in romanisation ("Build *ṭhīk hai*", "Which letter is *ṭe*?"). Answers are always Urdu script (typed romanisation is accepted by the matcher, §10).

### 6.7 What the script lessons must teach, so the aid can be read and dropped

1. `letters-1`: the macron = long vowel; ا آ و ی ے as vowel letters; the dot below = retroflex in the romanisation and the small ط on the letter; short vowels are not written and the romanisation supplies them.
2. `letters-2`: ڑ = *ṛ*, a flap; non-joining letters.
3. `letters-3`: ch / chh; ḵh and ġ (not kh / gh); letters that sound the same (s, z, t, h).
4. `letters-4`: final ہ is usually *-ā*; ق = q; ک / گ.
5. `aspirates`: h after a letter = a puff of air; the tilde = nasal vowel (ں); zabar, zer, pesh shown once, with the line "real Urdu leaves them out".
6. Every later lesson: "Read the Urdu first; glance down only to check."

## 7. Standing practice rule

**Rule** (the same two strands as en-hi, so the two courses train the same habits):
- **(a) Agreement.** Verb, adjective and کا / کی / کے agree in gender and number: -ā / -e / -ī (ا / ے / ی), ہے / ہیں / ہوں, the speaker's gender in the first person, plural for آپ and respected people; with نے, agreement with the object.
- **(b) Postpositions and the oblique.** The postposition comes after the noun (گھر میں, اسٹیشن کے پاس), and the noun, adjective or possessive before it takes the oblique (لڑکے کو, میرے گھر میں, بڑے شہروں میں); pronouns with کو / نے have their own forms (مجھے, آپ کو, اُس نے).

**Why these two.** They occur in almost every sentence from `hona` on; English has neither; and **both are visible in unvowelled Urdu** (ا / ے / ی, ہے / ہیں, لڑکا / لڑکے, میں / مجھے), so tiles and typed answers can test them with a twin as the distractor. The only learner data (Montrul et al. 2019, §4.2–4.3) shows weakness exactly in case and نے.

**Not chosen:** verb-final order (practised by every `or` item; a why that only says "the verb goes last" does not count); نے alone (one lesson; its halves are in (a) and (b)); مجھے-constructions alone (counted under (b) when the hinge is مجھے / آپ کو).

**The script stage.** The five script lessons have no grammar yet, so their hinges are a **sound strand**: dental / retroflex (ت / ٹ, د / ڈ, ر / ڑ), aspirated / unaspirated (ک / کھ, پ / پھ, ب / بھ), long vowel (a word with and without ا / و / ی), nasal (ں / ن). Tiles are **whole words** (Urdu letters change shape when split, so syllable tiles like en-hi's would show false shapes). Every `or` and `ty` answer is a phrase of two or more words built from (1) words whose letters the lesson has taught and (2) three **sight words** taught as whole shapes in `letters-1`: یہ *yeh* "this", ہے *hai* "is", ہیں *haĩ* "are". `x` holds the **sound twin**, which may be a non-word (توپی): a non-word is wrong by construction, as TARGET-BRIEF requires. Prompts give the romanisation and the English (the script-stage exception, request 11).

Two `or` and two `ty` hinges per script lesson (checked by a script in scratch: every tile's letters are taught by that lesson or earlier, or the tile is a sight word; every answer has two or more words; every answer passes `respell`):

| Lesson | `or` (answer · distractor · keyword) | `ty` (answer · distractors · keyword) |
|---|---|---|
| `letters-1` | یہ ٹوپی ہے · توپی · retroflex; یہ پیٹ ہے · پیت · retroflex | یہ ٹوپی ہے · توپی تپی · retroflex; یہ باپ ہے · بپ بات · long vowel |
| `letters-2` | یہ بڑا ہے ("It is big") · برا · retroflex; یہ ڈبا ہے · دبا · retroflex | یہ دور ہے ("It is far") · ڈور در · dental; یہ بڑی ہے · بری بڑ · retroflex |

(The same pair may serve an `or` and a `ty` item in one set; a writer may swap in another pair that meets the rule.)

**Count** (`minOrder: 2`, `minType: 2`): in every set at least 2 of the 3 `or` items and 2 of the 3 `ty` items hinge on the rule, and their `why` names it with a keyword.

**What counts as hinging** (mechanical):
- **`or`, (a):** `a` contains an agreeing form and `x` its gender or number twin: میری (x میرا), اچھی (x اچھا), ہیں (x ہے), جاتی (x جاتا), پڑھی after نے (x پڑھا).
- **`or`, (b):** `a` contains noun / pronoun + postposition with the noun oblique, `x` the direct form: لڑکے (x لڑکا), میرے before a postposition (x میرا), مجھے (x میں), اُس نے (x وہ).
- **`ty`:** `a[0]` contains the same and `x` holds the twin. The validator rejects an `x` word that rebuilds an accepted answer, so **the other-gender form must not be accepted**: cue the speaker or addressee (§2.3). `a[0]` has at least two words.
- **Script stage:** as above; `ty` answers are phrases of two or more words with two or more sound-twin distractors.
- **`fi` answers are respelled one by one, without their sentence**, so a blank whose answer is the postposition میں would get the romanised twin *maĩ*: write the answer as مِیں (and list میں second). Likewise کیا meaning "did" in a blank: write کِیا.
- **Never a hinge or a distractor:** ی against ئ spellings, یے against ئے spellings, ہ against ح, س against ص (spelling, not sound), anything shown only by a vowel mark, اللہ حافظ against خدا حافظ (both accepted).

Per-lesson notes for writers:
- Every set meets §7: two `or` and two `ty` hinges (sound strand in lessons 1–5).
- The last two items of a set may mix in a neighbour. Preferred: کا / کی / کے agreement, oblique before a postposition, ہے / ہیں with آپ, مجھے-constructions, and (after lesson 25) نے.
- Accepted answers list the natural variants: with and without the subject pronoun in replies; مجھے and مجھ کو; اللہ حافظ and خدا حافظ; کیجیے-forms and -یں forms where a polite request is asked; both orders where both are neutral (کل میں جاؤں گا / میں کل جاؤں گا); the homograph-marked and unmarked spellings both (until request 1, list اِس کمرے میں|اس کمرے میں).
- Lesson bodies follow §2.6. Example rows `{{ex:Urdu|pron|English}}` take the generated pron.

**The script decks are ordinary decks** (no letter-drill machine needed). Rules:
- **Every target is something the voice can read:** a stand-alone letter (the generator gives its Urdu name: ب *be*, ٹ *ṭe*; the Azure voice should say the name [inference, check]), a letter with one vowel mark (بَ *ba*, script lessons only), an aspirate pair that is not a word (بھ *bha*), or a word. Never a joined middle form (it needs tatweel, which the voice reads oddly); joined forms live in lesson tables.
- **Meanings must differ:** "the letter te (dental t)", "the letter ṭe (retroflex ṭ)".
- Letter cards' notes give a word built only from letters taught by then.
- Suggested content: Letters 1 (17): ا آ ب پ ت ٹ ث و ی ے + آپ, باپ, بیٹی, ٹوپی, پتا, تب, ٹیپ. Letters 2 (16): د ڈ ذ ر ڑ ز ژ + بڑا, برا, ڈبا, دور, دیر, روز, بڑی, پر, دو. Letters 3 (18): ج چ ح خ س ش ص ض ط ظ ع غ + صبح, خط, غریب, شادی, سچ, جوتا. Letters 4 (18): ف ق ک گ ل م ن ہ ھ ء + کمرہ, گھر, لال, نام, قلم, فون, کتاب, ماں. Aspirates (18): پھل, پل, کھانا, کہاں (round ہ: no puff), ٹھیک, بھائی, دودھ, گھر, جھوٹ, چھ, تھا, ہاں, ہیں, میں, نہیں, بَ, بِ, بُ.
- **Traps for English speakers** (about 18): about 6 calques (کوئی بات نہیں vs خوش آمدید, میں ٹھیک ہوں, مجھے سردی لگ رہی ہے, میری عمر پچیس سال ہے, مجھے بھوک لگی ہے, مجھے پانی چاہیے); about 6 grammar traps (میری کتاب, میں نے کھانا کھایا, مت کرو, میرے ابو ہیں, میرے پاس گاڑی ہے, لڑکے کو); about 6 words and politeness (کل, آم, تُو, سنیے, جی, اللہ حافظ / خدا حافظ).

## 10. Typing and script

### 10.1 Script and `targetScript`

- Urdu script: 38-odd letters (the Arabic 28 plus ٹ ڈ ڑ ں ھ ہ ے ی ک گ چ پ ژ in Urdu forms), right to left, cursive, short vowels unwritten, aspiration written with ھ.
- **Urdu letters beyond (or instead of) Arabic, with code points:** پ U+067E, ٹ U+0679, چ U+0686, ڈ U+0688, ڑ U+0691, ژ U+0698, ک U+06A9 (keheh; Arabic ك U+0643), گ U+06AF, ں U+06BA, ھ U+06BE, ہ U+06C1 (Arabic ه U+0647), ۂ U+06C2, ی U+06CC (Arabic ي U+064A, ى U+0649), ے U+06D2, ۓ U+06D3, ۃ U+06C3; the Urdu full stop ۔ U+06D4; Urdu digits ۰–۹ U+06F0–06F9 (Arabic-Indic ٠–٩ U+0660–0669; r12a: the Urdu shapes of 4, 5 and 7 differ from the Arabic ones). Sources: r12a's Urdu notes ([r12a](https://r12a.github.io/scripts/arabic/ur)) and the Unicode block; the r12a page does not discuss ۔ or ک / ك, which are the author's knowledge [inference].
- **`targetScript`:** `[ء-غف-ٰٟ-ۓ]`: Arabic-block letters and marks and the Urdu letters, **excluding** punctuation (، ؛ ؟ ۔), tatweel and all digits. An English line with ؟ does not count; a romanised answer added by `pronAnswers` passes the validator through `pronPattern` instead (validate.js accepts `TS.test(a) || (P.pronAnswers && PRON.test(a))`).

### 10.2 Direction (already built)

`RTL_LANGS` in `src/app.html` includes `ur`; `tgtRtl()` turns on `.tgt-rtl` for any right-to-left target in an English interface, `bidiPass` wraps every run of Arabic-block characters in a `<bdi dir="rtl">`, and `.tgt-rtl .built, .tgt-rtl .pool` are right to left. `RTL_RUN` covers U+0600–06FF, U+0750–077F and the presentation forms, which includes every Urdu letter and ۔. Nothing to build for direction; the lead should still look at Urdu tiles and a mixed English / Urdu `why` on a device (§11). Screenshots of the Arabic course (`rtl-ar-*.png` in the repository root) show the same machinery.

### 10.3 The script stage (works with ordinary lessons and decks)

| Lesson | Deck | What the learner can do after it |
|---|---|---|
| `letters-1` | Letters 1 | read ا آ ب پ ت ٹ ث و ی ے in all positions; knows right to left, joining, unwritten short vowels, the retroflex sign |
| `letters-2` | Letters 2 | read the seven non-joiners; see why words have gaps; ڑ |
| `letters-3` | Letters 3 | read ج to غ; knows that several letters share one sound |
| `letters-4` | Letters 4 | read every letter; final ہ = -ā; ھ only for aspiration |
| `aspirates` | Aspirates, nasals and vowel marks | read aspirated pairs and ں; recognise zabar, zer, pesh, shadda, jazm |

- Letter groups follow the roadmap (shape families, as Urdu primers and *Let's Study Urdu Script* do [curriculum convention: the roadmap's basis; the primer itself was not opened]).
- **No handwriting**; joined forms are taught in lesson tables (with tatweel in `<td class="k">` cells only).
- If the letter-drill machine is generalised (request 10), the five decks gain "hear, tap the letter" drills with ت / ٹ, د / ڈ, ر / ڑ, ک / کھ pairs. Nothing else changes.

### 10.4 What a typed answer is

**Default: word tiles** (`tilesDefault: true`). Urdu writes spaces between words, so tiles are words as written: postpositions separate (گھر / میں), fused pronouns whole (مجھے), compound postpositions two tiles (کے / پاس), verb groups separate (جا / رہا / ہے), the future two tiles (جاؤں / گا). No ` | ` separator is needed.

**Typing is optional**:
- **Urdu script from a phone keyboard.** iOS has had an Urdu keyboard drawn in Nastaliq since iOS 11 ([Express Tribune 2017](https://tribune.com.pk/story/1514433/apple-finally-enables-nastaleeq-typeface-urdu-keyboard-ios-11)); Gboard has Urdu [inference: not opened]. Learners with only an Arabic or Persian keyboard will type ي ك ه, which today's matcher rejects (below).
- **Romanisation on an English keyboard.** `pronAnswers: true` with `pronAnswersKeepSpaces: true` and `pronAnswersScript: "[؀-ۿ]"`: `gen.js` respells each Urdu `fi` / `ty` answer (spaces kept) and appends the romanisation; with `romaAlmost: true` the matcher compares typed Latin text to it through `romaLoose`, and a match is "almost" (correction shown). **A homograph the generator cannot decide makes `respell` throw, and `gen.js` swallows the throw, so that answer silently gets no romanised twin**: writers put the marked form in `a[0]` (اِس کمرے میں) and may list the unmarked one second.

### 10.5 What the matcher does today, and what it must fold

Measured with copies of `pracNorm` and `romaLoose` from `src/app.html` (node harness in scratch; for `ur`, `accSame` is plain `a === b`, because `arSame` / `arSlip` are gated on `isAr()`, and the `romaAlmost` branch gives Urdu-script input no "almost" path):

| Typed | Expected answer | Today | Should be |
|---|---|---|---|
| آپ کیسے ہیں (no ؟) | آپ کیسے ہیں؟ | ✓ exact | exact |
| … ہوں۔ typed, or ۔ in the answer | … ہوں | ✗ wrong | exact (strip ۔ U+06D4 and ؛ U+061B) |
| کيسے with Arabic ي, كيسے with Arabic ك | کیسے | ✗ wrong | exact (ي ى → ی, ك → ک: same letter from an Arabic keyboard) |
| شکریه / کهانا with Arabic ه | شکریہ / کھانا | ✗ wrong | almost (ه → ہ or ھ by what the answer has) |
| گیٔی (ی + hamza above) | گئی | ✗ wrong | exact (ی + U+0654 → ئ) |
| شُکریہ (learner added a mark) | شکریہ | ✗ wrong | exact (marks ignored both ways in Urdu) |
| اس کمرے میں | اِس کمرے میں (answer marked) | ✗ wrong | exact (the course's homograph marks are never required) |
| کتاب‌یں with ZWNJ | کتابیں | ✓ exact | exact |
| ۲۵ / ٢٥ | 25 | ✗ | exact (digits folded; course answers avoid digits anyway) |
| اچھی | اچھے | ✗ wrong | **wrong**: ی / ے is the agreement point |
| *aap kaise hain*, *ap kaise hain* | āp kaise haĩ? | ✓ almost | almost |
| *shukriya*, *shukriyaa*, *khana*, *larki*, *kya*, *nahin*, *voh kamre men hain* | shukriyā … | ✓ almost | almost |
| *main theek hoon*, *mein theek hun* | maĩ ṭhīk hū̃ | ✗ wrong | almost: ee → i, oo → u, and *mein* accepted for *maĩ* (Roman Urdu spells "I" both ways) |
| *mujhe pani chahiye* | mujhe pānī chāhie | ✗ wrong | almost: *iye* → *ie* |
| *wo kamre mein hain* | voh kamre mẽ haĩ | ✗ wrong | almost: w → v; final h after a vowel optional (*wo / woh / voh*, *ye / yeh*) |
| *nahi* | nahī̃ | ✗ wrong | wrong (nasal is meaning-bearing; many Roman-Urdu writers drop it, so a reviewer may prefer "almost") |
| *hai* | haĩ | ✗ wrong | **wrong** (ہے vs ہیں is the agreement point) |

**Never fold** (they are the point of items): ی / ے, ہ / ھ (except Arabic ه), ت / ٹ, د / ڈ, ر / ڑ, ن / ں, ا present / absent, gender endings, ہے / ہیں.

### 10.6 Fonts, Nastaliq and line height

- **What the course tells the learner** (one paragraph in `letters-1`, one line in `aspirates`): "Urdu books, newspapers and signs use a flowing, sloping style called Nastaliq. Depending on your device, this app may show the letters in a plainer style called Naskh. The letters and words are the same; only the handwriting style differs, the way print and cursive differ in English." [curriculum convention: r12a: "the underlying characters used are not different for nasta'liq vs. other styles"; Google Fonts: Urdu print is mostly Nastaliq, and Naskh is what Urdu readers have often had to use].
- **Today's engine:** `.tgt-rtl bdi.tx, .tgt-rtl [lang=ar]` get a Naskh-first stack (`'Geeza Pro','SF Arabic','Noto Naskh Arabic','Segoe UI','Noto Sans Arabic',Tahoma`) and line height 1.9; `[lang=ur]` gets direction only; `bidiPass`'s `<bdi>` carries no `lang`. So on an iPhone, which has a built-in Nastaliq face for Urdu ([Apple Community](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/255911679); macOS's pre-installed Nastaliq font is mentioned in [WebKit bug 241717](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=241717)), the app still shows Geeza Pro Naskh.
- **Decision (system stack, request 4):** for `ur`, set `lang="ur"` on `bdi.tx` and on card, tile, option and answer elements, and give `.tgt-rtl [lang=ur]` the stack `'Noto Nastaliq Urdu', 'Jameel Noori Nastaleeq', 'Urdu Typesetting', 'Gulzar', 'Noto Naskh Arabic', 'Geeza Pro', 'Segoe UI', Tahoma, sans-serif` with **line height about 2.3 and taller tiles** (Nastaliq "can lead to a need for quite large line height settings": r12a; the exact value is an [inference] to tune on devices). Names: Noto Nastaliq Urdu (Apple platforms, many Linux and Android builds); Jameel Noori Nastaleeq (the font many Pakistani users install themselves); Urdu Typesetting (Windows, optional Urdu supplemental font) [inference: Windows and Android availability not opened]. Where none is present, the stack falls to Naskh, which is correct and readable. **This is enough for the course**: nothing in the content depends on Nastaliq.
- **The brief's premise and the code disagree.** The brief says no web font can be loaded (strict CSP). In fact `build.sh` sets `style-src 'self' 'unsafe-inline' https://fonts.googleapis.com` and `font-src 'self' https://fonts.gstatic.com data:`, `src/app.html` line 3 already loads Noto Sans KR and DM Sans from Google Fonts, and the service worker caches `fonts.googleapis.com` / `fonts.gstatic.com` responses. **Noto Nastaliq Urdu is on Google Fonts** ([specimen](https://fonts.google.com/noto/specimen/Noto+Nastaliq+Urdu/about); Gulzar too). So the lead can (a) keep the system stack only (default here), (b) load Noto Nastaliq Urdu from Google Fonts only when `en-ur` is the active course (no CSP change), or (c) self-host it under `'self'`. (b) and (c) give every device Nastaliq at the cost of a large font file (size not measured) and heavier layout testing; the native store wrappers' CSP was not checked (§11). Recommendation: (a) for release, (b) as a follow-up after a device test of tiles and the answer box in Nastaliq.
- **Pron line font:** the body font must contain ṭ ḍ ṛ ṇ ḵ ġ and ā̃ (as en-hi §10.6).

### 10.7 Punctuation, digits, accent bar

- ۔ ends statements in card targets, examples, dialogues and `fill` sentences; ؟ ، as Urdu marks; ! as in English. **No ۔ in `a`, tiles or `x`** until request 1. The pron converts them (§6.2).
- **Numbers:** words in all Urdu strings (§2.7). `numbers` shows a recognition table of ۰–۹ with Western digits, and one paragraph: "Pakistani shops, prices and phones mostly use the digits you know (Rs 250); Urdu digits appear in newspapers, books and some signs." [inference: r12a confirms that Urdu "may use ASCII digits, or may use the extended arabic-indic digits"; the shop-and-phone claim is the brief's and the author's knowledge, §11].
- **Accent bar** (`keys`): `ٹ ڈ ڑ ں ھ ہ ے ء`: the Urdu letters a learner with an Arabic or Persian keyboard lacks (Persian keyboards have پ چ ژ گ ک ی). `accents: true` turns missing romanisation marks into "almost". A second key set for romanised typing (ā ī ū ṭ ḍ ṛ ̃) would only matter if `romaAlmost` were off.

## 11. Gaps and unverified claims

**No native speaker has checked this profile or the lexicon.** Check these first, in this order:
1. **Every romanisation in `respell.js`'s lexicon** (about 850 written entries). It is the author's knowledge, cross-checked only against the Hindi generator for 133 shared words. Especially: final ہ as -ā (کمرہ *kamrā*, والدہ *vālidā*, خالہ *ḵhālā*) against the numbers' -ah (بارہ *bārah*); یہ *yeh* / وہ *voh*; a beside h written as *a* (*bahan, shahar, kahnā, dopahar*) against *mehrbānī, mehmān* (written with *e*); *isṭeshan* / *skūl* / *ḍākṭar*; *jumerāt*, *jumā*, *itvār*; *chāy* for چائے; *phuphpho*; *tālib-e-ilm*; the number words 21–99 and their Urdu spellings (ستتر *sathattar*, اٹھہتر *aṭhhattar*, نوے *navve*, چون *chavvan*).
2. **The homograph rules** (§2.8): whether the میں rule (clause-initial = I) and the کیا rule (after نے = did) are right often enough; whether marked forms (مَیں, مِیں, اِس, اُس, کِیا, تُو, ہَوا) look acceptable to Urdu teachers on card fronts.
3. **Spelling choices** (§2.7): چاہیے, لیے, کیجیے / بیٹھیے with یے vs ئے (Pakistani usage varies), سکول vs اسکول, اسٹیشن vs سٹیشن, امریکہ, کہہ for the stem.
4. **Register and greetings:** اللہ حافظ as the active goodbye with خدا حافظ equal (the only source is a 2009 opinion column); شب بخیر and صبح بخیر in the `greet` slots (Urdu speakers greet with السلام علیکم all day; شب بخیر is a goodnight when parting, صبح بخیر is used but less than in Persian [inference]); the -یں polite form as "slightly less formal"; آداب as older; پلیز and سوری.
5. **Traps and calques** (§4.11): خوش آمدید vs کوئی بات نہیں, میں ٹھنڈا ہوں, "I am agree".
6. **The sample cards** in §9 and the prices in §9.1.
7. **The claim about digits** (§10.7): that Pakistani shops and phones mostly use Western digits.
