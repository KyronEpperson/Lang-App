# English speakers learning French: course profile

Course `en-fr`: French for English speakers. Every explanation, prompt, `why`, `hint`, meaning and card note is in English. Answers, examples and card targets are in French. Machine-readable twin: `tools/target/fr/profile.json`, generated from the same data as §6, §8 and §9 so the two files agree. The pronunciation aid is generated by `tools/target/fr/respell.js` (§6); writers never hand-write a `pron`.

**Evidence tags.**
- **[research-backed]**: an empirical study that was opened for this profile. The study is named and its sample given. "abstract only" means only the abstract or a summary page was read. **(second-hand)** means the finding was read in another paper's review.
- **[curriculum convention]**: what textbooks and courses do.
- **[practitioner opinion]**: a teacher's, blog's or guide's stated view, with no data behind it.
- **[inference]**: this profile's own reasoning, including the author's own knowledge of French. Such claims are listed for a native check in §11.

Untagged rules in §6 to §10 are design decisions, not findings.

## 1. Summary

- **Variant:** French of France (`fr-FR` voice), standard spelling, France vocabulary and prices in euros. Québec, Belgian and Swiss forms appear only in card notes, and only where a learner would otherwise be confused (*déjeuner*, *septante*).
- **Register:** *tu* first, for friends and peers; *vous* in lesson 2 as the form for strangers, staff and anyone older, and as the only plural "you". Every shop, café and directions scene uses *vous*.
- **Biggest English-speaker traps, ranked (§4):** gender and agreement (often silent in speech); verb endings matching the subject; silent letters, liaison and the nasal vowels; *avoir* where English says "be"; *être* verbs in the passé composé; articles (*du / de la*, *le* for "in general") and contractions (*au, du*); object pronouns before the verb; *ne … pas*; *c'est* vs *il est*; false friends.
- **Standing practice rule (§7):** every practice set keeps re-testing **agreement**, in two strands: gender and number on articles, contractions, possessives and adjectives; and the verb ending matching the subject.
- **Pronunciation aid (§6):** an English-letter respelling (`bohN-zhoor`, `lay zah-mee`), required on every card and example row and **generated by `respell.js`**. Nasal vowels carry a capital N, and the linking consonant of a liaison starts the next word. On the test sets it was right on about 96% of new strings before fixes; known failures are listed.
- **Lessons:** 28. All 27 roadmap items are kept with their ids. `adjectives` moves up to lesson 6 and `polite` moves into stage 2. One lesson is added: `spoken` ("Spoken French: what you'll hear").
- **Decks:** 18 in 5 groups, including "Traps for English speakers" and "Spoken French". **Typing:** a missing accent is "almost", except on strict items (*a / à, ou / où, la / là, du / dû*, *parle / parlé*).

## 2. Variant, register and voice

### 2.1 Variant: French of France

The roadmap and BUILD-PLAN set this ("French of France"). The reasons that support it for US learners:
- [curriculum convention] US college textbooks teach the French of France as the default. The open UT Austin text *Français interactif*, whose chapter list was opened, is set in France ("Les vacances en France", "Les études en France") ([CUNY French 1001 chapter list, based on *Français interactif*](https://french1001.commons.gc.cuny.edu/chapitres/)).
- [inference] The `fr-FR` voice, the euro prices and France settings must match. Québec French is a large and legitimate variety, but its vowels and vocabulary would contradict the voice.

**Vocabulary rule.** Use the France word. Add a note only where a learner who meets Québec, Belgian or Swiss French would be confused:

| Use (France) | Note on the card | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| le petit-déjeuner, le déjeuner, le dîner | "In Québec: le déjeuner (breakfast), le dîner (lunch), le souper (dinner)." | The same words name different meals [practitioner opinion: [Université Laval](https://www.ulaval.ca/en/student-zone/international-student-community/life-and-culture-in-quebec/french)]. |
| le week-end | "In Québec: la fin de semaine." | Laval page above. |
| faire du shopping, faire les courses | "In Québec: magasiner." | Laval page above. |
| soixante-dix, quatre-vingts, quatre-vingt-dix | "Belgium and Switzerland: septante, nonante (and huitante in parts of Switzerland)." | [inference] A learner who travels there must recognise them. |
| actuellement | "Not actually" (Traps deck). In Québec *présentement* is also common. | Laval page above. |
| un portable, le métro, une voiture | none needed | |

**Pronunciation follows the voice:** the throat *r* (uvular), no distinction taught between *un* and *in* in listening (many speakers in France merge them), and *é / è* kept apart in the aid (*ay / eh*). §6 encodes this.

### 2.2 Roadmap items changed by this variant

None. The roadmap is already "French of France". Changes made for the learners (not the variant) are in §8.

### 2.3 Forms of "you": who is addressed, and when

**Taught first: *tu*.** It is the form of every dialogue between friends, classmates and family, and the form the learner meets most in the first conjugations (*tu es, tu as, tu parles*).

**Vous:**
- Introduced in `greetings` as the form for strangers, shop and café staff, officials, a friend's parents and anyone clearly older (*Comment allez-vous ? Excusez-moi.*), and as the only plural "you" (*vous* to two friends too).
- From `etre-avoir` onward it is drilled as a person with its own ending (*vous êtes, vous avez, vous parlez*), so it is an agreement point under the standing rule (§7).
- `polite`, `modals` and `imperative` use it actively, because that is what the learner says to staff and what a stranger says to the learner (*Vous pourriez… ?, Tournez à gauche*).
- [research-backed, abstract only] Choosing *tu* or *vous* is hard for instructed learners; Dewaele's study of a questionnaire (125 participants) and a conversation corpus (9 native and 52 non-native speakers) frames it as a "tightrope" on which "many never reach the summit" ([Dewaele 2004, *IRAL* 42.4, YorkSpace record](https://yorkspace.library.yorku.ca/items/0ca4a67f-ec6b-446d-8558-44b4cd8de596)). The abstract does not give the learners' first languages or the results.
- [inference] A US adult's first real conversations in France are with staff and strangers, so *vous* cannot wait; the course keeps *tu* for the first conjugation tables because it is the shorter, more frequent form in learner dialogues.

**Default rule for writers:** when in doubt, a learner speaking to an adult they do not know uses *vous*. Using *tu* with a stranger is the costlier error in France [practitioner opinion].

**How examples address people.** Dialogues follow the relationship: friends, family and classmates use *tu*; customer and server, passenger and driver, a stranger asked for directions, a doctor, a boss at first meeting use *vous*. Card notes say which one a phrase uses when it matters ("vous form, to a stranger"). English prompts never say "you (formal)" unless the item tests *vous*; then the prompt says "(to a stranger)" or "(to two friends)" so the answer is decidable.

**Québec note** (one note in `greetings`): "In Québec, *tu* is used much more widely, even with teachers; *vous* is kept mainly for age and respect" [practitioner opinion: [Université Laval](https://www.ulaval.ca/en/student-zone/international-student-community/life-and-culture-in-quebec/french)].

**On:** taught in `pronouns` as the everyday spoken "we" (with the *il* form: *on va*), and recycled in `spoken`. *Nous* stays the form in tables and in typed answers; *on* is accepted wherever the prompt says "we" and the item is not testing the *nous* form (§10).

### 2.4 Voice of the English text

- Plain, friendly US English with US spelling (*color, favorite*). A `why` is one sentence.
- French inside English text: in lesson bodies, put French in `<b>`. In plain-text fields (`why`, `hint`, notes), write French bare. Never add English endings to French words: "the verb *aller*", not "aller's forms".
- **Spaces before ? and !:** French typography puts a space before ? ! : ;. This course writes **no space** before ? and ! in every French string (card targets, examples, dialogues, practice answers, tiles and fill sentences), because a separate "?" would become its own tile and count as a word for the validator, and one rule everywhere keeps the lint's "same target, same pron" comparison clean.
- Sample voice lines:
  1. "French nouns have gender. *Voiture* is feminine, so it's *la voiture blanche*, not *le voiture blanc*."
  2. "Use *vous* with someone you don't know: *Excusez-moi, vous parlez anglais ?*"
  3. "Most final letters are silent: *Paris* ends in an *ee* sound."

## 4. Error zones of English speakers, ranked

**How the list is ranked** [inference]:
1. Items with opened learner data come first.
2. Then items that recur in nearly every sentence and can be checked in typed practice.
3. Then lexical and lesson-local traps.

Learner-corpus counts for English-L1 learners of French were thin in what could be opened (§11). Several studies are known only from abstracts.

1. **Gender and agreement** (articles, adjectives, possessives, past participles with *être*)
   - Wrong → right: *le voiture* → *la voiture*; *la chemise blanc* → *la chemise blanche*; *les chaussures noir* → *les chaussures noires*; *ma amie* → *mon amie*; *Elle est allé* → *Elle est allée*; *son mère* (for "his mother") → *sa mère*.
   - Why: English marks gender only on *he / she / his / her*. French marks it on every article, adjective and many past participles, and much of it is **silent** (*ami / amie*, *allé / allée*, *noir / noire*), so learners get little help from what they hear.
   - [research-backed] Three advanced Anglophone learners interviewed in France (13–19 months of immersion): determiner gender accuracy 91% on feminine nouns vs 95% on masculine; adjective agreement 87% feminine vs 93% masculine. Adjectives were worse than determiners, and feminine worse than masculine; errors ran in both directions ([Herschensohn & Arteaga, "Parameters, processing and feature reassembly in the L2 French determiner phrase", ch. 8 in *The Acquisition of French in Multilingual Contexts*](https://linguistics.washington.edu/sites/linguistics/files/documents/research/guijarro-proofs-herschensohn_and_arteaga.pdf); three learners, no statistics).
   - [research-backed, abstract only] English-L1 learners of French showed native-like P600 brain responses to gender violations, where German-L1 learners did not, so the problem for English speakers is production and memory for each noun's gender rather than an inability to process it ([EUROSLA Yearbook 9, 2009, abstract](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/eurosla.9.06fre)).
   - [research-backed, abstract only] Learners whose first language has no grammatical gender over-assigned the masculine more (Pieters 2018, English and German L1 groups; the summary does not name which group, and English is the obvious candidate) ([*Círculo de Lingüística Aplicada a la Comunicación* 74](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/CLAC/article/view/60523)).
   - [research-backed, abstract only; Dutch L1, not English] 27 advanced Dutch-L1 learners made 519 gender errors in 9,378 modifiers; gender stays a problem "at all levels" ([Dewaele & Véronique 2001, *Bilingualism: Language and Cognition* 4.3](https://yorkspace.library.yorku.ca/items/e2875ad8-4011-4cab-86e8-a726af4490f4)).
   - Design consequence: this is strand (a) of the standing rule (§7). Cards always show nouns with an article that shows gender (*la clé*, and *une école* or *l'école (f.)* for vowel-initial nouns, §9). Feminine forms get their own items.

2. **Verb endings that must match the subject**
   - Wrong → right: *Nous parle français* → *Nous parlons français*; *Ils va* → *Ils vont*; *Je suis parler* (for "I am speaking") → *Je parle*; *On allons* → *On va*.
   - Why: English verbs barely change. French has five or six forms per tense, and three of the *-er* forms sound the same (*parle, parles, parlent*), so the written ending is the test.
   - [practitioner opinion] Standard first-year teaching lore; no learner count was opened.
   - [inference] It is ranked second because it occurs in every sentence from `etre-avoir` onward and is fully testable in typing. It is strand (b) of the standing rule.

3. **Silent letters and liaison**
   - Wrong → right (spoken): *Paris* with an *s*; *ils parlent* with *-ent* sounded; *les amis* said "lay ah-mee" (no liaison) → "lay zah-mee"; *et un* with a linking *t* (liaison after *et* is never made).
   - [research-backed] 12 Canadian Anglophone university learners (A2 to C1) made 86.5% of obligatory liaisons overall, 90% in spontaneous speech but 75% when reading aloud; most misses were determiner + noun and fixed expressions; spelling caused wrong consonants (*grand [d] honneur*) ([Tennant 2015, *Bulletin VALS-ASLA* 102](https://bulletin.vals-asla.ch/article/download/10360/9662/28801)). The same paper reviews other Anglophone studies with obligatory-liaison rates from 60.7% (reading task) to 95% (second-hand: Mastromonaco 1999, Thomas 2004, Howard 2005, De Moras 2011).
   - Design consequence: the aid shows every obligatory liaison and never a forbidden one (§6). Reading aloud is where learners do worst, which is exactly what an aid shown above the text trains.

4. ***Avoir* (and *faire*) where English says "be"**
   - Wrong → right: *Je suis 25 ans* → *J'ai 25 ans*; *Je suis faim* → *J'ai faim*; *Je suis froid* (for "I feel cold") → *J'ai froid*; *Il est froid* (weather) → *Il fait froid*; *J'ai raison* for "I'm right" is correct and must be taught.
   - [practitioner opinion] Listed as a common English-speaker error by a commercial grammar site ([elon.io, "Les erreurs communes pour anglophones"](https://elon.io/grammar/french/errors/overview)). No learner count opened.
   - Design consequence: `etre-avoir` links to the Traps deck; the calques get cards there.

5. ***Être* verbs in the passé composé, and agreement with them**
   - Wrong → right: *J'ai allé* → *Je suis allé(e)*; *Elle a parti* → *Elle est partie*; *Nous avons arrivé* → *Nous sommes arrivés*.
   - [practitioner opinion] Listed first on the same site ([elon.io](https://elon.io/grammar/french/errors/overview)). A University of Reading paper on the passé composé could not be opened (§11).
   - Design consequence: `passe-compose` items with *être* count for the standing rule (the participle agrees with the subject).

6. **Articles: partitive, general, contraction**
   - Wrong → right: *Je voudrais pain* → *Je voudrais du pain*; *J'aime café* → *J'aime le café*; *Je vais à le cinéma* → *Je vais au cinéma*; *le livre de le professeur* → *le livre du professeur*; *Je n'ai pas des frères* → *Je n'ai pas de frères*.
   - Why: English often uses no article for "some" and for things in general; French requires one. *à / de + le / les* must contract.
   - [practitioner opinion] No source opened with counts.
   - Design consequence: contractions are part of strand (a) of the standing rule, because the choice of *au / à la / aux* and *du / de la / des* is a gender and number choice.

7. **Object pronouns before the verb**
   - Wrong → right: *Je prends le* → *Je le prends*; *Je vois elle* → *Je la vois*; *Je donne le à lui* → *Je le lui donne* (A2, recognition).
   - [practitioner opinion] Standard teaching lore. The Spanish profile's evidence (pronouns as the highest error category in English-speakers' Spanish compositions) concerns Spanish, not French.

8. ***Ne … pas* and *pas de***
   - Wrong → right: *Je ne pas parle* → *Je ne parle pas*; *Je n'ai pas une voiture* → *Je n'ai pas de voiture*; *Je pas sais* → *Je ne sais pas* (or spoken *Je sais pas*).

9. ***C'est* vs *il / elle est*, and no article before a job**
   - Wrong → right: *Il est un médecin* → *Il est médecin* or *C'est un médecin*; *C'est mon amie. Il est sympa* (for Léa) → *Elle est sympa*.
   - [practitioner opinion] Listed on the same site ([elon.io](https://elon.io/grammar/french/errors/overview)).

10. **Nasal vowels, *u* vs *ou*, and *r***
    - Wrong → right (spoken): *bon* and *banc* said alike (*on / an* merged); *tu* said as "too" (that is *tout*); *rue* said as *roue*; English *r*.
    - [research-backed] 10 English-speaking and 10 Spanish-speaking learners: the English speakers were less accurate at telling /ɑ̃/ (*an*) from /ɔ̃/ (*on*) and produced them with a smaller acoustic distance; the Spanish speakers had no such difficulty ([Chanethom & Mather 2019, *Journal of Linguistics and Language Teaching* 10, record](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-52d3df9f7bf7444a8b879ac1d823f2a6/Details)).
    - [practitioner opinion] Duolingo's French pronunciation guide singles out *u* vs *ou*, the nasal vowels (*sont / sent*), *r* and silent final letters ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-to-pronounce-french)).
    - Design consequence: the aid gives *ahN* and *ohN* different spellings and *ew* vs *oo* (§6); the Sounds deck has minimal pairs (*le vin / le vent*, *la rue / la roue*).

11. **Adjective position**
    - Wrong → right: *une rouge voiture* → *une voiture rouge*; *un homme grand* (means "a tall man", fine) vs *un grand homme* ("a great man", recognition only); *une petite maison* is right (short group before the noun).

12. **Reflexive pronouns left out**
    - Wrong → right: *Je lève à sept heures* → *Je me lève à sept heures*; *Comment tu appelles ?* → *Comment tu t'appelles ?*

13. **False friends**
    - Wrong → right: *la librairie* for "library" → *la bibliothèque*; *actuellement* for "actually" → *en fait*; *attendre* for "attend" → *assister à*; *sensible* for "sensible" → *raisonnable*; *le collège* for "college" → *l'université* / *la fac*; *la journée* for "journey" → *le voyage*; *blessé* for "blessed" → *béni*; *rester* for "rest" → *se reposer*; *le pain* for "pain" → *la douleur*; *location* for "location" → *l'emplacement*; *demander* for "demand" → *exiger*.
    - [practitioner opinion] Pairs from a commercial school blog ([Lingoda, "17 false cognates in French"](https://lingoda.com/blog/en/french-false-cognates)) and [elon.io](https://elon.io/grammar/french/errors/overview); the French equivalents for the English meaning are this profile's [inference]. No frequency or error data.
    - Design consequence: the Traps deck takes a small high-frequency set; most French–English cognates are reliable, so the deck does not warn against cognates in general.

14. **Calques of English structure (A1 set)**
    - Wrong → right: *J'écoute à la radio* → *J'écoute la radio*; *Je cherche pour une veste* → *Je cherche une veste*; *J'ai téléphoné Marie* → *J'ai téléphoné à Marie*; *Je vais à visiter* → *Je vais visiter*; *depuis* with the passé composé for an ongoing action → present (*J'étudie le français depuis deux ans*).
    - [practitioner opinion] All listed on [elon.io](https://elon.io/grammar/french/errors/overview).

## 5. Learners: who, why, what first

**Who**
- [research-backed] French is the second most studied language in US higher education: 135,088 enrolments in fall 2021, 11.4% of foreign-language enrolments (MLA census as tabulated on [Wikipedia, *List of most commonly learned second languages in the United States*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_most_commonly_learned_second_languages_in_the_United_States)). In K-12 public schools (2007–08) French was 14% of foreign-language enrolments (same page; old data).
- [research-backed] On Duolingo, French is the third most studied language worldwide and the most common second choice, first in 73 countries ([Duolingo 2024 report](https://blog.duolingo.com/2024-duolingo-language-report/)). In a 2017 state-by-state analysis it was the second language in most US states ([Duolingo blog, 2017](https://blog.duolingo.com/the-united-states-of-languages-an-analysis-of-duolingo-usage-state-by-state)).
- [inference] Many US adults are **false beginners** from high-school French: they recognise *bonjour, merci, croissant* and cognates and read more than they can say or hear. The order stays the same for all; a placement check lets them skip `sounds` and `greetings`.

**Why they study**
- [research-backed, global only] For French, Duolingo's top stated reasons worldwide are "support my education", then "just for fun", then "connect with people" ([Duolingo 2024](https://blog.duolingo.com/2024-duolingo-language-report/)). No US-only breakdown was found.
- [inference] US adult motives: travel to France and other French-speaking places; school credit and a return to school French; culture (food, film, music); work (international firms, diplomacy, Canada); family and partners. Unlike Spanish, few learners hear French at work or in the neighbourhood every day, so listening material must come from the app.

**What they need first** [inference]
1. **Reading the spelling aloud.** French spelling hides more than it shows to an English reader (silent endings, nasal vowels, liaison). `sounds` is lesson 1 and the aid is on every card.
2. **Polite survival exchanges in *vous*:** *bonjour* before every request, *excusez-moi*, *je voudrais*, *l'addition*, *où est… ?*, numbers and prices in euros. That is why `polite` moves into stage 2 and `numbers` stays there.
3. **Talking about themselves in *tu*:** name, origin, job, age with *avoir*.
4. **Agreement and verb endings from day one,** because most of it is silent and only typing catches it (§4.1, §4.2).
5. **Hearing real spoken French:** dropped *ne*, *on* for "we", fast liaison. The added `spoken` lesson and deck exist for this.

**Check of the roadmap's order against a US first-year course** [curriculum convention]
- The roadmap follows the CEFR A1–A2 key contents (read through a summary). Its order matches *Français interactif* in outline: pronouns and *être*, gender and articles (ch. 1); *avoir*, *-er* verbs, possessives, *est-ce que*, *ne … pas*, adjectives (ch. 2); *faire*, *aller*, futur proche, prepositions with places (ch. 3); *c'est* vs *il est*, pronominal verbs, comparisons (ch. 4); partitive, *prendre*, question words (ch. 5); contractions, demonstratives, passé composé with *avoir* and *être* (ch. 6); object pronouns, imparfait (ch. 7); *y*, *en*, imperative, *venir* (ch. 8); *qui / que*, modal verbs, passé composé vs imparfait (ch. 9); future (ch. 12–13) ([CUNY French 1001 chapter list](https://french1001.commons.gc.cuny.edu/chapitres/)).
- Changes made for US learners:
  - `adjectives` moves to lesson 6 (*Français interactif* teaches adjectives in chapter 2, with *avoir* and *-er* verbs), so agreement is practised from the first description.
  - `polite` (*je voudrais*) moves into stage 2 next to `partitive`: ordering food is a first-week need, and it is taught as a fixed phrase.
  - `modals` stays in stage 3 although the textbook puts modal verbs in chapter 9: *Je peux… ? / Vous pouvez… ?* is a survival request [inference].
  - `spoken` is added at the end of stage 3.

## 6. Pronunciation aid (`pron`)

**Evidence.**
- No study comparing respelling, IPA and no aid for English-speaking learners of French was found. The choice below is [inference] and [practitioner opinion].
- [research-backed] What the aid must carry is documented: liaison (Tennant 2015: worst when reading aloud, and spelling causes wrong consonants) and the *an / on* contrast (Chanethom & Mather 2019), §4.3 and §4.10.
- [practitioner opinion] A capital N for nasal vowels is an existing convention in English-language French guides: *For Dummies* writes *en France* as "ahN frahNs", *on tombe* as "ohN tohNb" and *nation* as "nah-syohN" ([Dummies.com, spelling and letter combinations](https://www.dummies.com/article/spelling-and-letter-combinations-understanding-spoken-french-173583)).
- BUILD-PLAN §2.5 already names French as the case for "a plain respelling only where spelling misleads English speakers". For an English reader, French spelling misleads in almost every word (silent endings, *eau, oi, ou, u*, nasal vowels, *-er / -ez / -et*), so the aid is on **every** card and example row.

**Options weighed.**

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| (a) English-letter respelling, generated | A day-one learner can read it. Matches what US phrasebooks and *For Dummies* do. Shows liaison, which spelling hides. One script = one string per target everywhere. | Cannot show the French *r*, the exact *u*, or pure (unglided) *ay / oh*. A rule-based generator for French makes some errors (measured below). |
| (b) IPA | Exact; generatable from a dictionary. | US adult self-learners cannot read it, and teaching it costs a lesson [inference]. No dictionary is available offline in the pipeline. |
| (c) Hand-written respelling with a strict pattern | No generator errors. | Drifts between writers; the lint would then catch differences only after the fact. |

**Decision: (a).** `pron: "required"`. The aid is **generated by `tools/target/fr/respell.js`** from the French spelling, never hand-written. `pronPattern` (§6.2) rejects raw French spelling, so a writer cannot paste the target into `pron`.

**Recommended engine default** (decision for the lead): shown in stage 1 ("Sounds and first words"); from stage 2 on, blurred (tap to reveal) with a setting to keep it on. Never shown in practice items (PRACTICE-SPEC). The probe/feature flag `features.pron` must be `true` for `en-fr` (gen.js currently writes `pron: false` for every en-* course).

**What the respelling cannot carry** goes into lesson 1 and card notes: the throat *r*; *ew* is *ee* with rounded lips (not English "you"); *ay* and *oh* are pure, with no glide (*ay* is not the *a* in "day" with a *y* at the end, *oh* is not the *o* in "go"); nasal vowels are said through the nose with no *n* or *ng* at the end; no word stress (each syllable even, the last of a group a little longer); the final sounded consonant of a word runs into a following vowel (enchaînement) even though the aid keeps the words apart.

**What lesson 1 (`sounds`) must teach, so the aid can be read:**
1. The vowel spellings of the aid, each one sound: *ah ay eh ee oh oo ew uh*, with *ew* (say *ee*, round the lips) and *uh* (as in "the" but with rounded lips) as the two new ones.
2. A capital **N** means a nasal vowel: *ahN* (*an, en*), *ohN* (*on*), *aN* (*in, ain*), *uhN* (*un*). Don't pronounce the N. *ahN* vs *ohN* is the pair English speakers merge (§4.10).
3. Most final consonants are silent; *c, r, f, l* (CaReFuL) are usually sounded; *-er* and *-ez* sound *ay*; a final *e* is silent except in one-syllable words (*le, de, je* = *luh, duh, zhuh*).
4. Liaison: in a few fixed places a silent final consonant is said before a vowel, as *z*, *t* or *n*. The aid writes it at the start of the next word (*les amis* = `lay zah-mee`). Never after *et*.
5. Elision: *le, la, je, de, ne, que, ce* drop their vowel before a vowel (*l'école, j'aime*); the aid joins them (`lay-kohl`).
6. *r* is made at the back of the throat; *zh* is the *s* in "measure"; *ny* is the *ny* in "canyon"; *gh* is a hard *g*.
7. No stress marks: French words have even syllables. The aid is a crutch: listen first, and turn it off once the spelling rules are known.

### 6.1 The key (exact format)

**Format**
- Lowercase ASCII letters, plus capital **N** after a nasal vowel. Words separated by single spaces, syllables inside a word by hyphens.
- One respelled word per spoken word, with three exceptions: an elided word is joined to the next (*l'école* → `lay-kohl`); a liaison consonant starts the next word (`lay zah-mee`); fixed phrases from the lexicon (*est-ce que* → `ehs kuh`).
- No stress marks, no punctuation, no digits (write numbers as words; the generator throws on a digit).

**Vowels**

| French spelling | Aid | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a, à, â | ah | la → `lah` |
| é; -er, -ez, -ed at the end; et ("and"); -ai at the end of a future (je finirai) | ay | parler → `pahr-lay`; et → `ay` |
| è, ê, ë; ai, ei; e before two consonants or a sounded final consonant; -et at the end; est, es | eh | mère → `mehr`; merci → `mehr-see`; lait → `leh` |
| i, î, y (as a vowel) | ee | lit → `lee` |
| o, ô, au, eau (both French o sounds) | oh | dos → `doh`; porte → `pohrt` |
| ou, où, oû | oo | vous → `voo` |
| u, û | ew | tu → `tew` |
| eu, œu, œ; unstressed e (le, petit) | uh | deux → `duh`; sœur → `suhr`; petit → `puh-tee` |
| oi | wah | moi → `mwah` |
| ui | wee | huit → `weet` |
| ill after a vowel; final -il after a vowel; y between vowels | y | travail → `trah-vahy`; soleil → `soh-lehy`; voyage → `vwah-yahzh` |
| ill after a consonant | ee + y | fille → `feey` (listed exceptions: ville, mille, tranquille, village) |
| i before a vowel | y | pied → `pyay`; but after two consonants ee + y: bibliothèque → `bee-blee-yoh-tehk` |

**Nasal vowels** (vowel + n or m, not followed by a vowel or a second n / m)

| French spelling | Aid | Example |
|---|---|---|
| an, am, en, em | ahN | France → `frahNs`; temps → `tahN` |
| on, om | ohN | bon → `bohN`; nom → `nohN` |
| in, im, yn, ym, ain, aim, ein; en after i or é (bien, européen) | aN | vin → `vaN`; pain → `paN`; bien → `byaN` |
| un, um | uhN | un → `uhN`; parfum → `pahr-fuhN` |
| oin | waN | loin → `lwaN` |

**Consonants** (only those that differ from English; b d f k l m n p s t v z are as written)

| French spelling | Aid | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ch | sh | chat → `shah` (listed exceptions with k: technique, orchestre) |
| j; g before e, i, y | zh | je → `zhuh`; rouge → `roozh` |
| g elsewhere; gu before e, i | g; **gh** before *eh* and *ee* | gare → `gahr`; baguette → `bah-gheht`; guide → `gheed` |
| gn | ny | montagne → `mohN-tahny` |
| c before e, i, y; ç; s at the start or doubled | s | merci → `mehr-see`; garçon → `gahr-sohN` |
| s between vowels | z | maison → `meh-zohN` |
| qu; q; c elsewhere; k | k | qui → `kee` |
| ph | f | photo → `foh-toh` |
| th | t | thé → `tay` |
| h | (nothing) | homme → `ohm` |
| r | r (the French throat r) | rue → `rew` |
| x | ks; gz in ex + vowel | taxi → `tahk-see`; examen → `ehg-zah-maN` |
| -tion | syohN | nation → `nah-syohN` (but question → `kehs-tyohN`) |
| w | w (loanwords) | week-end → `wee-kehnd` |

**Final letters.** A final consonant is silent, except *c, r, f, l, q, k* (and *-er* is `ay`). With a silent *e* (or *-es*, or verb *-ent*) after it, the consonant is sounded: *grand* → `grahN`, *grande* → `grahNd`. Verb *-ent* is silent; nouns and adverbs in *-ment* and a listed set of *-ent* nouns (*souvent, parent, accident*) are `ahN`.

**Liaison** (written at the start of the next word, only where it is obligatory or near-universal):
- after a determiner: *les, des, mes, tes, ses, ces, nos, vos, leurs, aux, un, aucun, mon, ton, son, deux, trois, six, dix, quels, plusieurs, quelques, vingt, cent* (*mes amis* → `may zah-mee`, *mon ami* → `mohN nah-mee`, *deux heures* → `duh zuhr`);
- after a subject or object pronoun: *nous, vous, ils, elles, on, en* (*nous avons* → `noo zah-vohN`, *on a* → `ohN nah`);
- after *dans, chez, sans, très, quand, tout*, and *bien / rien* only before *à*;
- after *est / c'est* only before *un, une, à, en, au, aux, ici, important, impossible, aussi, encore*; after *suis* before a vowel (*je suis américain* → `zhuh swee zah-may-ree-kaN`); after *sont* before *en, à, au, aux, ici*;
- after the short adjectives *petit, grand, gros, bon, mauvais, beaux, nouveaux, vieux, autres* before a noun (*un petit ami* → `uhN puh-tee tah-mee`; *bon* loses its nasal: *bon appétit* → `boh nah-pay-tee`);
- in inversion and imperatives: *est-il* → `eh teel`, *va-t-il* → `vah teel`, *allons-y* → `ah-lohN zee`;
- never after *et*, never before an h aspiré word (*les haricots* → `lay ah-ree-koh`, *en haut*, *huit*, *onze*), never across punctuation.
- Linking sounds: *s, x, z* → `z`; *t, d* → `t`; *n* → `n`; *f* → `v` only in *neuf heures, neuf ans*.

**Not shown: enchaînement.** A pronounced final consonant runs into the next vowel in speech (*il est* sounds "ee leh"), but the aid keeps such words apart (`eel eh`), because the consonant is already visible and moving it would break the word-by-word reading. Lesson 1 teaches this once.

**Syllables.** One consonant goes with the following vowel; two consonants split, except consonant + *r / l* and consonant + *y / w*, which start a syllable together. An unstressed *e* between two single consonants inside a word is dropped (*samedi* → `sahm-dee`, *maintenant* → `maNt-nahN`), but kept in the first syllable (*petit* → `puh-tee`) and after two consonants (*vendredi* → `vahN-druh-dee`).

**Names and English words** inside French sentences: the lexicon holds the course's names (Emma, Jack, Ryan, Megan, Chicago, Boston, New York). Prefer French names and cities (§9 Localisation). A name not in the lexicon is respelled by the French rules, which is how the French voice will say it.

### 6.2 `pronPattern`

```
^(?:sh|zh|ny|gh|[bdfgklmnprstvwyz])*(?:ahN?|ohN?|uhN?|aN|ay|eh|ee|oo|ew)(?:sh|zh|ny|gh|[bdfgklmnprstvwyz])*(?:-(?:sh|zh|ny|gh|[bdfgklmnprstvwyz])*(?:ahN?|ohN?|uhN?|aN|ay|eh|ee|oo|ew)(?:sh|zh|ny|gh|[bdfgklmnprstvwyz])*)*(?: (?:sh|zh|ny|gh|[bdfgklmnprstvwyz])*(?:ahN?|ohN?|uhN?|aN|ay|eh|ee|oo|ew)(?:sh|zh|ny|gh|[bdfgklmnprstvwyz])*(?:-(?:sh|zh|ny|gh|[bdfgklmnprstvwyz])*(?:ahN?|ohN?|uhN?|aN|ay|eh|ee|oo|ew)(?:sh|zh|ny|gh|[bdfgklmnprstvwyz])*)*)*$
```

What it enforces (syllable grammar: consonants, exactly one vowel unit, consonants):
- Vowel units are only `ah ay eh ee oh oo ew uh` and the nasals `ahN ohN uhN aN`. So a bare *a*, *e*, *o*, *u* (raw French spelling such as *le*, *bonjour*, *une*) is rejected.
- Consonants are only `b d f g k l m n p r s t v w y z` and `sh zh ny gh`. So *c, i, j, q, x*, accented letters, apostrophes and capitals other than N are rejected.
- Every syllable has exactly one vowel unit; words are separated by single spaces; no punctuation.

It is compiled with the `u` flag. It cannot catch a wrong but well-formed respelling (`luh vaN` written for *le vent*); the generator and the lint (same target, same `pron` everywhere) catch that.

### 6.4 The generator: `tools/target/fr/respell.js`

- Usage: `node tools/target/fr/respell.js "Les amis arrivent."` prints `Les amis arrivent. -> lay zah-mee ah-reev`. It exports `respell(text)`, like `tools/target/es/respell.js`.
- Names: a word capitalised inside a sentence is treated as a name, and no liaison is made before it except after a determiner (*Je suis Emma* → `zhuh swee ay-mah`). Abbreviations and symbols with no vowel (*TGV*, *km*) make it stop with an error: write them out.
- How it works: a phrase lexicon (*est-ce que, il y a, s'il vous plaît, quatre-vingts, vingt-deux…*), a word lexicon (about 300 entries: function words, numbers, exceptions such as *femme, monsieur, ville, fils, août, examen, oignon*, loanwords, the course's names), then letter rules to sounds, schwa dropping, syllables, and the liaison table above. A subject *ils / elles* makes the next verb's *-ent* silent; a short list of verbs in *-ment* (*aiment, dorment, ferment…*) is always silent.
- **Measured reliability** [inference, measured on this profile's own test sets; expected forms checked by the author, not by a native speaker]:
  - A held-out set of 167 phrases and words (greetings, family, home, food, travel, health, work, feelings, false friends, spoken French), written before the generator was run on them: **157 right on the first run.** Three of the ten misses were errors in the expected forms. The seven real errors were *vingt-deux* (t not sounded), *soixante* (x said as ks, twice), *quatre-vingt-dix* (phrase not matched through hyphens), *pull* (double l), *foot* (English loanword) and *bibliothèque* (missing ee + y): **about 96%.** All seven are fixed.
  - A second batch of 122 candidate card strings had four real errors (*film*, *je finirai*, *t'as*, *Lyon*), **about 97%**; all fixed.
  - Liaison contexts were under-sampled in those sets. A targeted probe of 12 liaison strings then found **12 errors**: no link before glide-initial words (*les yeux, un oiseau, les huîtres*), none after number phrases (*vingt-deux ans, quatre-vingts ans, deux cents euros*) or elided words (*j'en ai, d'un ami, qu'ils ont*), a wrong link before a name (*Je suis Emma*), and a garbled hyphenated plural (*les week-ends*). All fixed; liaison is the area most likely to hold further errors.
  - After fixes, all 404 test strings pass and match the pattern.
  - Expect **roughly 1 string in 25 to need a fix** in new material, mostly in loanwords, names, numbers in compounds, rare *-ent* nouns and words with irregular final consonants.
- **Spellings it cannot tell apart, and its fixed default:**

| Spelling | Default | Wrong when |
|---|---|---|
| plus | `plew` | "more" at the end of a clause or *en plus* (`plews`; *en plus* is in the phrase list) |
| tous | `too` | pronoun "all" (*Ils sont tous là* is said `toos`) |
| est | `eh` (verb) | *l'est*, "the east" (`ehst`) |
| fils | `fees` ("son") | "threads" (`feel`), rare |
| six, dix | `sees`, `dees` alone; `see z…` / `dee z…` before a vowel | before a consonant (*six livres* is said `see leevr`); the default keeps the *s* |
| cinq, huit, sept | final consonant always sounded | *cinq / huit* before a consonant are often said without it in speech |
| -ent | verb (silent) unless the word ends in *-ment* or is in the noun list | homographs: *président / ils président*, *le couvent / elles couvent* |
| liaison after *est, suis, sont, pas, mais, vais* | only the cases listed in §6.1 | optional liaison in careful speech is not shown; the voice may make it |
| un / in | different (`uhN`, `aN`) | many Paris speakers merge them; the voice may say `aN` |
| o / au | always `oh` | French has an open and a closed o (*porte* vs *rose*); the aid does not separate them |

- **When the output is wrong** (writers): writers do not edit `respell.js`. (1) Prefer rewording (a different name, a number written another way). (2) Otherwise keep the generator's output so the validator stays consistent, and list the string under "pron to fix" in the report. The lead adds a word or phrase entry (`LEX` or `PHRASES` at the top of the file) and regenerates. A phrase entry overrides any rule, so every case is fixable without a code change.

### 6.5 Known limits

- The respelling is a crutch for reading, not a model of the sound: *r*, *ew*, pure *ay / oh*, and nasal vowels without an *n* come from audio.
- Liaison policy is "obligatory only", so the aid can disagree with a careful TTS voice on optional liaisons (*Vous êtes_américain*, *Je vais_à Paris*). Writers should not use sentences whose meaning depends on an optional liaison.

## 7. Standing practice rule

**Rule: agreement.** Every lesson's practice set keeps re-testing that words agree. Two strands:
- **(a) Noun-phrase agreement.** Gender and number on articles (*le / la / l' / les, un / une / des*), contractions (*au / à la / aux, du / de la / des*), partitives, possessives (*mon / ma / mes*), demonstratives (*ce / cet / cette / ces*), *quel*, adjectives and, with *être*, past participles (*elle est allée*). Country prepositions (*en France / au Canada / aux États-Unis*) count, since they follow the country's gender and number.
- **(b) Verb agreement.** The verb ending matches the subject's person and number, including *on* = the *il* form, *vous* (one person or several) = the *vous* form.

**Why these two.**
- [research-backed] Gender and agreement errors persist even in advanced Anglophone learners, worse on adjectives and on feminine nouns (§4.1).
- [inference] Agreement is present in almost every French sentence from lesson 1 (*bonne journée*, *les amis*), so the rule can be met in every lesson, and the JSON has no exemption field.
- [inference] Much French agreement is **silent** (*parle / parles / parlent*, *ami / amie*), so speaking practice and listening do not expose it; only typed and built answers do. That makes it the best use of the practice set.
- Elision (*je → j'*) was considered and left out: errors in it are already wrong answers in every typed item, and it is not a gender or person choice.

**Count** (`minOrder: 2`, `minType: 2`): in every lesson's practice set at least **2 `order` items** and at least **2 `type` items** must hinge on agreement, and their `why` must name it with a keyword.

**What counts as hinging** (mechanical, so a validator can check it):
- **`order`** hinges if the `why` matches the keywords **and either**:
  - `x` contains an agreement twin of a tile in `a`. A twin is the same word in another gender, number or person:
    - articles and contractions: *le / la / les*, *un / une / des*, *du / de la / des*, *au / aux*, and *à* paired with *au* (tiles split on spaces, so *à la* is two tiles: the twin of *au* is *à*);
    - elided forms are glued to their word on a tile (*l'école*, *j'ai*). They do not make twins, because elision does not count for the rule; to test the gender of such a noun, use a tile that shows it (*une école* vs *un*, *cette école* vs *ce*);
    - possessives *mon / ma / mes, ton / ta / tes, son / sa / ses, notre / nos, votre / vos, leur / leurs*; demonstratives *ce / cet / cette / ces*; *quel / quelle / quels / quelles*;
    - adjective and participle forms: *-/-e/-s/-es* (*vert, verte*), *-eux / -euse*, *-f / -ve*, *-on / -onne*, *-el / -elle*, *beau / belle / beaux*, *blanc / blanche*, *allé / allée*;
    - verb forms of the same verb in another person: *suis / es / est / sommes / êtes / sont*, *ai / as / a / avons / avez / ont*, *parle / parles / parlons / parlez / parlent*, *vais / va / allons / vont*;
  - **or** `a` contains article + noun + adjective, and the distractor is the other-gender adjective.
  - Example: `['or', 'the white shirt', 'la chemise blanche', 'blanc', 'chemise is feminine, so the adjective agrees: blanche.']`
- **`type`** hinges if the `why` matches the keywords **and either**:
  - the fallback words hold an agreement twin of a word in `a[0]`;
  - **or** `a[0]` contains an article, contraction, possessive or demonstrative plus a noun;
  - **or** `a[0]` contains an adjective or *être* participle that agrees with its noun or subject;
  - **or** `a[0]` contains a conjugated verb whose subject the English prompt names as anything other than "he / she / it" (*I, you, we, they, you all*, or "(to a stranger)").
  - Example: `['ty', 'We speak French (use nous)', 'Nous parlons français', 'parler · français', 'parle parlez', 'The nous form: the verb ending matches the subject, -ons.']`
- **Accepted answers** list both *nous* and *on* versions when the prompt says "we" and the item does not test the *nous* form; and both *tu* and *vous* versions only when the prompt does not say who is addressed (prefer adding "(to a friend)" or "(to a stranger)").

**Lessons where the rule is hardest, with a hinge for each:**
- `sounds`: the deck gives every noun its article. `order` *les amis* (x: *le*), *la clé* (x: *le*); `type` "the keys" → *les clés* (x: *la*); "the street" → *la rue* (x: *le*). The body introduces *le / la / les* as "the"; `articles` explains it.
- `greetings`: *Bonne journée* (x: *Bon*); "Nice to meet you (said by a woman)" → *Enchantée* (x: *Enchanté*); *Comment allez-vous* (x: *vas*).
- `numbers`: *Il est deux heures* (x: *heure*, *sont*); *vingt et une personnes* (x: *un*); *Les pommes coûtent deux euros* (x: *coûte*).
- `polite`: *Je voudrais une baguette* (x: *un*); *Vous pourriez m'aider ?* (x: *pourrais*); *deux croissants* (x: *croissant*).
- `connectors`: *Je suis fatiguée mais contente* (x: *fatigué*); *Nous sommes en retard, alors nous partons* (x: *partent*).
- `prepositions`: *Je vais au cinéma* (x: *à*, *la*); *J'habite aux États-Unis* (x: *au*); *en France* (x: *au*).
- `imperative`: *Tournez à gauche* (x: *Tourne*) with "(to a stranger)"; *Allons au parc* (x: *Allez*) with "Let's".
- `spoken`: *On est fatigués* (x: *sommes*); *On va au cinéma ?* (x: *allons*). Typed answers here are the standard forms; *on* takes the *il* form.

**Validator keywords** (`standingRule.keywords`, flags `i`; compiled exactly as `new RegExp(keywords, 'i')`, plain ASCII, because `\b` is ASCII-only):

```
\bagree(?:s|ment)?\b|\bmasculine\b|\bfeminine\b|\bgender\b|\bsingular\b|\bplural (?:noun|adjective|article|ending|form|verb|subject)|\bverb ending|\bmatch(?:es)? the subject|\bcontract(?:s|ion)\b|\b(?:je|tu|il|elle|on|nous|vous|ils|elles)[ -]form
```

Use them like this:

## 9. Decks

**18 decks in 5 groups**, 16–18 cards each (about 300 cards). Card shape: `target | pron | meaning | note`.
- `target`: French, no space before ? and !, with an article on every noun that shows its gender: *la clé*, *le chat*. For a noun that starts with a vowel or mute *h* (where *l'* hides the gender), use the indefinite article (*une école*, *un hôtel*) or a phrase that shows it (*la petite école*); if *l'* is needed, the note starts with "(f.)" or "(m.)".
- `pron`: generated (§6). Run `node tools/target/fr/respell.js "<target>"` and paste the output.
- `meaning`: short US English.
- `note`: one English line, or empty for at most a quarter of a deck. Agreement, regional forms and false-friend warnings go here. A spoken-only form starts its note with "Spoken:"; a regional form with "In Québec:" or "In Belgium and Switzerland:".

**Groups, in order:**

| Group | Decks |
|---|---|
| First steps | Sounds and spelling · Greetings and courtesy · About me · People and family |
| Everyday words | Numbers, time and prices · Days, dates and weather · Food and drink · Shopping and clothes · Home and routine |
| Out and about | Around town and directions · Travel and transport · Health and emergencies · Work and school |
| Conversation | Feelings and states · Likes and free time · Questions and connectors |
| Watch out | Traps for English speakers · Spoken French |

**Deck rules for writers**
- Outside "Sounds and spelling", nouns mostly appear in short phrases that force agreement (*la chemise blanche*, *les chaussures noires*), so the standing rule stays visible in review.
- Adjectives describing people: give the form that fits the sentence and name the other gender in the note, saying whether it sounds different (*américain / américaine*: different; *fatigué / fatiguée*: same sound).
- **Sounds and spelling** (16 cards): minimal pairs for the traps in §4.10 (*le vin / le vent*, *le bon / le banc*, *la rue / la roue*, *tu / tout*), silent letters (*le chat, le lit, Paris*), CaReFuL (*le sac, la mer, le chef, le sel*), liaison (*les amis, un ami*), elision (*l'eau (f.)*).
- **Traps for English speakers** (about 18 cards): about 10 false friends (*librairie, actuellement, attendre, sensible, collège, journée, blessé, rester, location, demander*); about 6 calques (*J'ai faim, J'ai froid, J'ai vingt-cinq ans, Il fait froid, J'écoute la radio, Je cherche…*); 2 cards on cognates said the French way (*le restaurant*, *l'hôtel (m.)*: same spelling, silent final letters).
- **Spoken French** (16 cards): every note starts "Spoken:" and gives the written form. Candidates: *Je sais pas, C'est pas grave, Il y a pas de quoi* (said *y a pas de quoi*), *On y va ?, ouais, ça marche, t'as vu ?, Tu viens ?, Bof, Génial !, C'est sympa, Pas de souci, D'accord, Bisous, Salut !, À plus !* Leave out vulgar words and verlan beyond *ouf*, and check every card with a native speaker (§11).

## 10. Typing and script

**Script.** Latin letters plus *é è ê ë à â ç î ï ô ù û ü ÿ œ æ* and the apostrophe. `targetScript`: `[A-Za-zÀÂÄÇÉÈÊËÎÏÔÖÙÛÜŸŒÆàâäçéèêëîïôöùûüÿœæ]`. Every French string must contain at least one character from this class.

**Accent bar** (`keys`): `é è à ç ê ô â î û ù œ ë ï ü`, ordered by frequency in beginner French (é far ahead; ë ï ü are rare: *Noël, maïs, aigüe*). 14 keys against Spanish's 9: **the lead should check the bar's width at 390px** and wrap it to two rows or put ë ï ü behind a long-press if it overflows. Capitals are not on the bar; the matcher does not check case. *ÿ* and *æ* are in `targetScript` only (place names, *L'Haÿ-les-Roses*, *ex æquo*), not on the bar.

**`accents: true`.** As in the Spanish profile, the flag means: French uses accent marks; they are part of the correct answer; the engine's accent-insensitive comparison may grade an answer **"almost"** (accepted, correction shown), never silently correct. **The lead should confirm the engine reads `accents:true` this way.**

**Strict items** (an accent-only difference is wrong, not "almost"). Mark the item `!strict` when the accent is the point or changes the word:
- *a / à* (has / to), *ou / où* (or / where), *la / là* (the / there), *du / dû* (some / owed), *sur / sûr* (on / sure), *mur / mûr*;
- *parle / parlé*, *aime / aimé*, *mange / mangé* in `passe-compose` (present vs past participle);
- *ces / ses* and *c'est / s'est* are different letters, not accents, so they are wrong anyway;
- any `mc` item whose options differ only by an accent.

Everything else (*ecole* for *école*, *fenetre* for *fenêtre*, *tres* for *très*) is "almost".

**ç and œ.**
- ***ç*** folds to *c* under NFD stripping, so *garcon* would be "almost" for *garçon*. Decision: that is acceptable ("almost", correction shown), the same as a missing accent.
- ***œ*** is a ligature, not a letter plus accent, and does not decompose: *soeur* is a different string from *sœur* and would be graded wrong. Decision: **"oe" must be accepted as an exact equivalent of "œ"** (most English keyboards cannot type œ, and French itself writes *oe* in some contexts). **Engine change for the lead:** in the normaliser, map *œ → oe* and *Œ → OE* on both sides before comparing. Until then, writers list the *oe* spelling as an accepted answer for every typed answer containing *œ* (*sœur|soeur*, *œuf|oeuf*). Same for *æ → ae* (rare).

**Apostrophes.** French uses the apostrophe in nearly every sentence (*j'ai, l'école, c'est, qu'est-ce que*). PRACTICE-SPEC says ’ and ' are treated as the same "for English"; **the lead should check that this folding also applies in en-fr** (iOS and macOS keyboards type ’ by default). Authors always write the straight `'` in data.

**Punctuation.**
- The engine strips `. , ! ? ~` and quotes; it should also strip « » (French quotes) and treat a no-break space as a space.
- No space before ? and ! in any French string (§2.4). If a learner types the French space before ?, the normaliser's space collapsing and punctuation stripping must make it equal; **engine check**.
- Hyphens are part of the word (*est-ce que, peut-être, quatre-vingts, grand-mère*) and are not stripped. A learner who types *est ce que* gets "almost" at best; writers add the hyphen-less form to `a` only where it is a common keyboard slip (*est ce que*), not for compounds.

**Case.** Not checked. Authors capitalise the first word of sentences in `a[0]`. Days, months, languages and nationality adjectives are lowercase in French (*lundi, juillet, français, américaine*), and model answers follow that.

**What a typed answer must include**
- Every word French requires: articles (*J'aime le café*, *du pain*), contractions (*au cinéma*), *ne … pas* in written answers (spoken *pas* alone only in `spoken` items marked "(spoken)"), reflexive pronouns (*Je me lève*), elision (*j'ai*, never *je ai*).
- The correct agreement and verb ending (the standing rule), including silent ones (*tu parles*, *elle est allée*).
- Accent marks on strict items.

**What it need not include**
- Final punctuation, capitals, « ».
- Spoken forms: never required (written answers keep *ne*).
- *On* or *nous*: both accepted when the prompt says "we", unless the item tests one of them. Subject pronouns cannot be dropped in French, so (unlike Spanish) there are no pronoun-less variants.

**`fill` and tile fallbacks.** The three wrong options `o` should be agreement or person twins wherever possible (*blanche → blanc, blancs, blanches*; *parlons → parle, parlez, parlent*; *au → à la, aux, à le*), so the tap fallback still tests the standing rule. A fallback word for a `type` item must not be able to rebuild an accepted answer.
