# English speakers learning German: course profile

Course `en-de`: German for English speakers. Every explanation, prompt, `why`, `hint`, meaning and card note is in English. Answers, examples and card targets are in German. Machine-readable twin: `tools/target/de/profile.json`, generated from the same data as §6, §8 and §9, so the two files agree. The pronunciation aid is generated by `tools/target/de/respell.js` (§6); writers never hand-write a `pron`.

**Evidence tags.**
- **[research-backed]**: an empirical study that was opened for this profile. The study is named and its sample is given. "abstract only" means only the abstract or a summary page was read. **(second-hand)** means the finding was read in another source's summary, not in the original. **(not English-L1)** means the learners in the study did not speak English as a first language, so the finding is a pointer, not proof, for this course.
- **[curriculum convention]**: what textbooks and courses do.
- **[practitioner opinion]**: a teacher's, blog's or guide's stated view, with no data behind it.
- **[inference]**: this profile's own reasoning, including the author's own knowledge of German. Such claims are listed for a native check in §11.

Untagged rules in §6 to §10 are design decisions, not findings.

## 1. Summary

- **Course:** `en-de`, Standard German of Germany for US adult beginners, A1 (Goethe A1 inventory), 25 lessons in 3 stages and 18 decks in 5 groups; all explanation in US English.
- **Register:** *Sie* first and *du* in the same lesson (`greetings`), because a US adult's first German exchanges are with strangers; *ihr* from `present`; every English "you" in a prompt carries a cue (§2.3).
- **Biggest traps:** gender and case on articles; verb second, verb last and the sentence bracket; verb endings; stem-changing verbs; *sein / haben* calques; false friends (§4).
- **Standing rule (§7):** every practice set re-tests (a) gender and case of articles and (b) verb position and verb endings; at least 2 `order` and 2 `type` items hinge on it, named by a tested keyword regex.
- **Pron (§6):** required, generated by `tools/target/de/respell.js` (English-letter respelling, CAPITALS for stress, `kh` / `hh` for the two *ch* sounds, `ew` / `ur` for *ü / ö*), never hand-written; reliable on everyday words, weakest on loanword stress (warned on stderr).
- **Lessons (§8):** the 22 roadmap items, reordered (`gender` before `sein-haben`, `negation` after `accusative`, `pronouns` after `dative`), plus `stem-change`, `gern` and `traps`.
- **Typing (§10):** `accents: true`, keys `ä ö ü ß`; *a* for *ä* and *ss* for *ß* are "almost", *ae / oe / ue* are not accepted; capitals are taught but not checked; umlaut pairs that change meaning (*schon / schön*, *fahrt / fährt*) are strict.
- **Evidence:** thin for English-L1 learners of German (§11); no native speaker has checked this profile.

## 2. Variant, register and voice

### 2.1 Variant: Standard German of Germany

The roadmap (`packs/roadmaps.js`, entry `de`) sets "German of Germany", and BUILD-PLAN §1 keeps it. Decisions that follow:
- **Voice and pronunciation:** `de-DE`. The aid (§6) follows the standard pronunciation of Germany, which is what a `de-DE` voice produces: final *-ig* as the *ich* sound (*zwanzig*, *König*), *ch* at the start of *China* and *Chemie* as the *ich* sound, *st / sp* at the start of a word or word part as *sht / shp*, and a vocalised *r* in the ending *-er* (*Lehrer*, *Wasser*). Southern and Austrian pronunciations (*-ik* for *-ig*, *k* in *China*) are not taught [inference].
- **Spelling:** the current (post-1996) rules: *dass*, *muss*, *Fluss*, *ss* after a short vowel, *ß* after a long vowel or a diphthong (*Straße*, *heißen*, *groß*). The *ß* rule is stated on Wikipedia's *German orthography* page ("used only after long vowels and diphthongs") [tertiary source]. Swiss spelling, which has no *ß*, is mentioned once in `sounds` for recognition.
- **Vocabulary:** the common Germany word, with an Austrian or Swiss word in a card note only where a learner would otherwise be confused [inference]:

| Use | Note on the card (only on that card) | Avoid as the taught form |

| das Brötchen | South Germany: die Semmel; Austria: die Semmel | Semmel, Schrippe (Berlin) |
| Tschüss! | South Germany and Austria also: Servus, Ciao | Servus, Pfiat di |
| Guten Tag! | South Germany and Austria: Grüß Gott | Grüß Gott |
| der Januar | Austria: Jänner | Jänner |
| die Kartoffel | South and Austria: der Erdapfel | Erdapfel |

- **Prices** in euros (*zwei Euro fünfzig*), times on the 24-hour clock for timetables and the 12-hour clock in conversation (§9). Dates day before month.

### 2.2 Roadmap items changed by this variant

None. Every roadmap item fits Standard German of Germany. The changes in §8 are made for English-speaking learners, not for the variant.

### 2.3 Forms of "you": who is addressed, and when

German has three forms: *du* (one person you know well), *ihr* (several such people) and *Sie* (one or more people you would call *Herr / Frau* + surname). *Sie* takes the same verb form as *sie* "they" and is always capitalised.

**Decision: *Sie* is taught first; *du* follows in the same lesson.**
- `greetings` opens with *Guten Tag. Wie heißen Sie? Woher kommen Sie?* and then contrasts *Hallo! Wie heißt du?* within the same lesson. The lesson title is "Greetings: Sie and du" (the roadmap says "du and Sie").
- Reasons:
  - [curriculum convention] The Goethe A1 exam deliberately mixes the two: its inventory says the example phrases were chosen so that *du / Sie* (and *er / sie*, *Frau / Herr*) occur in a balanced way, and in the speaking test the examiner addresses candidates with *Sie* while candidates use *du* with each other ([Goethe-Institut, *Prüfungsziele, Testbeschreibung A1 Start Deutsch 1*](https://goethe.de/pro/relaunch/prf/et/Pruefungsziele_Testbeschreibung_A1_SD1.pdf)). So both are A1, and neither can wait.
  - [practitioner opinion] Usage is shifting and people disagree: a Goethe-Institut language column notes that some people in Germany find an immediate *du* fine and others are put off by it, that younger people are more informal, and that *Sie* signals "politeness and respectful distance" ([Kazim 2022, Goethe-Institut "Word!" column](https://goethe.de/prj/ger/en/kre/spk/23004837.html)).
  - [inference] The risk is not symmetric. *Sie* to a friendly peer sounds stiff and is quickly corrected (*Wir können uns duzen*); *du* to an official, a shop assistant or an older stranger can sound rude. A US adult's first real German exchanges (a bakery, a ticket counter, a hotel, an office, a colleague's parent) call for *Sie*. That is the opposite default from the Spanish and French courses, which teach the informal form first; German's social cost of the wrong choice is higher with strangers.
  - [inference] *Sie* is also the easiest verb form: it is the infinitive (*Sie kommen, Sie wohnen, Sie sind* is the one exception to learn). The *du* form, with its *-st* ending, is then the first real verb-ending point.
- **Conjugation tables** list six rows: *ich, du, er / sie / es, wir, ihr, sie / Sie*. *Sie* shares the last row with *sie* "they", with a note: "Sie (formal you, capital S) = the sie (they) form".
- ***ihr*** is taught from `present` onward and drilled lightly (it is universal in Germany, unlike Spain's *vosotros* in the Spanish course): friends and family addressed as a group (*Kommt ihr mit?*), and the *ihr* imperative in `imperative`.

**How examples address people.**
- Strangers, staff, customers, officials, doctors, teachers of adults, older people → *Sie*, with *Herr / Frau* + surname (*Frau Becker*, *Herr Wagner*).
- Friends, family, children, classmates, flatmates, people the learner's age at a party or in a sports club → *du*.
- Colleagues: *Sie* by default in the first dialogue, then a dialogue with *Wir duzen uns hier* to show that many workplaces use *du* [practitioner opinion, from the column above; no workplace data opened].
- **Every English prompt that contains "you" carries a cue**: "(to a friend)" → *du*; "(to friends)" → *ihr*; "(formal)" or "(to a stranger)" → *Sie*. An uncued "you" is allowed only when the accepted list contains all three forms. This is required, because the matcher has no way to know which "you" the learner meant (§10).
- Card notes say which form a phrase uses when it matters ("Sie form, to a stranger. To a friend: Wie heißt du?").

### 2.4 Voice of the English text

- Plain, friendly US English with US spelling (*color, favorite*). A `why` is one sentence.
- German inside English text: in lesson bodies, German goes in `<b>`. In plain-text fields (`why`, `hint`, notes), German is written bare, with its capitals (*der Tisch*, *Sie*). Never add English plural or possessive endings to German words: write "the verb *fahren*", not "fahren's forms".
- Nouns are always written with their capital, also in English sentences (*the word Handy*).
- Sample voice lines:
  1. "Every German noun has a gender. Tisch is masculine, so it's der Tisch: learn the article with the noun."
  2. "In a statement the verb is always the second element. Put heute first, and the subject moves behind the verb: Heute arbeite ich."
  3. "Nice work! Review these cards tomorrow so they stick."

## 4. Error zones of English speakers, ranked

**How the list is ranked** [inference]:
1. Items with opened learner data come first, giving preference to studies of English-L1 learners.
2. Then items that occur in nearly every sentence and can be checked in typed practice.
3. Then lexical and lesson-local traps.

The opened evidence for English-L1 learners of German is thin (§11). Two of the best-known findings (the word-order stages, gender errors) come from learners with other first languages and are tagged **(not English-L1)**.

1. **Gender and case on articles** (*der / die / das*, *den*, *dem*, *einen*, *einem*, *kein-*, possessives)
   - Wrong → right: *die Tisch* → *der Tisch*; *das Frau* → *die Frau*; *Ich habe ein Bruder* → *Ich habe einen Bruder*; *Ich sehe der Mann* → *Ich sehe den Mann*; *mit die Kinder* → *mit den Kindern*; *Ich fahre mit der Bus* → *Ich fahre mit dem Bus*; *das Mädchen … sie* treated by gender, not grammar (*das Mädchen* takes *es* in grammar teaching, though *sie* is common in speech).
   - Why: English has one article, *the*, and marks case only on a few pronouns (*he / him*). German marks gender, number and case on the article, and the noun usually gives no clue to its gender.
   - [research-backed, not English-L1] Beginning L3 learners of German (L1 Spanish, L2 English) overused the most frequent and simplest forms, *ein* and *die*, borrowed gender from translation equivalents, and showed sensitivity to gender cues only for the most frequent cue types and only with definite articles ([Ecke, ch. 6 in Ayoun (ed.), *The Acquisition of Gender*, Benjamins](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/sibil.63.06eck/html); abstract only, no rates).
   - [research-backed, not English-L1] A longitudinal study of two naturalistic learners (L1 Italian and L1 Turkish) traced their gender errors to lexical learning, mapping, processing pressure and parsing, and suggests these are especially hard because German gender is tied to case and number on the same words ([Spinner & Juffs 2008, *IRAL* 46(4)](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/IRAL.2008.014/html); abstract only).
   - [research-backed] 51 L1-English learners of German (third and fourth semester) relied on a first-noun strategy: they read the first noun as the subject and misread object-first sentences such as *Den Hund beißt der Mann*. Training learners to attend to the masculine accusative *den* improved interpretation; after training, about 60% of the processing-instruction group (and about 31% of the traditional-instruction group) scored at or above chance on object-first items ([Henry 2022, *Applied Psycholinguistics* 43(4)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/offline-and-online-effects-of-processing-instruction/3C7757B196C1CE529EB6CB6D0CEEEA94)). So even in the third semester, English speakers do not use the article's case as a cue.
   - Design consequence: this is strand (a) of the standing rule (§7). Every noun on every card has its article (*der Tisch*, plural on the note). Gender is colour-free in text but named in notes. Masculine *den / einen / keinen* is drilled as the one accusative change, and object-first sentences appear from `accusative` on (in its items and in later word-order items; not in the `word-order` lesson, which comes before the accusative), so learners must read the article, not the position.

2. **Verb position: verb second, verb last, the sentence bracket**
   - Wrong → right: *Heute ich arbeite* → *Heute arbeite ich*; *In Berlin ich wohne seit zwei Jahren* → *Seit zwei Jahren wohne ich in Berlin*; *Ich kann sprechen Deutsch* → *Ich kann Deutsch sprechen*; *Ich habe gegessen Pizza* → *Ich habe Pizza gegessen*; *Ich aufstehe um sieben* → *Ich stehe um sieben auf*; *…, wenn ich habe Zeit* → *…, wenn ich Zeit habe*.
   - Why: English keeps subject–verb order after a fronted time or place (*Today I work*) and keeps the verb parts together (*I can speak German*). German keeps the conjugated verb in second place, whatever comes first, and sends the other verb part to the end.
   - [research-backed, second-hand, not English-L1] The best-known developmental sequence for German word order (canonical order, then adverb preposing without inversion, then verb separation, then inversion, then verb-end in subordinate clauses) comes from naturalistic learners whose first languages were Spanish, Portuguese and Italian (Pienemann 1987, as summarised by [Sotillo, Montclair State University course page](https://msuweb.montclair.edu/~sotillos/stages-sequences.htm)). Its example of the adverb-preposing stage is exactly the English speaker's error: *da kinder spielen* ("there children play").
   - [research-backed] 39 adult classroom learners of German acquired three obligatory word-order rules in the same order as naturalistic learners, even though the instruction presented them in a different order; classroom learners got further, faster ([Ellis 1989, *SSLA* 11(3)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/are-classroom-and-naturalistic-acquisition-the-same/793A3CAA86D061E0C1BE4CA19B7D544C); abstract only; the abstract does not state the learners' first language).
   - [research-backed, not English-L1] Norwegian upper-secondary learners of German (L2 English) still produced verb-third order in 20.2% of verb placements (*ich oft bestelle*), more after long adverbials ([Kvifte 2026, *Zeitschrift für Sprachwissenschaft* 45(1)](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/zs/index.php/main/en/article/download/70/30/623); 64 learners). Norwegian is itself a verb-second language, so English speakers are unlikely to do better [inference].
   - Design consequence: strand (b) of the standing rule. Verb position is taught in `word-order` and returns in every set: a fronted time word, a modal, a separable verb or a Perfekt in nearly every `order` item.

3. **Verb endings that must match the subject**
   - Wrong → right: *Er wohnen in Hamburg* → *Er wohnt in Hamburg*; *Du kommen aus Chicago?* → *Kommst du aus Chicago?*; *Wir ist müde* → *Wir sind müde*; *Sie (formal) hast…* → *Sie haben…*.
   - Why: English has one ending (*-s*). German has five spoken forms in the present (*-e, -st, -t, -en*, plus *sein*).
   - [practitioner opinion] Standard first-year teaching lore; no learner count opened.
   - Design consequence: joins verb position in strand (b), as in the Spanish course.

4. **Stem-changing verbs**
   - Wrong → right: *Er fahrt nach Köln* → *Er fährt nach Köln*; *Du esst* → *Du isst*; *Er nehmt* → *Er nimmt*; and the overcorrection *Ich fähre* → *Ich fahre*.
   - [curriculum convention] The Goethe online A1 course gives *essen, mögen, geben, nehmen* their own grammar slot in unit 5 ([Goethe-Institut, *Deutsch Online A1*](https://lernen.goethe.de/deutschonline/A1/PDF/DE/deutschonline_Ihr_Kurs_im_U%CC%88berblick.pdf)). The roadmap folds them into "Present tense"; §8 gives them a lesson.

5. ***sein* vs *haben* in calques; *Mir ist kalt***
   - Wrong → right: *Ich bin hungrig* (possible but marked) → *Ich habe Hunger*; *Ich bin kalt* (= "I am a cold person") → *Mir ist kalt*; *Ich bin heiß* (sexual or "I'm hot stuff") → *Mir ist heiß*; *Ich habe recht* (correct, unlike French-style copying the other way); *Ich bin 30 Jahre alt* is the same as English (good news, said so in `sein-haben`).
   - [practitioner opinion] Standard teaching list; no source opened.

6. **False friends**
   - Wrong → right: *Ich will ein Bier* meant as "I will" → *Ich nehme ein Bier* / *Ich werde…* (*will* = want); *bekommen* for "become" → *werden* (*Ich bekomme ein Steak* = I'm getting a steak); *also* for "also" → *auch*; *Gift* for "gift" → *Geschenk* (*Gift* = poison); *fast* for "fast" → *schnell* (*fast* = almost); *Chef* for "chef" → *Koch* (*Chef* = boss); *aktuell* for "actual" → *eigentlich / tatsächlich*; *eventuell* for "eventually" → *schließlich*; *sensibel* for "sensible" → *vernünftig*; *Handy* for "handy" → *praktisch* (*Handy* = cell phone); *Rezept* (recipe or prescription) for "receipt" → *Quittung*; *Kind* (child) vs "kind" → *nett*; *bald* (soon); *Rat* (advice); *spenden* (donate) for "spend" → *ausgeben*.
   - [practitioner opinion] Pairs from two glossaries: [*Dummies*, German false cognates](https://www.dummies.com/article/german-false-cognates-163082) and the UT Austin *Grimm Grammar* list ([COERLL, false friends](https://coerll.utexas.edu/gg/gr/mis_03.html), 75 pairs). No frequency or error data.
   - Design consequence: the `traps` lesson and the "Traps for English speakers" deck. *will* / *werden* gets its own items in `modals`.

7. **Negation: *nicht* vs *kein*, and the place of *nicht***
   - Wrong → right: *Ich habe nicht ein Auto* → *Ich habe kein Auto*; *Ich nicht verstehe* → *Ich verstehe nicht*; *Ich trinke nicht Kaffee* (meant generally) → *Ich trinke keinen Kaffee*; *Das ist nicht gut* is right.
   - [research-backed, not English-L1] In Kvifte 2026 (above), negation placement was harder than verb placement: target-like placement of non-verb-adjacent negation was rare (about 17% with lexical verbs).
   - Design consequence: *kein* is an article, so it follows the gender and case rule (*keinen Kaffee*), and `negation` comes after `accusative` (§8).

8. **Word order in the middle of the sentence** (time before place; *gern* after the verb; pronoun objects before noun objects)
   - Wrong → right: *Ich fahre nach Berlin morgen* → *Ich fahre morgen nach Berlin*; *Ich gern spiele Fußball* → *Ich spiele gern Fußball*; *Ich mag spielen Fußball* → *Ich spiele gern Fußball*.
   - [practitioner opinion] No source opened. The "time before place" rule is taught as a tendency, never drilled as a hard rule, because it is not one [inference].

9. **Prepositions and their cases** (*mit, bei, zu, nach, von, aus* + dative; *für, ohne* + accusative; *in, an, auf* both ways; *nach* vs *zu* vs *in* for "to")
   - Wrong → right: *Ich gehe nach den Supermarkt* → *Ich gehe zum Supermarkt*; *Ich fahre zu Berlin* → *Ich fahre nach Berlin*; *für dir* → *für dich*; *Ich warte für den Bus* → *Ich warte auf den Bus*.
   - [practitioner opinion] No source opened. At A1: a closed set of "to" places (*nach* + city or country, *zu* + person or shop, *in* + *die Stadt / ins Kino*), and dative after *mit*, *bei*, *zu*, *von*.

10. **Perfekt: *haben* or *sein*, and the participle**
    - Wrong → right: *Ich habe nach Berlin gefahren* → *Ich bin nach Berlin gefahren*; *Ich habe gegeht* → *Ich bin gegangen*; *Ich habe geverstanden* → *Ich habe verstanden*; *Ich habe eingekauft* is right (ge- in the middle).
    - [curriculum convention] Goethe online A1 treats the Perfekt in units 14–15, with *-ieren* verbs and separable and inseparable verbs together ([Goethe-Institut](https://lernen.goethe.de/deutschonline/A1/PDF/DE/deutschonline_Ihr_Kurs_im_U%CC%88berblick.pdf)).
    - [practitioner opinion] The *sein* verbs (movement from A to B, change of state, *bleiben*) are a classic error; no data opened.

11. **Capitalisation of nouns and *Sie***
    - Wrong → right: *ich habe einen hund* → *Ich habe einen Hund*; *Wie heißen sie?* (= "what are they called?") → *Wie heißen Sie?*.
    - [inference] Not checkable by the matcher (§10), so it is taught in lessons and `mc` items, never as a typed-answer penalty.

12. **Pronunciation**
    - **Final devoicing:** *Hund* sounds *hunt*, *Tag* sounds *tahk*, *gelb* sounds *gelp*. English speakers voice the final consonant.
      - [research-backed] 29 English-speaking learners at a North American university: explicit instruction produced significantly more word-final devoicing than implicit exposure on all four acoustic measures ([Stratton 2023, *Journal of Second Language Pronunciation* 9(1)](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/explicit-pronunciation-instruction-in-the-second-language-classro/); abstract only, no rates). So it is worth teaching explicitly, and the aid shows it (§6).
    - **ü and ö** heard and said as *u / o* or *ee / e*: *schon / schön*, *Mutter / Mütter*, *fünf* said as *funf*. [inference, standard teaching lore; one perception study was found but not on the fetch allowlist (§11)]. The aid gives them their own spellings (*ew*, *ur*).
    - **ch:** *ich* said as *ick* or *ish*, *Bach* said as *back*. [practitioner opinion] The Lonely Planet phrasebook describes *kh* as "a throaty sound, like the *ch* in Scottish *loch*" ([Lonely Planet, *German Phrasebook* sample](https://media.lonelyplanet.com/shop/pdfs/german-phrasebook-7-preview.pdf)). The aid separates the two German sounds (`kh` after *a, o, u, au*; `hh` elsewhere).
    - **r:** the American *r* after vowels (*Bier*, *Lehrer* said with a strong *r*). [research-backed] In a conference abstract, naive American listeners often heard German uvular *r* as */h/*; experienced learners heard it as *r* but found the fricative variant harder to produce ([Tepeli 2010, *JASA* 127(3) supplement](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-crossref_primary_10_1121_1_33849593); conference abstract).
    - **Spelling-to-sound:** *w* said as English *w* (*Wasser*), *v* as English *v* (*Vater* = *f*), *z* as *z* (*Zeit* = *ts*), *ei / ie* swapped (*Wein / Wien*, *mein / Miene*), *j* as English *j* (*ja* = *yah*), *s* before a vowel as *s* (*Sonne* = *zon-*), *sp / st* at the start as *sp / st* (*Straße* = *shtrah-*), and silent final *-e* (*Bitte* has two syllables). [practitioner opinion] standard lore; the aid encodes every one of them.
    - **Vowel length** (*Staat / Stadt*, *Miete / Mitte*, *Ofen / offen*): [inference] partly shown by the aid (§6.1 lists which contrasts it keeps).

## 5. Learners: who, why, what first

**Who**
- [research-backed] German had 53,543 enrolments in US higher education in fall 2021, 4.5% of all foreign-language enrolments, ranked fifth (it was fourth in 2016, behind Spanish, French and American Sign Language) (MLA census, as tabulated on [Wikipedia, *List of most commonly learned second languages in the United States*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_most_commonly_learned_second_languages_in_the_United_States)). It fell 33.6% from 2016 to 2021, the steepest drop of any language named, and lost 172 programmes, more than any other language ([Axios 2023, on the MLA report](https://axios.com/2023/11/15/college-students-foreign-languages-enrollment-french-spanish-korean)). In K-12 public schools (2007–08) German was third, with 395,019 enrolments (same Wikipedia page). Fewer college courses mean more adults who learn on their own [inference].
- [research-backed] On Duolingo, German was the fourth most studied language worldwide in 2024, after English, Spanish and French ([Duolingo Language Report 2024](https://blog.duolingo.com/2024-duolingo-language-report/)). The report gives no US rank and no motivation data for German.
- [research-backed] About 40.2 million Americans reported German ancestry (alone or in combination) in the 2024 ACS, 11.8% of the population, and German was the most-reported ancestry in 23 states ([Wikipedia, *German Americans*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Americans), citing the ACS). [inference] Family history is a real motive (genealogy, a grandparent's letters, a heritage trip), but heritage speakers are rare: German has mostly not been spoken at home for generations.
- [inference] Many US adults are false beginners from two years of high-school or college German: they know *Guten Tag, danke, ja, nein, Kindergarten*, and can count, but fossilised English word order. A placement check lets them skip `sounds` and `greetings`, as in the Spanish course.

**Why they study** [inference; no US motivation data for German was found]
- Travel and study abroad in Germany, Austria and Switzerland.
- Work: a posting to a German company or a German employer in the US (automotive, engineering, pharmaceuticals), IT or research jobs in Germany, the military (US bases in Germany).
- Family and heritage.
- Moving to Germany: residence permits, the *Anmeldung*, a flat, and, for some, an A1 certificate (the Goethe A1 exam is the usual first certificate; its speaking test was opened for §2.3).

**What they need first** [inference]
1. **Survival exchanges with strangers, in *Sie*:** greetings, *Entschuldigung*, *Ich hätte gern…* / *Ich möchte…*, prices, *Wo ist…?*, numbers, times, the bakery and the ticket machine. That is why `greetings` leads with *Sie*, `numbers` stays in stage 2, and *möchte* appears as a phrase in the Food deck before `modals` explains it.
2. **Talking about themselves in *du* and *Sie*:** name, origin, where they live, job (no article: *Ich bin Ingenieurin*), family, languages. These are in `greetings`, `sein-haben`, `present` and `possessives`.
3. **Articles with every noun from day one,** because gender cannot be guessed and learners who learn bare nouns fossilise *die* for everything (§4.1).
4. **Verb second from the first fronted word,** because English order (*Heute ich arbeite*) fossilises (§4.2).
5. **Little terminology:** short rules with the glossary terms, many typed sentences.

**Check of the roadmap's order against beginner courses** [curriculum convention]
- The roadmap is built from the Goethe A1 inventory, which is grouped by category, not ordered (roadmap note). The Goethe-Institut's own online A1 course gives an actual order ([*Deutsch Online A1*](https://lernen.goethe.de/deutschonline/A1/PDF/DE/deutschonline_Ihr_Kurs_im_U%CC%88berblick.pdf), 18 units): statement and question word order with personal pronouns (1); gender, articles, plurals and *nicht* (2); yes/no questions, verb position, *haben*, indefinite and negative articles (3); nominative vs accusative and *es gibt* (4); stem-changing *essen, nehmen, geben* and the *Sie* imperative (5); modals and the sentence bracket (6); accusative pronouns and separable verbs (7); possessives (8); *war / hatte* (9); dative prepositions and articles (10); dative pronouns (11); two-way prepositions (12, 17); Perfekt (14–15); *und, oder, aber, denn* (18).
- The open US textbook *Deutsch im Blick* starts with gender, *haben, sein*, nominative pronouns and question words in chapter 1 ([COERLL intro](https://coerll.utexas.edu/dib/pdfs/intro.pdf)); later chapters' grammar lists were not on the pages opened.
- Changes made for US learners (details in §8):
  - `gender` moves before `sein-haben`, so *Das ist der Tisch* and *Ich habe ein Auto* have their articles before the verbs that use them (as in Goethe unit 2 → 3).
  - `negation` moves after `accusative`, because *kein* changes like *ein* (*keinen Kaffee*) (Goethe teaches *nicht* in unit 2 and *kein* in unit 3, with the accusative in unit 4; this course keeps *nicht* and *kein* together, after the accusative).
  - `pronouns` (*ich, mich, mir*) moves after `dative`, because *mir* is a dative form (Goethe: accusative pronouns unit 7, dative pronouns unit 11).
  - Three lessons are added: `stem-change` (Goethe unit 5), `gern` (likes: *gern, mögen, lieber*), and `traps` (false friends and calques, §4.5–4.6).

## 6. Pronunciation aid (`pron`)

**Evidence.**
- No study comparing respelling, IPA and no aid for English-speaking learners of German was found. The choice is [inference], as in the Spanish and Italian courses.
- [research-backed] Explicit instruction on final devoicing beat implicit exposure for 29 English-speaking learners (Stratton 2023, §4.12), so the aid shows devoicing (`hoont`, `tahk`) and `sounds` names it.
- [research-backed] Naive American listeners often hear the German uvular *r* as */h/* (Tepeli 2010, §4.12). The aid keeps the letter `r` and does not try to spell the sound; `sounds` and the voice teach it.
- [inference] German spelling maps to sound far more regularly than English, but the mismatches are exactly the letters English readers misread (*w, v, z, j, ei / ie, eu, s, sp / st, ch, ü, ö*, final *b d g*), and stress is not written. Native words stress the first syllable of the stem, but prefixes, compounds and loanwords break that rule constantly (*verSTEHen*, *AUFstehen*, *HauptBAHNhof* is wrong, *HAUPTbahnhof* is right, *TeleFON*, *UniversiTÄT*).

**Options weighed.**

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| (a) English-letter respelling, generated | Readable on day one. Same format as the Spanish and Italian courses, so the engine, lint and validator need nothing new. Shows stress, devoicing, the two *ch* sounds, *ü / ö*, and every letter English readers misread. | Cannot show the uvular *r*, the vocalised *r*, the exact *ü / ö* vowels, or vowel length for *a, u, ü, ö, ä*. Stress needs prefix, compound and loanword rules plus a word list. |
| (b) IPA | Exact. | US adult self-learners cannot read it [inference]. |
| (c) No aid after a spelling lesson | German spelling is regular. | Stress is not written, and day-one learners misread *w, v, z, ei / ie* in every sentence. |

**Decision: (a).** `pron: "required"`.
- The aid is **generated by `tools/target/de/respell.js`**, never hand-written: `node tools/target/de/respell.js "<text>"` prints `<text> -> <pron>`, and the module exports `respell`. The same German always gives the same `pron`, which is what the lint checks.
- **How a word is analysed**, in this order:
  1. the word list `LEX` (172 entries: loanwords, names, irregular stress, and the few native words whose vowel length the rules get wrong). Forms that add *-e, -en, -n, -s, -er, -es* to a listed word are derived (*Termine* from *Termin*);
  2. number words (*einundzwanzig* = *ein + und + zwanzig*);
  3. **compound heads**: a list of about 90 common last parts (*-bahn, -hof, -zimmer, -karte, -platz, -saft, -arzt, -zahl, -name*…); the word is split there and stressed on the first part (*Kühlschrank* `KEWL-shrahnk`, *Kartoffelsalat* `kahr-TAWF-uhl-zah-laht`);
  4. **separable prefixes** (*ab, an, auf, aus, bei, ein, mit, nach, vor, weg, zu, zurück, fern, los, um*): stressed (*aufstehen* `OWF-shtay-uhn`, *angekommen* `AHN-guh-kawm-uhn`);
  5. **inseparable prefixes** (*be, ge, er, ver, zer, ent, emp*): unstressed, the stem takes the stress (*verstehen* `fehr-SHTAY-uhn`, *Entschuldigung* `ehnt-SHOOL-dih-goong`, *gegessen* `guh-GEHS-uhn`). A stop list keeps look-alikes whole (*gehen, geben, gestern, beste, Abend, einige, anders, Mittel*);
  6. **suffix rules** for loanword stress: final stress on *-ei, -ie, -ität, -ur, -ent, -enz, -ant, -ist, -al, -ell, -ett, -on, -ik, -iv, -ion* (*Polizei* `poh-lee-TSAI`, *Telefon* `tay-lay-FOHN`), *-ieren* verbs on *-ie-* (*studieren* `shtoo-DEE-ruhn`), *-tion* as `TSYOHN`;
  7. otherwise, the first syllable.
- **Risky guesses are reported.** A word of three or more syllables that got the default and ends in a vowel or looks like a loanword writes a "check stress" line on stderr (*Europa, Kanada, Mexiko*). Stdout keeps the `<text> -> <pron>` shape. The writer reports the word, and the lead adds it to `LEX`. A writer never edits a `pron` by hand.
- **How reliable it is.** The expected outputs are the author's own judgment [inference], so a native should spot-check (§11). Lists run in this session, each before the fixes it led to:
  - **Everyday words and phrases** (three lists, about 220 items: sounds, devoicing, prefixes, compounds, greetings, numbers, food, travel). About 18 came out wrong, so roughly 92% were right first time. The wrong ones were compounds whose last part was not yet a head (*Stadtplan, Zahnarzt, Hausaufgaben, Bahnsteig, Postleitzahl, Kopfschmerzen, Kartoffelsalat, Dankeschön*), *über- / unter- / wieder-* verbs that are inseparable (*übersetzen, unterschreiben, wiederholen*), *Unterricht* and *Wörter* (an *r* lost or doubled), *Erdgeschoss*, *Ingenieur*, *Tim* and *Servus*. One wrong word was flagged (*Julia*). Two key problems were also fixed: initial *ch* (*China, Chemie* now soft, *Chor* `k`) and *eh* + soft *ch* (now `ehh`).
  - **A deliberately risky list** (65 loanwords, country and language names, *-ien* words, *-chen* after *s*, vowel-length pairs). 36 were right, 2 right but flagged needlessly (*Kanada, Mexiko*), 2 wrong and flagged (*Europa, Familienname*) and 25 wrong with no warning (*Italien, Australien, Französisch, Italienisch, Amerikaner, Amerikanerin, Adresse, Konzert, Gitarre, Friseur, Cousin, Garage, Jeans, T-Shirt, Steak, Abitur, Andreas, Mittel, Mitternacht, Hochzeit, Häuschen, Radieschen, Krankenschwester, Großeltern*, and *Herren*, broken by a rule added earlier in this session and then removed). So on loanwords the generator is only about 60% right, and the warning catches very little.
  - **The deck word lists of §9** (28 words from the Traps and Life in Germany lists, plus loanwords where *v* is said as v). 17 were right, 2 right but flagged (*Kanada, Mexiko* again), and 9 wrong with no warning (*eventuell, sensibel, Rezept, Mülltrennung, vegetarisch, nervös, Visum, Vanille, tatsächlich*). Loanwords with *v* = v are a known blind spot: the rules say *v* = `f`, so every such word needs a `LEX` entry.
  - **Names** (27 names and places for §9): 4 wrong (*Ben, Leon, Rhein, Ostsee*).
  - All of the above are now fixed (in `LEX`, the head list, the stop list or the rules). After the fixes, an 81-phrase regression list (every category in the brief: *ch* after front and back vowels, devoicing, *ei / ie*, *eu / äu*, *z, w / v*, *s* before a vowel, *sp / st*, *-ig*, unstressed *-e / -er / -en*, separable and inseparable prefixes, compounds, sentences) and the 90 sample cards of §9 come out as expected, and every output and every `LEX` value passes `pronPattern`.
  - **What this means.** On native A1 words the aid is reliable; the risk is concentrated in loanwords, names, compounds whose last part is not in the head list, and *über- / unter- / wieder- / durch- / um-* verbs (separable or inseparable by meaning). The lint cannot catch a wrong but consistent stress; only a native read can (§11).
- **Homographs** get one `pron` per spelling. Only one case-only pair is handled: *Weg* `vayk` "way" (capital W) vs *weg* "away" `vehk`. Do not use *umfahren*, *übersetzen* "ferry across" or *modern* "to rot", whose other reading would need another `pron`.
- **Recommended engine default** (as in es and it): shown in the first stage, then blurred (tap to reveal) after `sounds` plus three lessons; the learner can turn it back on. Practice items have no aid, as PRACTICE-SPEC requires.

**What the respelling does not show, on purpose** (each goes in `sounds` and card notes instead):
- **the German *r***: always written `r`. After a long vowel at the end of a syllable it is vocalised (*der, mir, Uhr* sound like `dayuh, meeuh, oouh`), but writing that would make `dayr` and `day` look alike. Unstressed *-er* is written `uh` (*Lehrer* `LAY-ruh`), which is the one place the aid shows the vocalised *r*;
- **vowel length for a, u, ü, ö, ä**: `ah, oo, ew, ur, eh` cover both long and short (*Staat / Stadt* are both `shtaht`; *Ofen / offen* are `OH-fuhn / AWF-uhn` and *Miete / Mitte* are `MEE-tuh / MIT-uh`, because *e, i, o* do have two spellings in the key);
- **the exact *ü* and *ö* vowels**: `ew` and `ur` are crutches; the voice teaches them;
- **the glottal stop** before a vowel at the start of a word or stem (*be|enden*), and aspiration of *p t k*.

**What lesson 1 (`sounds`) must teach, so the aid can be read:**
1. CAPITALS mark the stressed syllable. Most German words stress the first syllable, but *be-, ver-, ge-, er-, ent-* never take the stress, and many loanwords stress the last.
2. The vowel spellings of the key, especially `ay` (long e, as in *day* without the glide), `ew` (*ü*: say ee with rounded lips), `ur` (*ö*: say ay with rounded lips; the r is not pronounced), `uh` (unstressed *-e, -en, -er*), `ai` (*ei*), `oy` (*eu, äu*), `ow` (*au*).
3. The two *ch* sounds: `kh` (throaty, after *a, o, u, au*) and `hh` (soft, everywhere else, and in *-ig*). Neither is *k* or *sh*.
4. Letter values: *w* = `v`, *v* = `f`, *z* = `ts`, *j* = `y`, *s* before a vowel = `z`, *sp / st* at the start of a word or word part = `shp / sht`, *ß* = `s`.
5. Final *b, d, g* sound `p, t, k` (*Hund* `hoont`); the final *-e* is always said (*Bitte* has two syllables).
6. *r* is written `r` but said in the throat (or as a light `uh` after a vowel). Listen first; the aid is a crutch to turn off once the spelling rules are known.

### 6.1 The key (exact format)

**Format**
- Lowercase ASCII letters. Words are separated by single spaces; syllables inside a word by hyphens.
- In every word of two or more syllables, exactly one syllable, the stressed one, is in CAPITALS. One-syllable words are all lowercase (*der* → `dayr`, *ich* → `ihh`).
- No punctuation, no umlauts, no ß, no apostrophes, no digits. Write numbers as words. *geht's* is respelled as one word (`gayts`).
- Abbreviations of two to five capitals are spelled out letter by letter (*USA* → `oo-ehs-AH`, *WC* → `vay-TSAY`).
- A hyphenated German word is respelled part by part, joined with hyphens, stressed on the first part (*T-Shirt* is in `LEX`: `TEE-shurt`).

**Vowels**

**Syllable breaks**
- One consonant goes with the next vowel (`LAY-buhn`), except one that closes a short vowel: *ch, sch, ng, ck, tz, ss* and double consonants stay with the vowel before them (`MAHKH-uh`, `KEWHH-uh`, `VAHS-uh`). So *ch* always closes its syllable after a vowel; this is a convention of the key, not a claim about German syllables.
- Two consonants split, except *pr, br, tr, dr, kr, gr, fr, pl, bl, kl, gl, fl, kn, schl, schr, schm, schn, schw, qu*, which start the next syllable.
- Compound and prefix boundaries are always syllable breaks (`HOWPT-bahn-hohf`, `AHN-guh-kawm-uhn`).

**Names and English words** inside German sentences: use only the names in §9 (all checked with the generator). A new name is run through the generator first; if the result is wrong, the lead adds it to `LEX`. English-speaking characters (Emily, Ryan, Megan) are in `LEX` with an English-style respelling.

### 6.2 `pronPattern`

```
^(?!.*[cC](?![hH]))(?!.*[aA](?![hHiIyYwW]))(?!.*(?<![eE])[eE](?![hHeEwW]))(?!.*(?<![oO])[oO](?![hHoOyYwW]))(?!.*[uU](?![hHrR]))(?:[abcdefghijklmnoprstuvwyz]+|(?:[abcdefghijklmnoprstuvwyz]+-)+[ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPRSTUVWYZ]+(?:-[abcdefghijklmnoprstuvwyz]+)*|[ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPRSTUVWYZ]+(?:-[abcdefghijklmnoprstuvwyz]+)+)(?: (?:[abcdefghijklmnoprstuvwyz]+|(?:[abcdefghijklmnoprstuvwyz]+-)+[ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPRSTUVWYZ]+(?:-[abcdefghijklmnoprstuvwyz]+)*|[ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPRSTUVWYZ]+(?:-[abcdefghijklmnoprstuvwyz]+)+))*$
```

It is the Spanish pilot's pattern with a German letter set:
- Letters: *a–z* except *q* and *x*. *j* and *v* are allowed (`djawp`, `fehr`), *u* is allowed only in `uh` and `ur`.
- *c* only inside *ch*.
- *a* only in `ah, ai, ay, aw`; *e* only in `eh, ee, ew`; *o* only in `oh, oo, oy, ow`; *u* only before *h* or *r*.
- Every multi-syllable word has exactly one CAPITAL syllable; one-syllable words are lowercase.
- No punctuation, and single spaces only.

It is compiled with the `u` flag. Tested: it accepts all 24 worked examples below, the 90 sample cards in §9, the 81-phrase regression list, about 150 more everyday words, and every value in `LEX`. Raw German nearly always fails it (a bare *a, o, u*, an umlaut, a *ß*, or no capital in a long word), but **raw *ich* and *durch* pass** as they stand, because their spelling is legal in the key, so the pattern alone cannot prove a `pron` was generated. It cannot catch a wrong but well-formed respelling; the generator and the lint do.

## 7. Standing practice rule

**Rule: articles and verbs.** Every lesson's practice set keeps re-testing two strands:
- **(a) Gender and case on articles.** *der / die / das* and the plural *die*; the case forms *den, dem, einen, einem, einer, keinen*; possessives (*mein / meine / meinen*); and the pronoun forms that change with case (*ich / mich / mir*, *er / ihn / ihm*).
- **(b) Verb position and verb endings.** The conjugated verb second in a statement, first in a yes/no question, last after *wenn*; the sentence bracket (modal + infinitive, separable prefix, Perfekt participle at the end); and the ending that matches the subject (*-e, -st, -t, -en*, *Sie* = infinitive, stem changes in the *du* and *er / sie / es* forms).

**Why these two.**
- [research-backed] Even third- and fourth-semester English speakers read the first noun as the subject and ignore the case of the article (Henry 2022, §4.1).
- [research-backed, not English-L1] Gender errors persist and are tied to case (Spinner & Juffs 2008; Ecke, §4.1). Verb-third order (*Heute ich arbeite*) is a stage every learner passes through and still about 20% of verb placements for upper-secondary Norwegian learners (Pienemann via Sotillo; Kvifte 2026, §4.2).
- [inference] Both are in essentially every German sentence from lesson 1 (*der Hund*, *Wie heißen Sie?*), so the rule can be met in every lesson, which matters because the JSON has no exemption field.
- [inference] They pair like the Spanish and Italian rules (agreement + verb endings), so writers and reviewers carry the same habit across courses.
- **Not the standing rule:** *sein / haben* calques, *nicht / kein*, prepositions, false friends. They are lesson-local or artificial in `numbers` and `sounds`, and are recycled as "mixed neighbour" items instead.

**Count** (`minOrder: 2`, `minType: 2`): in every lesson's practice set, at least **2 `order` items** and at least **2 `type` items** must hinge on the rule, and their `why` must name it with a keyword. A 14-item set has 3 `order` and 3 `type` items (TARGET-BRIEF), so two of each three.

**What counts as hinging** (mechanical, so a reviewer can check it):
- **`order`** hinges if the `why` matches the keywords and **either**:
  - the distractor tiles hold a **twin** of a tile in the answer: the same word in another gender, case or person:
    - articles: *der / die / das / den / dem / des*, *ein / eine / einen / einem / einer*, *kein-*, *mein- / dein- / sein- / ihr- / unser- / Ihr-*;
    - pronouns: *ich / mich / mir*, *du / dich / dir*, *er / ihn / ihm*, *sie / ihr*, *Sie / Ihnen*;
    - verb forms of the same verb in another person: *bin / bist / ist / sind / seid*, *habe / hast / hat / haben / habt*, *wohne / wohnst / wohnt / wohnen*, *fahre / fährt*, *kann / können*;
  - **or** the answer's word order is the point: a fronted non-subject before the verb (*Heute arbeite ich*), a verb-first question, a bracket (*möchte … fahren*, *stehe … auf*, *habe … gegessen*) or a *wenn* clause. The wrong order can already be built from the answer tiles, so the distractor is still a twin (another person of the verb, another case of the article). **A distractor never repeats an answer tile**: the validator rejects it, and TARGET-BRIEF requires every distractor to be wrong.

  Example: `['or', 'Today I am working at home', 'Heute arbeite ich zu Hause', 'arbeitet', 'Verb second: heute first, then arbeite, then ich.']`
- **`type`** hinges if the `why` matches the keywords and **either**:
  - the fallback words hold a twin of a word in the first accepted answer;
  - **or** the first answer contains an article or possessive with a noun that is not in the nominative, or a masculine noun with *der / ein*;
  - **or** it contains a fronted element, a verb-first question, a bracket or a *wenn* clause;
  - **or** it contains a conjugated verb whose subject is not *er / sie / es* (*ich, du, wir, ihr, sie* "they", *Sie*).

  Example: `['ty', 'Where do you live? (to a friend)', 'Wo wohnst du', 'wohnen · du', 'wohnt|wohnen', 'The du form ends in -st: wohnst.']`
- **Accepted answers** list every natural order the prompt allows. German moves things freely in the middle and the front: "I work at home today" accepts *Ich arbeite heute zu Hause* and *Heute arbeite ich zu Hause*. When a fronted element is the point, the prompt says so ("(start with heute)"). Subject pronouns cannot be dropped in German, so there are no pronoun-less variants (unlike es and it).

**Lessons where the rule is hardest, with two hinges each** (order / type):
- `sounds` (lesson 1, before `gender`): its deck gives every noun its article, and the body introduces *der / die / das* as "the".
  - order: *die Straße* (x: *der*); *das Buch* (x: *die*)
  - type: "the wine" → *der Wein* (x: *die, das*); "the dog" → *der Hund* (x: *das*)
  - why: "Straße is feminine: die Straße." / "Wein is masculine: der Wein."
- `greetings`:
  - order: *Wie heißen Sie?* (x: *heißt*, prompt "(formal)"); *Woher kommst du?* (x: *kommen*, prompt "(to a friend)")
  - type: "My name is Anna" → *Ich heiße Anna* | *Mein Name ist Anna* (x: *heißt*); "Where are you from? (formal)" → *Woher kommen Sie* (x: *kommst*)
  - why: "The Sie form is the same as the infinitive: heißen." / "The du form ends in -st: kommst." / "The ich form ends in -e: heiße."
- `numbers`:
  - order: *Das kostet zwei Euro fünfzig* (x: *kosten*); *Um acht Uhr arbeite ich* (x: *arbeitet*, prompt "(start with um acht Uhr)")
  - type: "How much are the rolls?" → *Was kosten die Brötchen* | *Wie viel kosten die Brötchen* (x: *kostet*); "Today I work until six (start with heute)" → *Heute arbeite ich bis sechs* (x: *arbeitest*)
  - why: "Plural subject: kosten, the sie (they) form." / "Verb second: um acht Uhr first, then arbeite."
- `questions`:
  - order: *Wo wohnst du?* (x: *wohnt*); *Kommen Sie aus Kanada?* (x: *kommst*, prompt "(formal)")
  - type: "Where does Anna live?" → *Wo wohnt Anna* (x: *wohnen*); "Do you speak English? (formal)" → *Sprechen Sie Englisch* (x: *sprichst*)
  - why: "Yes/no question: the verb comes first." / "Anna takes the er/sie/es form: wohnt."
- `gern`:
  - order: *Ich spiele gern Tennis* (x: *spielt*); *Am Wochenende koche ich gern* (x: *kochst*)
  - type: "Do you like to cook? (to a friend)" → *Kochst du gern* (x: *kocht*); "I like the dog (use mögen)" → *Ich mag den Hund* (x: *der*)
  - why: "Verb second, and gern right after the verb." / "Hund is masculine and the direct object: den Hund."
- `traps`:
  - order: *Ich will einen Kaffee* (x: *ein*); *Ich bekomme ein Geschenk* (x: *bekommt*)
  - type: "My boss is nice" → *Mein Chef ist nett* (x: *Meine*); "I'm getting a new cell phone" → *Ich bekomme ein neues Handy* (x: *bekommt*)
  - why: "Kaffee is masculine: einen in the accusative." / "Chef is masculine: mein, not meine." / "The ich form ends in -e: bekomme."
- `word-order` (before the accusative): hinges come from position and endings only, never from object-first sentences.
  - order: *Morgen fahre ich nach Hamburg* (x: *fährt*); *Am Montag hat Lena frei* (x: *haben*)
  - type: "On Sunday we sleep in (start with am Sonntag)" → *Am Sonntag schlafen wir lange* (x: *schlaft*)
  - why: "The verb is the second element: am Sonntag first, then schlafen."

- For strand (a): "masculine", "feminine", "neuter", "gender", "nominative / accusative / dative", "direct object / indirect object", "plural article / form / noun / ending / verb / subject".

These are deliberately **not** keywords:
- Bare "case", so that "in this case" does not count; bare "plural", so that "the plural of Kind is Kinder" does not count; bare "subject", "ending" and "position".
- Bare "Sie", so that register lines ("Use Sie with strangers") do not count. Write register lines without the word "form".

Trap: Ich bin gewesen used everywhere (correct but heavy); Ich hatte gehabt; war / wahr spelling. | Days, dates and weather |
| 23 | `adjectives` | Adjectives and comparing | de-adjectives | Adjectives after sein take no ending (Der Pullover ist teuer); before a noun at A1, a small set of high-frequency patterns as chunks (ein neues Handy, einen schwarzen Pullover, die rote Jacke); comparatives -er and als (billiger als), am -sten, gut / besser / am besten, gern / lieber / am liebsten. Trap: denn with verb last; …, wenn ich habe Zeit; Wenn ich Zeit habe, ich komme. | Questions and connectors |
| 25 | `traps` | False friends: Gift, Chef, will | (added) | The false friends and calques of §4.5–§4.6 in sentences: will / werden, bekommen / werden, also / auch, Gift, Chef, fast, aktuell, eventuell, Rezept / Quittung, Handy; the sein / haben calques (Mir ist kalt, Ich habe Hunger). Added as the lesson home of the Traps deck. Trap: the English meaning of a German look-alike. | Traps for English speakers |

Per-lesson notes for writers:
- Every lesson's practice set satisfies §7.
- The last two items of each set may mix in a neighbour from an earlier lesson. Preferred neighbours: *sein / haben* calques (*Ich habe Hunger, Mir ist kalt*), *nicht / kein*, *nach / zu / in* for "to", and stem-changing verbs.
- Items whose answer differs from a wrong form only by an umlaut (*fährt / fahrt*, *schön / schon*, *Mütter / Mutter*, *zählen / zahlen*, *würde / wurde*) are strict (§10). They belong mainly in `sounds`, `gender` (umlaut plurals), `stem-change` and `modals` (*möchte / mochte* for recognition only).
- Every English "you" carries a cue (§2.3). Prompts with "I" or "you" that need a gendered noun (*Ingenieur / Ingenieurin*, *Student / Studentin*) say "(a woman speaking)" or accept both.
- `prepositions`: two-way prepositions are recognition only (mc, order); typed production is limited to the chunks *im, ins, am, zum, zur, nach Hause, zu Hause*.
- `adjectives`: adjective endings before a noun are taught as chunks (*die rote Jacke, einen schwarzen Pullover*), not as tables; typed items use only chunks from the lesson or its deck.
- `imperative`: typed production uses the *Sie* and *du* imperatives; *ihr* imperatives are mc and order only.
- `war-hatte`: typed production uses *war, hatte* and the Perfekt; *konnte, musste, wollte* are recognition only.

## 9. Decks

**18 decks in 5 groups**, about 16–18 cards each (about 300 cards in total). Card shape: `target | pron | meaning | note`.
- `target`: German, with its article for every noun (*der Hund*, never *Hund*; this includes the Sounds deck). Nouns are capitalised.
- `pron`: generated (§6).
- `meaning`: short American English.
- `note`: one English line, or empty for at most a quarter of a deck. Gender (when not obvious from *der / die / das* in the target), the plural, the *Sie / du* choice, a separable prefix, and false-friend warnings go here.

**Groups, in order:**

| Group | Decks |
|---|---|
| First steps | Sounds and spelling · Greetings and courtesy · About me · People and family |
| Everyday words | Numbers, time and prices · Days, dates and weather · Food and drink · Shopping and clothes · Home and routine |
| Out and about | Around town and directions · Travel and transport · Health and emergencies · Work and study |
| Conversation | Feelings and states · Likes and free time · Questions and connectors |
| Watch out | Traps for English speakers · Life in Germany |

**Deck rules for writers**
- Single nouns appear with *der / die / das*; the plural goes in the note (*Plural: die Väter*). Nouns in other decks mostly appear in short phrases that show case (*einen Kaffee, mit dem Bus, neben der Bank*). This keeps the standing rule visible in review too.
- Jobs and nationalities describing the speaker: give one form in the target and the other in the note ("A man says: Ich bin Ingenieur").
- Use *Sie* phrases where a traveler meets staff or strangers (shop, café, directions, health, offices), and say so in the note ("Sie form"). Use *du* phrases among friends, and say so.
- **Food and drink** contains *Ich hätte gern…* and *Ich möchte…* as phrases from the start, before `modals` explains *möchte*. The note says "the polite way to order".
- **Traps for English speakers** (about 18 cards):
  - about 12 false friends (§4.6: *Gift, Chef, will, bekommen, also, fast, aktuell, eventuell, sensibel, Handy, Rezept, Kind / nett*);
  - about 6 calques (*Ich habe Hunger, Mir ist kalt, Mir ist heiß, Ich habe recht, Ich bin dreißig* as the "same as English" contrast, *Wie alt bist du?*).
  - Never put a sexual or crude reading on a card (*Ich bin heiß*); name it in a note only as "means something else: say Mir ist heiß".
- **Life in Germany** (about 16 cards): practical words for travelers and newcomers: *der Termin, die Anmeldung, das Bürgeramt, der Ausweis, das Pfand, Nur Bargeld, die Kasse, der Feierabend, Kein Problem, Das geht nicht, geschlossen, der Mülleimer, die Mülltrennung, der Hausmeister, die Miete, die Nebenkosten*. Rules and customs are stated plainly and only where the author is confident; legal deadlines and fines are not stated (§11).

## 10. Typing and script

**Script.** Latin letters plus *ä ö ü ß* (capitals *Ä Ö Ü*; capital *ẞ* is not used). `targetScript`: `[A-Za-zÄÖÜäöüß]`. Every German string must contain at least one character from this class.

**Accent bar** (`keys`): `ä ö ü ß`, in that order. Capitals are not on the bar, and the matcher does not check case, so a learner typing *Ärzte* can type *ärzte*.

**`accents: true`.** As in the Spanish and Italian courses, the flag means:
- the language has marked letters (here the umlauts);
- they are part of the correct answer;
- an answer that differs only in them is graded **"almost"** (accepted, with the correct form shown), never silently accepted.

What the app's matcher does (read in `src/app.html`, `pracMatch` and `foldAcc`; the validator's `fold` in `tools/target/validate.js` does the same for umlauts):
- *ä ö ü* fold to *a o u*, so *Madchen* for *Mädchen* is "almost". That is the right default: a missing umlaut is a typing slip far more often than a grammar error [inference].
- *ß* folds to *ss* in the app, so *Strasse* for *Straße* is "almost", and *dass / muss* typed as *daß / muß* (old spelling) are "almost" too. The validator's `fold` does **not** fold *ß*, so a fill distractor that differs from the answer only in *ß / ss* is not caught there; do not write such distractors.
- **ae / oe / ue are not accepted** for *ä / ö / ü*: *Maedchen* is graded wrong. The accent bar makes the real letters easy to type, and a general rule would be unsafe because *ae, oe, ue* occur in ordinary words (*neue, Feuer, teuer, Steuer, aktuell*). Authors never list *ae / oe / ue* spellings in `a`. The lead may add a German-only engine rule later (§11).
- Capitals are ignored (`pracNorm` lower-cases everything).

**Strict items** (marked `'!strict'`, so an umlaut-only difference is wrong, not "almost"). Mark an item strict when the umlaut is the grammar or the meaning:
- stem-changing verbs: *fährt / fahrt*, *schläft / schlaft*, *trägt / tragt* (every `stem-change` item whose answer has the changed form);
- umlaut plurals: *Mütter / Mutter*, *Väter / Vater*, *Brüder / Bruder*, *Äpfel / Apfel*, *Städte*;
- word pairs where both forms are real words: *schön / schon*, *zählen / zahlen*, *würde / wurde*, *drücken / drucken*, *Bär / Bar*, *möchte / mochte* (`modals`), *hätte / hatte*;
- comparatives: *älter, größer, wärmer* (`adjectives`);
- any `mc` or `fill` item whose options differ only by an umlaut (the validator already demands `!strict` for such fill distractors).

Everything else is "almost": *Brötchen* typed *Brotchen*, *Tschüss* typed *Tschuss*, *Grüß* typed *Gruss*.

**Capitalisation.** The matcher ignores capitals, so *ich habe einen hund* is accepted. The course still teaches capitalisation:
- every model answer, card target and example capitalises nouns, *Sie / Ihnen / Ihr* (formal) and the first word;
- `sounds` and `gender` state the rule ("every noun has a capital letter"), and `greetings` contrasts *Sie* (you) with *sie* (she, they);
- `mc` items test it where it changes meaning (*Wie heißen Sie?* vs *Wie heißen sie?*: "which one asks a stranger's name?");
- a typed answer is never marked down for capitals, and no `why` says it was.

**Punctuation.** The engine strips `. , ! ? ~` and quotes. German has no inverted marks. Authors still write the final punctuation and the comma before *aber, denn, wenn, dass, weil* in `a[0]`, card targets and examples. *geht's* keeps its apostrophe in model answers; the matcher drops a word-final apostrophe and an apostrophe inside a word must match, so list *Wie geht's* and *Wie gehts* both if *geht's* is a typed answer.

**What a typed answer must include**
- Every word German requires: the subject pronoun (German never drops it: *Ich wohne in Berlin*, not *Wohne in Berlin*), the article (*Ich habe einen Bruder*; no article only before jobs, nationalities and most countries), *es* in weather and *es gibt*, the separable prefix at the end.
- The right gender and case of the article, the right verb position and verb ending (the standing rule), the right helping verb in the Perfekt, and the umlaut on strict items.
- *ß* or *ss* as spelled; in a non-strict item *ss* for *ß* is "almost".

**What it need not include**
- Capitals, final punctuation, commas.
- *bitte* when the prompt does not say "please".
- Regional forms (*Servus, Semmel, Grüß Gott*), which the course never asks for in typing.

**`fill` and tile fallbacks.** The three wrong options `o` should be case, gender or person twins wherever possible (*den → der, dem, die*; *wohnst → wohnt, wohne, wohnen*; *mir → mich, ich, mein*), so that the tap fallback still tests the standing rule. A fallback word for a `type` item must not rebuild another accepted word order.

## 11. Gaps and unverified claims

**No native speaker has checked this profile.** Check these first, in this order:
