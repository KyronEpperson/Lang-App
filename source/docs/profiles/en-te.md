# English speakers learning Telugu: course profile

Course `en-te`: Telugu for English speakers. Every explanation, prompt, `why`, `hint`, meaning and card note is in English. Answers, examples and card targets are in Telugu, written in Telugu script. Machine-readable twin: `tools/target/te/profile.json`, written from the same data as §8 and §9, so the two files agree. Pronunciation generator: `tools/target/te/respell.js` (§6). The course folder has a `HOLD` file until the lead releases it.

Models used: the finished Punjabi profile (`docs/profiles/en-pa.md`, whole) and the Hindi generator (`tools/target/hi/respell.js`), whose romanisation key (shared by en-hi and en-pa) this course uses where the sounds are the same; `en-hi.md` itself was not read; Telugu is Dravidian, so no Hindi grammar is carried over. Planned lesson list: `docs/roadmaps/te.json` (the `te` block in `packs/roadmaps.js`, checked identical); its 26 ids are kept and two blocks are reordered (§8).

**Evidence tags.**
- **[research-backed]**: an empirical study or a data source opened for this profile, named where it is used. **(abstract only)** says only an abstract or a record was read.
- **[curriculum convention]**: what syllabuses, textbooks, grammars, Unicode notes and style guides do.
- **[practitioner opinion]**: a teacher's, school's, blog's or guide's stated view, with no data behind it.
- **[inference]**: this profile's own reasoning, **including the author's own knowledge of Telugu**. Every claim of that kind that writers will copy into the course is in the native-check list (§11).

Untagged rules in §6 to §10 are design decisions, not findings. Every Telugu string that carries a pron in this file was generated by `respell.js` when the file was rendered; every other Telugu string was run through it in a final sweep (609 distinct strings: no throws on words; the 49 throws are, by design, the bare signs, vattu and suffix fragments, digits and old letters that the key and the rules name on purpose).

## 1. Summary

- **Which Telugu?** The everyday spoken standard of educated speakers in Telangana and Andhra Pradesh as modern media use it (చేశాను, వెళ్తాను, చేస్తున్నాను), not the literary grānthika style; Telugu script; `te-IN` voice (Azure te-IN-ShrutiNeural, confirmed by Microsoft; MohanNeural from a third-party list only). iOS has no confirmed Telugu voice, so every card and example gets an Azure clip (§2.1).
- **Register:** మీరు (with -ఆరు verbs) is the default "you"; నువ్వు for friends and children; -గారు after names and titles of adults; నమస్కారం / నమస్తే (§2.3–2.4).
- **Pronunciation aid (§6):** the en-hi / en-pa learner romanisation plus ē and ō (ā ī ū ē ō, ṭ ḍ ṇ ḷ, kh gh ch chh, sunna resolved to n / ṇ / m by rule: అంత *anta*, పండు *paṇḍu*, సింహం *simham*). **Generated** by `respell.js`, which is reliable because Telugu writes every vowel it says (no silent a). The ts / dz said for చ / జ before back vowels is taught, not marked. It throws on non-Telugu and warns where it guesses.
- **Biggest traps (§4):** verb agreement (person endings, masculine / non-masculine, human / non-human); no word for "is" (నేను డాక్టర్ని); case endings after the noun on the oblique stem; the నాకు-construction (నాకు … కావాలి / ఇష్టం / తెలుసు); negation as its own verb form and the three "no"s (కాదు, లేదు, వద్దు); inclusive మనం vs exclusive మేము; human counting forms (ఇద్దరు, not రెండు, for people); length, retroflex and doubled consonants.
- **Standing rule (§7):** two strands: **(a) agreement and person endings**, **(b) case endings after the noun, on the oblique** (including the నాకు-construction); in the script lessons a sound strand. Keywords tested on 47 lines.
- **Lessons and decks (§8, §9):** 26 lessons (the roadmap's 26 ids); **the present (habitual, then continuous) now comes before the past**, as the brief asks, and the vowel signs move before the consonants; 18 decks in 5 groups, including five ordinary script decks and "Traps for English speakers".
- **Typing (§10):** word tiles by default; typing optional in Telugu script or romanisation (missing macrons and dots = "almost", already built). Two holes measured: Telugu-script input has no "almost" path, and the past and the negative of many verbs differ only in vowel length (వెళ్ళాను "I went" / వెళ్ళను "I won't go"), which today's romanised matching marks "almost" for each other. Four engine items before writers start (§10.8).

## 2. Variant, register and voice

### 2.1 Variant: the spoken standard, in Telugu script

- **The roadmap names** "Modern standard Telugu as spoken by educated speakers in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana (everyday spoken standard, not the literary grānthika style)"; this profile keeps it. Telugu has a well-known split between the old literary language (grānthika) and the modern standard written the way it is spoken (vyāvahārika), which newspapers, TV and most modern prose use [curriculum convention: Vemuri's grammar, as the roadmap note reports it; not reopened]. The course writes **spoken verb forms**: చేశాను (not చేసితిని), వెళ్తాను (not వెళ్ళెదను / వెళ్లుతాను), చేస్తున్నాను, ఏమిటి / ఏంటి, ఉంది.
- **Telangana and coastal Andhra.** The two big regional standards share the grammar taught here and differ in vocabulary, some verb endings and the amount of Urdu and English in everyday words [inference]. The course uses forms that both understand and that media treat as neutral; regional forms appear only in notes ("in Hyderabad you'll also hear …"), never as the only accepted answer. Where the two differ in a word a learner meets early (చాయ్ in Hyderabad vs టీ; ఏంటి vs ఏమిటి), both are accepted.
- **Say it plainly to users** (course blurb and lesson 1): "This course teaches everyday standard Telugu as it is spoken in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana and heard on TV and in films, in the Telugu script. Books and formal speeches sometimes use older forms; the course points them out when you need them."
- **Voice: `ttsLang: "te-IN"`.** Microsoft's own 2020 announcement lists "te-IN | Telugu | Female | Shruti" among its new neural voices ([Microsoft Tech Community, Ignite 2020 neural TTS update](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/azure-ai/ignite-neural-tts-updates-new-language-support-more-voices/ba-p/1698544)) [curriculum convention]. A third-party catalogue lists exactly two Azure Telugu voices, **te-IN-ShrutiNeural** (female, "#1 of 2 Telugu Azure") and **te-IN-MohanNeural** (male, "#2 of 2") ([json2video, Telugu voices](https://json2video.com/ai-voices/languages/telugu/)); Mohan was not confirmed on a Microsoft page (§11).
- **Android:** Google's TalkBack help lists "Telugu (India)" among the Google Text-to-Speech languages, with "Some devices may not support all languages in all regions" ([Google, *Languages supported by TalkBack*](https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/11101402?hl=en)).
- **iOS:** not confirmed either way. Apple's VoiceOver language page redirected and was not read; an AppleVis thread of November 2025 has blind users saying "VoiceOver is not reading Telugu, Tamil, Kannada, or Malayalam correctly" and that they must switch the rotor to "that particular South Indian voice", which suggests a voice exists but behaves badly ([AppleVis](https://applevis.com/comment/199202)); a change.org petition asks Apple to add Telugu TTS (title only seen in search). §11.
- **What happens with no device voice:** the app shows "No voice for this language on this device" once when `voiceMatch` finds no voice (`src/app.html`, `speak`), and plays a recorded clip whenever one exists (`audio/<target>/index.json`). Decision, as for en-pa and en-ur: **record Azure clips (Shruti; Mohan for male lines if available) for every card target and example row** (request 3, §10.8). `profile.json` sets `voiceMatch: "^te(?:[-_]|$)"`.

### 2.3 మీరు, నువ్వు, and respect

| Form | Verb with it | Used for | In the course |
|---|---|---|---|
| మీరు *mīru* | -ఆరు: మీరు ఎక్కడ ఉంటారు? Commands in -ండి: రండి, కూర్చోండి | strangers, elders, shopkeepers, teachers, in-laws, colleagues; also "you all" | **default** in every prompt, example and dialogue from lesson 6 |
| నువ్వు *nuvvu* | -ఆవు: నువ్వు ఎక్కడ ఉంటావు? Command = the bare form: రా, కూర్చో | close friends of your age, children, younger siblings; rude to a stranger or an elder | taught in `greetings` and `requests`; used only when the prompt says "(to a friend)" or "(to a child)" |

- **Third person** [inference]: అతను *atanu* (he, neutral) and ఆమె *āme* (she, neutral) are the defaults; ఆయన *āyana* and ఆవిడ *āviḍa* (he / she, respectful, with plural -ఆరు verbs) for elders and anyone with -గారు; వాడు *vāḍu* (he, familiar: boys, close friends; rude for an adult man) and అది (it; also "she" in familiar speech) are taught for recognition and in friend dialogues. ఆమె takes the **non-masculine** ending, the same as అది (ఆమె వచ్చింది, అది వచ్చింది), which is the split English speakers do not expect (§4.1).
- **-గారు** *gāru*: after a name, a surname or a title (రావు గారు, లక్ష్మి గారు, డాక్టర్ గారు, మా నాన్న గారు) for an adult you respect; the verb is plural (-ఆరు). **Never after your own name.** Written as a separate word in the course; the attached spelling (రావుగారు) is accepted.
- **Rules for writers.** A prompt whose answer contains "you" says who is addressed when it matters: "(to a friend)" → నువ్వు; no cue or "(politely)" → మీరు. A bare "you" accepts only మీరు forms. Telugu verbs do not show the speaker's gender in the first person (నేను వెళ్ళాను for a man or a woman), so **no speaker cue is needed**; the third person does show gender, so every prompt about "he / she" or a named person makes it clear.
- **Inclusive and exclusive we** [inference]: మనం *manam* = we including you (a plan with the listener: మనం వెళ్దాం, "let's go"); మేము *mēmu* = we not including you (my family and I). Both take -ఆము / -ఆం verbs. Taught in `no-is`, practised in Traps.

- `greet` slots in `profile.json`: శుభ రాత్రి (good night) late at night, నమస్కారం in the other three.

### 2.5 Everyday Telugu, not bookish and not Hindi

**Rule:** use the word an educated speaker uses in neutral, polite speech.
- **Spoken forms, never literary ones** [inference]: చేశాను, వెళ్తాను, వస్తాను, ఉంది, లేదు, ఏమిటి / ఏంటి, ఎక్కడ, ఇక్కడ.
- **English loans in Telugu script when they are the normal word:** టైం, బస్సు, స్టేషన్, ఆఫీసు, బ్యాంకు, డాక్టర్, ఫోన్, టికెట్, కాఫీ, టీ, సినిమా, స్కూలు. Code-mixing with English is heavy in real urban speech; the course uses a loan where a speaker would, but teaches the Telugu word for core vocabulary (నీళ్ళు, not వాటర్; అన్నం, not రైస్).
- **Sanskrit words that are everyday Telugu stay** (ధన్యవాదాలు, సంతోషం, సమయం in notes); Urdu words that are everyday in Hyderabad appear in notes (జవాబు, సామాను).
- **No Hindi.** Heritage learners who watch Hindi films may reach for Hindi words (हाँ, अच्छा, ठीक है); Telugu has its own (అవును, సరే). Hindi forms are never accepted answers.
- **Never Latin letters in Telugu strings.**

### 2.6 Politeness basics an A1 learner needs

1. మీరు + -ఆరు verbs with anyone you do not know (§2.3).
2. -ండి for requests (ఇవ్వండి, చెప్పండి, రండి); "please" (దయచేసి) only on signs and in very formal requests [inference].
3. -గారు after names and titles; respected third persons take plural verbs: మా నాన్న గారు వచ్చారు.
4. Address terms: అన్నా / అక్కా ("brother / sister": a man or woman a bit older, also shopkeepers and drivers), అమ్మా (to an older woman, also a seller), సార్ / మేడం (offices), అంకుల్ / ఆంటీ (a friend's parents) [inference].
5. Offer and take things with the right hand [inference; cultural note only].

### 2.7 Voice of the English text

- Plain, friendly US English, US spelling. A `why` is one sentence.
- Telugu inside English text: in lesson bodies `<b>` around the Telugu, then the romanisation in `<i>` once at first mention in that lesson: `<b>ఇల్లు</b> <i>illu</i>`. In plain-text fields (`why`, `hint`, notes) write the Telugu bare; add the romanisation in brackets only when the point is the sound ("ట (ṭ, tongue curled back)").
- Never add English endings to Telugu words ("two case endings", not "two -లోs").
- Sample voice lines: "ఆమె takes the same ending as అది, so it's ఆమె వచ్చింది, not ఆమె వచ్చాడు." · "In Telugu the ending goes on the noun: ఇంట్లో, 'in the house'." · "Nice work! Review these cards tomorrow so they stick."

### 2.8 Telugu text conventions (every file)

- **Telugu script only**, Unicode NFC, spaces between words, no ZWNJ / ZWJ (the validator rejects them; §10.3).
- **Endings attached, longer postpositions separate:** -ని / -ను, -కి / -కు, -లో, -తో and the question -ఆ are written on the word (ఇంట్లో, బడికి, అమ్మతో, బాగున్నారా); కంటే, నుంచి, దగ్గర, మీద, కోసం, వరకు, పక్కన are separate words after the oblique (దీని కంటే, ఇంటి నుంచి, బల్ల మీద, నా దగ్గర) [inference: both spellings occur in print; this is the course's choice, §11].
- **English loans** take the nativised -u form where that is the everyday written form (బస్సు, ఆఫీసు, బ్యాంకు, స్కూలు, కారు, రైలు); others keep the pollu (డాక్టర్, స్టేషన్, ఫోన్, టైం). A loan with pollu takes an ending directly (డాక్టర్ని, స్టేషన్లో); place names take -u before an ending (హైదరాబాదులో, హైదరాబాదుకి) [inference, §11].
- **One spelling per word** (the lint compares whole strings): ళ్ళ not ళ్ల (వెళ్ళాను, వాళ్ళు, పెళ్ళి, నీళ్ళు); చేయ- (చేయను, చేయాలి; చెయ్య- accepted as an answer); ఐదు (అయిదు accepted); ఏమిటి in model answers (ఏంటి accepted); చేశాను (చేసాను accepted); నుంచి (నుండి accepted); ధన్యవాదాలు; నమస్కారం.
- **Punctuation:** ASCII . ? ! , (r12a: modern Telugu punctuation is mostly ASCII). No danda.
- **Numbers as words** in Telugu strings (యాభై రూపాయలు, ఏడు గంటలకు); the generator throws on any digit. Prices in real life are written in Western digits; the course says so in English and in notes.
- **No Latin letters, no Telugu digits, no archaic letters** (ఱ, ఁ; the generator throws).

## 4. Error zones of English speakers, ranked

**How the list is ranked** [inference]: (1) points that occur in nearly every sentence and can be checked in tiled or typed practice; (2) points where English gives a learner a confident wrong model (no "is", the dative subject, "no"); (3) lesson-local, sound and vocabulary traps.

**Evidence is thin.** No study of errors by English-speaking learners of Telugu was found: searches return only studies of the reverse direction (Telugu speakers' errors in English). The grammar facts below are standard in descriptions of Telugu (the *Telugu grammar* article gives the case endings, the masculine / non-masculine split in the 3rd-person singular (*veḷḷāḍu* / *veḷḷindi*), the human and non-human plural (*veḷḷāru* / *veḷḷāyi*), the negatives *veḷḷalēdu*, *veḷḷanu*, *veḷḷaku*, and the human numerals *iddaru*, *mugguru*; [mirror](https://wiki.krisyotam.com/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic_2026-03/Telugu_grammar)) [curriculum convention]; which of them English speakers get wrong, and how often, is [inference] and in the native-check list (§11).

1. **Verb agreement: person endings, masculine / non-masculine, human / non-human**
   - Wrong → right: ఆమె వచ్చాడు → ఆమె వచ్చింది · మీరు ఎక్కడ ఉన్నావు? → మీరు ఎక్కడ ఉన్నారు? · నేను వెళ్ళారు → నేను వెళ్ళాను · కుక్కలు వచ్చారు → కుక్కలు వచ్చాయి · మా నాన్న గారు వచ్చాడు → మా నాన్న గారు వచ్చారు · ఆమె వెళ్తాడు → ఆమె వెళ్తుంది.
   - [curriculum convention] the *Telugu grammar* article's forms above; the roadmap lists "Verb endings for person and gender (చేశాను, చేశాడు, చేసింది)" among its four hardest points.
   - Design consequence: strand (a) of the standing rule (§7); every lesson with a 3rd-person verb contrasts అతను / ఆమె / అది.

2. **No word for "is" in "X is Y": the person ending, and కాదు**
   - Wrong → right: నేను డాక్టర్ ఉన్నాను → నేను డాక్టర్ని · ఇది పుస్తకం ఉంది → ఇది పుస్తకం · నా పేరు సారా ఉంది → నా పేరు సారా · నేను డాక్టర్ని లేదు → నేను డాక్టర్ని కాదు · but location does take ఉండు: రవి ఇంట్లో → రవి ఇంట్లో ఉన్నాడు; నేను ఇంట్లో లేను (not కాదు).
   - [inference] English "is" splits three ways in Telugu: nothing (identity), ఉండు (place and state), కాదు / లేదు in the negative. The roadmap lists "no word for 'is' in sentences like నా పేరు రవి" as a hardest point.
   - Design consequence: the `no-is` lesson; strand (a) counts person endings on nouns.

3. **Case endings after the noun, on the oblique**
   - Wrong → right: లో ఇల్లు → ఇంట్లో · ఇల్లుకి → ఇంటికి · ఇల్లులో → ఇంట్లో · నేనుకి → నాకు · అతనుని → అతన్ని · హైదరాబాద్ నుంచి is right, but నుంచి హైదరాబాద్ is wrong · కాఫీని తాగాను (marked; an indefinite object takes no -ని) → కాఫీ తాగాను.
   - [curriculum convention] the *Telugu grammar* article's case list (ఇంటినుండి, గదిలో, బడికి); the roadmap lists "Endings instead of prepositions (ఇంట్లో, బడికి)" as a hardest point.
   - Design consequence: strand (b); the `case`, `directions` and `have` lessons; deck cards show nouns with their endings (ఇంట్లో, బస్సులో).

4. **The నాకు-construction: want, like, know, can speak, have relatives, feel**
   - Wrong → right: నేను కాఫీ కావాలి → నాకు కాఫీ కావాలి · నేను బిర్యానీ ఇష్టం → నాకు బిర్యానీ ఇష్టం · నేను తెలుసు → నాకు తెలుసు · నాకు తెలుసు లేదు → నాకు తెలియదు · నేను తెలుగు మాట్లాడగలను is possible, but the everyday A1 form is నాకు తెలుగు వచ్చు · నేను ఆకలి → నాకు ఆకలిగా ఉంది · but must is నేను వెళ్ళాలి, not నాకు వెళ్ళాలి [inference: the last one is the opposite of Hindi मुझे जाना है, a trap for heritage learners who know Hindi].
   - Design consequence: strand (b) counts these items; lessons `requests`, `like`, `have`, `must-can`.

5. **Negation is a verb form, and English "no" has three Telugu words**
   - Wrong → right: నేను వెళ్తాను లేదు → నేను వెళ్ళను · నేను వెళ్ళాను లేదు → నేను వెళ్ళలేదు · వెళ్ళలేదాను → వెళ్ళలేదు (one form for all persons) · "No, I'm not a teacher": లేదు, నేను టీచర్ని కాదు → కాదు, నేను టీచర్ని కాదు · "No (I don't want it)": కాదు → వద్దు · "Is there milk?" "No": కాదు → లేదు.
   - [curriculum convention] the *Telugu grammar* article's negative forms (veḷḷalēdu "used for all persons", veḷḷanu, veḷḷaku).
   - **The length twin:** వెళ్ళను (I won't go) and వెళ్ళాను (I went) differ only in ā; learners who do not hear or type length swap them (§10.5). Design consequence: `future` teaches the pair side by side; the twin rule for writers (§8); request 2.

6. **The verb at the end; question words in place; -ఆ for yes-no**
   - Wrong → right: నేను తింటాను అన్నం → నేను అన్నం తింటాను · మీరు ఉంటారు ఎక్కడ? → మీరు ఎక్కడ ఉంటారు? · మీరు బాగున్నారు? (rising voice only) → మీరు బాగున్నారా? · ఎక్కడ బాగున్నారా? (both at once) → one or the other.
   - Design consequence: tiles in `or` items; order alone does not count for the standing rule.

7. **Inclusive and exclusive we**
   - Wrong → right: (inviting a friend) మేము సినిమాకి వెళ్దాం → మనం సినిమాకి వెళ్దాం · (telling a friend about your family) మనం హైదరాబాద్ నుంచి వచ్చాం → మేము హైదరాబాద్ నుంచి వచ్చాం · మన ఇల్లు / మా ఇల్లు likewise.
   - Design consequence: `no-is`, `family`, the Traps deck.

8. **Counting people**
   - Wrong → right: రెండు పిల్లలు → ఇద్దరు పిల్లలు · మూడు అన్నలు → ముగ్గురు అన్నలు · ఇద్దరు పుస్తకాలు → రెండు పుస్తకాలు · రెండు పుస్తకం → రెండు పుస్తకాలు (the plural after numbers above one).
   - [curriculum convention] the *Telugu grammar* article: *iddaru* (non-human *reṇḍu*), *mugguru*, *naluguru* … and *mandi* from eight up.
   - Design consequence: `family`, `numbers`; strand (a) counts it ("human").

9. **Respect slips**
   - Wrong → right: నువ్వు to a shopkeeper or an elder → మీరు · నా పేరు సారా గారు → నా పేరు సారా · రండి said, then ఇవ్వు → ఇవ్వండి · వాడు for an adult stranger → అతను / ఆయన · a teacher's first name alone → with గారు or మేడం / సార్.
   - Design consequence: §2.3–2.6; `greetings`, `requests`.

10. **Sounds English does not separate**
    - Wrong (said) → right: పాట (song, retroflex ṭ) and పాత (old, dental t) merged · ల and ళ merged (కాలు, leg / కాళ్ళు, legs) · గది (room) / గద్ది (throne): a doubled consonant said single (Bhaskararao & Ray's pair) · ఎ / ఏ and ఒ / ఓ merged (English has only the long ones, with a glide) · an English puff on every k, t, p · ఫ read f in a Telugu word · final -u said as a full English "oo".
    - [research-backed] Bhaskararao & Ray (2016): length contrasts in all vowels but /ɛ/ and /æː/; gemination contrasts; retroflexes including /ɭ/ (§6). [inference] English speakers' difficulty with the retroflex / dental contrast is well known for Hindi (en-hi §4.7 cites Werker & Tees); Telugu has the same contrast plus ల / ళ.
    - Design consequence: the script lessons; the sound strand (§7); minimal pairs in the decks.

11. **Reading the script**
    - Wrong → right (read): the vattu read before the letter it hangs under (అమ్మ, ఇంట్లో) · the tick kept when ి is added · ై's lower part read as a separate letter · ం read as m everywhere (అంత is *anta*, not "amta") · ొ / ో confused · ఱ met in an old text read as a different sound.
    - Design consequence: `vowel-signs`, `conjuncts`; the romanisation stays on until the learner turns it off.

12. **Sandhi in everyday speech**
    - Wrong → right: insisting on బాగా ఉన్నాను → బాగున్నాను (the normal form) · ఎక్కడ ఉన్నారు and ఎక్కడున్నారు are both fine · ఇల్లు లో → ఇంట్లో · ఏమి ఉంది → ఏముంది.
    - [inference] Vowels meet and merge across word boundaries in speech, and modern writing often writes the merged form. Design consequence: the course stores the merged forms that are the everyday word (బాగున్నాను, ఇంట్లో, ఏముంది) as single words and teaches them as wholes; accepted answers list both forms where both are normal (§8); the voice reads what is written.

13. **False friends and calques**
    - Wrong → right: "you're welcome" as స్వాగతం (that is "welcome!" to a guest) → పర్వాలేదు or nothing · నేను ఇరవై ఐదు సంవత్సరాలు → నా వయసు ఇరవై ఐదు (సంవత్సరాలు) · "I have a brother": నేను ఒక అన్న ఉంది → నాకు ఒక అన్న ఉన్నాడు (the verb agrees with the brother) · one English "brother" is అన్న (older) or తమ్ముడు (younger), "sister" అక్క or చెల్లెలు, "uncle" మామయ్య, బాబాయి or పెదనాన్న by side and age · ఇష్టం is "liking", not a verb · రేపు (tomorrow) and నిన్న (yesterday) are different words, unlike Hindi कल.
    - [inference] Teaching lore and the author's knowledge; native check (§11).
    - Design consequence: the Traps deck; `family`.

14. **Numbers and time**
    - Wrong → right: ఒకటి టీ → ఒక టీ (ఒక before a noun) · digits typed for numbers (the course uses words) · 7:30 built from English → ఏడున్నర · "at seven" ఏడు గంటలు → ఏడు గంటలకు. Good news to tell learners: from 21 up, Telugu numbers are built regularly (ఇరవై ఒకటి, ఇరవై ఐదు), unlike Hindi [inference].
    - Design consequence: `numbers`, `time`; the Numbers and money deck.

## 5. Learners: who, why, what first

**Who**
- [research-backed, secondary] An analysis of American Community Survey data reported by The News Minute (2018) found "more than 400,000 Telugu speakers in the US" in 2017, up 86% from 2010, calling Telugu "the fastest growing language in the US" ([The News Minute](https://www.thenewsminute.com/news/telugu-fastest-growing-language-us-says-study-90332)). The study's authors are not named in the article; the Census table itself was not opened (§11).
- [inference] The groups, probably in this order of size:
  - **heritage learners**: children and grandchildren of the large wave of Telugu-speaking engineers, doctors and students who came to the US from the 1990s on (Dallas, the New York / New Jersey area, the Bay Area, Atlanta, Chicago); many understand some spoken Telugu, mixed with English, cannot read the script, and want to talk to grandparents;
  - **partners and in-laws** of Telugu speakers;
  - **film fans** (Telugu cinema has a large audience, and its hits are dubbed and subtitled worldwide);
  - **travellers and professionals** going to Hyderabad (a major IT centre) or Vijayawada, Visakhapatnam and Tirupati.

**Why they study**
- No survey of adult Telugu learners' motives was found (§11). [inference] Family and identity, a partner's family, films and music, work trips, temple visits.

**What they need first** [inference]
1. **The script, well enough to use the course**: five short script lessons, as the roadmap and MSU's chapter 1 do.
2. **Survival politeness:** నమస్కారం, మీరు, -గారు, ధన్యవాదాలు, క్షమించండి, అవును / కాదు, introducing yourself.
3. **Family words with age** (అన్న / తమ్ముడు, అక్క / చెల్లెలు, అమ్మమ్మ / నాన్నమ్మ, తాతయ్య) and the respect plural: what heritage learners and partners use first (`family`).
4. **The verb forms of everyday life before the past:** habits and plans (వెళ్తాను, ఉంటాను, చేస్తాను) and "right now" (చేస్తున్నాను) let a learner say where they live, what they do and what they are doing; hence the reorder (§8).
5. **Numbers, prices, ordering food, getting around.**

**Check of the roadmap's order** [curriculum convention, from the roadmap's own basis]
- The roadmap follows MSU *Basic Telugu*: script; simple sentences, requests, numbers, introductions, family; the past and past negative; the present continuous; the future, habitual and obligation; time, routine, travel and shopping. The book's contents page did not list its chapters when fetched for this profile (§11), so the order is as the roadmap reports it. This profile keeps the stage-2 order and the stage-4 topics, and moves the present before the past (§8), which also puts the "daily routine" lesson after the form a routine needs.
## 6. Pronunciation aid (`pron`)

**Evidence.**
- [research-backed] Bhaskararao & Ray (2016), the *JIPA* illustration of Telugu (formal speech of an educated speaker from Vizianagaram, eastern dialect): "All the vowel phonemes except /ɛ/ and /æː/ contrast for length"; "Native lexical items do not end in a long vowel"; /o/ does not occur word-finally; "While [ʧ] and [ʤ] occur before front vowels, [ʦ] and [ʣ] occur before non-front vowels", with Sanskrit (tatsama) words bringing [ʧ] into contrast (/ʦɐːpɐ/ 'mat' vs /ʧɐːpɐm/ 'bow'); "The only aspirated plosive that is found in native words is /dʱ/", and only in a couple of numerals; /ɭ/ does not start a word; gemination contrasts, as in /ɡɐdi/ 'room' vs /ɡɐddi/ 'throne' ([Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/546440D379A9139BFBB590254E50ED6B/core-reader)).
- [curriculum convention] r12a's Telugu orthography notes: the sunna (ం) "is pronounced as a homorganic nasal" before a plosive, and as m before other consonants and at the end of a word; words are separated by spaces; punctuation is mostly ASCII, the danda being chiefly for verse ([r12a, *Telugu orthography notes*](https://r12a.github.io/scripts/telu/te)). The XPF grapheme-to-phoneme table says the same of the sunna and notes that /r/ is a flap between vowels ([XPF, Telugu](https://cohenpr-xpf.github.io/XPF/conv_resources/info/te.html)).
- [curriculum convention] MSU *Basic Telugu* 1.5 writes an informal romanisation with no macrons and no dots (*akka*, *attha* for అత్త, *tatayya*) ([MSU 1.5](https://openbooks.lib.msu.edu/basictelugu/chapter/1-5/)). This course does not copy it: it shows neither length nor retroflexion, which are exactly the contrasts English speakers miss (§4.10).

**Decision.** `pron: "required"`. The pron is **the en-hi / en-pa learner romanisation**, with Telugu's two extra long vowels ē and ō, and **generated by `tools/target/te/respell.js`**, never typed. `node tools/target/te/respell.js "<text>"` prints `<text> -> <pron>`; the module exports `respell`, `pattern`, `warnings`, `FSTEM`, `EXC`, `VER`.

**Why a generator is reliable here.** Telugu spelling is close to phonemic and, unlike Hindi, it writes every vowel it says: there is **no silent a** (కల *kala*, not "kal"), so the hard part of the Hindi generator does not exist. What remains is a letter table, the virama (pollu), the sunna rule and a few words said against their letters (English loans with ఫ = f, listed by stem so that కాఫీని and ఫోన్లో follow).

**Measured** (self-measured; no native check, §11):
- **Test set 1** (75 words and phrases of course vocabulary, expected outputs written before the first run, scratch `set1.tsv`): **1 mismatch**, an error in the expectation (క్షమించండి: I had written ñ for the sunna before చ; the rule gives n, as en-hi does before every palatal). 3 warnings, all intended (ళ్ల for ళ్ళ, ృ, ః).
- **Throws** as designed on Latin letters, Western and Telugu digits, ఱ, ఁ, and a lone vowel sign.
- **Silent-error classes that remain** (no warning): (1) **English loans** said against the letters: æ written ్యా or ఏ (బ్యాంకు *byānku* ≈ bænku), English o and short a; (2) **Sanskrit words with aspirates and శ / ష**, said more or less carefully by different speakers; the pron keeps the letters; (3) **చ / జ as ts / dz** (decided, not shown: below); (4) **ఎ- / ఒ- at the start of a word** said ye- / wo-; (5) **sunna before a consonant in a Sanskrit word** where some speakers say a nasal vowel; (6) words where the spoken form drops a written vowel (sandhi in fast speech): the pron follows the spelling; the course stores the spoken forms as words where they are the everyday form (బాగున్నాను, ఇంట్లో, §4.12).
- **Adding words:** writers never edit a pron. When the generator throws or warns, the writer reports the word; the lead adds an English-loan stem to `FSTEM` (its ఫ is f, endings included), a whole word to `EXC` (wrong output) or to `VER` (right, checked) and runs `node tools/target/repron.js te`.

**Decisions inside the key.**
- **ch, not c,** for చ (and chh for ఛ, cch for చ్చ): the en-hi and en-pa key, so a learner who takes two of these courses sees one system; `romaLoose` folds ch and chh to c anyway, so a learner who types *c* is "almost" (§10.5).
- **చ / జ said ts / dz** before a, ā, u, ū, o, ō in native words (చాలా ≈ tsālā, జరిగింది ≈ dzarigindi; Bhaskararao & Ray) are **not shown**: the split is lexical (Sanskrit words keep ch before the same vowels: Bhaskararao & Ray's pair is native చాప 'mat', said with ts, against Sanskrit చాపం 'bow', said with ch), so the generator cannot place it from the spelling, and a mark that is wrong half the time is worse than none. The `consonants-1` lesson teaches it in one table with frequent native words (చాలా, చూడు, చదువు, జరుగు, జవాబు is a loan and keeps j; చెప్పు and చిన్న have front vowels and keep ch), and the voice models it. Whether younger Telangana speakers keep the split is unverified (§11).
- **ē and ō** with a macron, like ā ī ū: Telugu has short ఎ ఒ and long ఏ ఓ, and English speakers hear both as the vowel of "say" / "go" (§4.10).
- **Sunna** is not written ṁ: it is resolved by rule to n, ṇ or m (అంత *anta*, పండు *paṇḍu*, సింహం *simham*, పుస్తకం *pustakam*), so the pron says what to do.
- **శ and ష both sh**, as in en-hi; క్ష is ksh (క్షమించండి *kshaminchaṇḍi*); ఋ and ృ are ru, the Telugu reading (కృష్ణ *krushṇa*, warns); visarga ః is h (దుఃఖం *duhkham*, warns; rare, Sanskrit).
- **ళ్ల** (common in print for ḷḷ) is read ḷḷ with a warning; the course spells ళ్ళ (§2.8).
- **Archaic letters throw:** ఱ (write ర), ఁ (half sunna: leave it out), ౘ ౙ ౚ ఴ, the Sanskrit vowels ఌ ౡ ౠ.

### 6.1 The key (exact format)

| Telugu | pron | Note for learners |
|---|---|---|
| అ / no vowel sign | a | the u of "but"; always said (no silent a) |
| ఆ ా | ā | "father", held long |
| ఇ ి / ఈ ీ | i / ī | "sit" / "seat", held long |
| ఉ ు / ఊ ూ | u / ū | "put" / "boot"; final -u is short and light |
| ఎ ె / ఏ ే | e / ē | "bet" / "say" held long, without the glide |
| ఒ ొ / ఓ ో | o / ō | short "o" / "go" held long, without the glide |
| ఐ ై / ఔ ౌ | ai / au | "aisle" / "out" |
| ఋ ృ | ru | as in కృష్ణ *krushṇa* (some say ri) |
| క ఖ గ ఘ ఙ | k kh g gh ṅ | kh, gh: with a puff of air (Sanskrit words only) |

### 6.4 Card front, and hiding the romanisation

- **Card front:** the Telugu script, large. Under it the pron line, **shown by default**: after five script lessons the learner reads slowly, and length and retroflexion are easy to misread.
- **Hideable:** the app's "Blur romanization in Browse" setting exists (`S.hideRom`, `src/app.html`) but covers Browse only; request 7 (§10.8) extends it to cards and example rows per course ("always / tap to reveal / off"). Suggested prompt after lesson 14 (`case`). Heritage learners who already read Telugu may switch it off from day one.
- **Practice items carry no pron** (PRACTICE-SPEC). Exception, as in en-hi and en-pa: in the five script lessons, English prompts may name a sound with its romanisation ("Build kāki (crow)"); answers are always Telugu script.

### 6.5 What the script lessons must teach, so the aid can be read and later dropped

1. `vowels`: a macron = hold the vowel long (ā ī ū ē ō); e and o are short, ē and ō long, and Telugu tells them apart; ai and au.
2. `vowel-signs`: every consonant carries a unless a sign changes it, and the a is always said; the talakaṭṭu (the tick) and how a sign replaces it.
3. `consonants-1`: h after a letter = a puff (kh, chh), rare outside Sanskrit words; the dot below = retroflex; **ch / j are often said ts / dz before a ā u ū o ō**, with the word table.
4. `consonants-2`: t d n plain = dental; ph is not f (except English loans); ḷ; r is a tap.
5. `conjuncts`: a doubled letter is held (*amma*); the sunna becomes n / ṇ / m by the next letter; pollu = no vowel.
6. Every lesson body: "The romanisation is a crutch: read the Telugu first and glance down only to check."

## 7. Standing practice rule

**Rule:** every lesson's practice set keeps re-testing two things, as in en-hi and en-pa.
- **(a) Agreement, and the person endings.** The verb ends in the person and number of its subject: -ఆను *ānu* (నేను), -ఆవు *āvu* (నువ్వు), -ఆరు *āru* (మీరు, and any respected person), -ఆడు *āḍu* (అతను, వాడు), -ఇంది *indi* or -ఉంది *undi* (ఆమె, అది: **non-masculine singular**), -ఆము *āmu* (మేము, మనం), -ఆరు (people in the plural), -ఆయి *āyi* (**non-human plural**). A noun or adjective used as "am / are" takes the same kind of **person ending** (నేను డాక్టర్ని, మేము విద్యార్థులం, మీరు అమెరికన్?) because Telugu has no word for "is" there.
- **(b) Case endings after the noun, on its oblique form.** Telugu marks "in, to, with, from, the object" with an ending after the noun (ఇంట్లో, బడికి, అమ్మతో, ఇంటి నుంచి, అతన్ని), some nouns change before it (ఇల్లు → ఇంటి-, చెట్టు → చెట్టు-, నేను → నా-), and the one who likes, wants, knows or has takes -కి / -కు: **the నాకు-construction** (నాకు కాఫీ కావాలి, నాకు తెలుసు, నాకు ఇష్టం).

**Why these two.** They occur in nearly every sentence from `no-is` on, have no English counterpart, and can be checked mechanically with a twin form as the distractor (§4.1–4.4). They are Telugu's own: Hindi's agreement is about masculine / feminine endings on adjectives and participles, Telugu's is about person endings and the masculine / non-masculine, human / non-human split; and the నాకు-construction is far wider in Telugu (want, know, like, have a relative, be hungry, be able to speak a language) than in Hindi or Punjabi.
- **Not chosen:** verb-final order (every `or` item practises it anyway); negation (§4.5: it gets its own lessons, `future`, `past-neg`, and a twin-distractor writer rule, §10.5, but a why line about it would not name a fixed term); inclusive / exclusive we (one lesson's point; it does count under strand (a) when the hinge is the verb ending -ఆం / -దాం); human counting forms (counted under (a) through the keyword "human").

**The script stage.** The five script lessons have no grammar. In those sets only, the hinges are **sound strand** items: dental / retroflex (త / ట, ద / డ, న / ణ, ల / ళ), aspirated / unaspirated, long / short vowel (ి / ీ, ె / ే, ొ / ో, plain / ా), the vowel sign itself, the doubled consonant and the vattu (గది / గద్ది), sunna, pollu. One sound `mc` item per later set is recommended, not counted.

**Count** (`minOrder: 2`, `minType: 2`): in every set, at least **2 `or` items** and **2 `ty` items** hinge on the rule, and their `why` names it with a keyword.

**What counts as hinging** (mechanical): `or`, strand (a): `a` contains an agreeing form and `x` its twin from another person, number or class (వెళ్ళింది / వెళ్ళాడు; ఉన్నారు / ఉన్నావు; వచ్చాయి / వచ్చారు; డాక్టర్ని / డాక్టర్). `or`, strand (b): `a` contains a noun or pronoun with its case ending, and `x` the bare or wrong form (ఇంట్లో / ఇల్లు; ఇంటికి / ఇల్లుకి; నాకు / నేను; అతన్ని / అతను). `ty`: `a[0]` contains the same, `x` the twin, and **the twin must not be accepted**. Script stage: `or` tiles are the syllables of one word and `x` holds the sound twin; a one-word `ty` is written syllable-spaced with the unspaced word as the second answer (`కా కి|కాకి`) and two or more distractors; the `why` spells the word unspaced.

**Hinges in the script stage** (letters taught by that lesson only; distractor in brackets, keyword in the why):
- `vowels` (vowel letters, క and the inherent a): or "Build īka (feather)" ఈ క (x ఇ, "long vowel"); or "Build ūka (bran)" ఊ క (x ఉ, "long vowel"). ty "Type ē (which)" `ఏ` (x ఎ ఈ, "long vowel"); ty "Type īka (feather)" `ఈ క|ఈక` (x ఇ ఎ, "long vowel"). ## 8. Lessons (final order)

**Changes from the roadmap** (all [inference] built on §4–§7):
- **Kept: all 26 items, no lessons added.** Ids lose the `te-` prefix (`te-future` becomes `future`); each roadmap id appears exactly once in a `roadmap` field. Additions considered and folded in instead: a pronoun lesson (`no-is` carries the pronouns, including మనం / మేము), a counting-people lesson (`family` and `numbers` carry ఇద్దరు, ముగ్గురు), a negation lesson (negation is taught with each tense: `future` చేయను, `continuous` చేయడం లేదు, `past-neg` చేయలేదు, and `no-is` కాదు).
- **Reordered, move 1 (the brief's): the present comes before the past.** The roadmap follows the MSU textbook, which teaches the past (chapter 3) before the present continuous and the future-habitual. New stage 3, "Present and past": `like` → `future` (habitual and future) → `continuous` → `past` → `past-neg` → `and-then`. Reasons: (1) the habitual-future form is what Telugu uses for "I live in Hyderabad", "I don't eat meat", "what do you do?", so it is the most useful verb form for introductions and routines, and the roadmap's own "daily routine" lesson (`time`) needs it; (2) the continuous చేస్తున్నాను is built on the same stem as the habitual చేస్తాను (చేస్తు- + ఉన్నాను), so habitual first, continuous second teaches one stem twice; (3) the past's 3rd-person forms (వెళ్ళాడు / వెళ్ళింది / వెళ్ళారు / వెళ్ళాయి) then come after the learner already met the same agreement split in the habitual (వెళ్తాడు / వెళ్తుంది / వెళ్తారు / వెళ్తాయి). No strong teaching reason against the move was found: the past is not easier to form than the habitual in Telugu (both have irregular stems: తిన్నాను / తింటాను). `like` moves to the head of stage 3 because it needs no verb forms (నాకు … ఇష్టం) and so bridges from `have` to the tenses. `future` keeps its id but is titled "Habitual and future", since that is what the form is.
- **Reordered, move 2 (separate reason): `vowel-signs` goes from 4th to 2nd**, right after `vowels`. With the roadmap's order, the two consonant lessons would have only the inherent a to build words with (their practice would be letter naming); with the signs known first (taught on క alone), `consonants-1` can build చీకటి, గాజు, కోడి, జడ and `consonants-2` పాట, పాత, పాలు, తల. en-pa made the same move for the same reason. The consonant lessons keep the chart order of rows.
- **Moved with move 1:** `future` leaves stage 4, so stage 4 ("Routine and getting around") has five lessons: `must-can`, `time`, `shopping`, `directions`, `compare`.
- **Renamed:** titles name the point or give a gloss (table). `no-is` "Pronouns, and no word for 'is'"; `future` "Habitual and future: చేస్తాను, చేయను".
- **Widened:** `greetings` also teaches -గారు and the three words for "no" in outline (అవును / కాదు / లేదు, వద్దు); `no-is` the negative కాదు and inclusive / exclusive we; `family` the human counting forms; `have` ఉన్నాడు / ఉంది for people and things and నాకు ఒక అన్న ఉన్నాడు for relatives; `future` the negative చేయను and its length twin (చేస్తాను / చేయను; later వెళ్ళను / వెళ్ళాను).
- **Stages:** "Script, sounds and greetings" 6, "First sentences" 9, "Present and past" 6, "Routine and getting around" 5.

**The script decks are ordinary decks** (no letter-drill machine needed, §10.1). Rules:
- **Every target is something the voice can read:** a vowel letter, a consonant letter (said with its a), a consonant with a vowel sign, or a word; never a bare vowel sign, sunna, pollu or a vattu alone (the generator throws on a bare sign).
- **Lone consonant letters:** the pron is the syllable (క *ka*); the meaning names the sound ("ka (k, no puff of air)"). Whether Azure reads a lone letter as the syllable is unverified (request 9); if not, record clips.
- **Meanings must differ** within a deck (the validator checks).
- Letter-card notes give a word built only from letters taught by then.
- `Vowels` (16): the twelve everyday vowel letters (అ ఆ ఇ ఈ ఉ ఊ ఎ ఏ ఐ ఒ ఓ ఔ), plus ఆ / ఈ / ఏ as words (that / this / which) and ఈక. ఋ in the lesson only.
- `Vowel signs` (17): క with each sign (కా కి కీ కు కూ కె కే కై కొ కో కౌ), plus కాకి, ఆకు, కేక, ఈక, ఊక.

### 9.1 Localisation

- **Names** (Telugu in Telugu strings, English in prompts): learner personas **సారా** (Sarah, an American woman) and **డేవిడ్** (David, an American man); heritage personas **ప్రియ** (Priya, a woman) and **అర్జున్** (Arjun, a man), Telugu Americans. Telugu characters: రవి, లక్ష్మి, శ్రీనివాస్, సుధ, అనిత, వెంకట్, రాజు, కిరణ్ (unisex: give a cue); elders అమ్మమ్మ, నాన్నమ్మ, తాతయ్య, with -గారు for adults outside the family (రావు గారు). ## 10. Typing and script

### 10.1 The script stage (works with ordinary lessons and decks)

Stage 1 is five script lessons and five script decks, all ordinary: nothing depends on the Korean letter-drill machine.

| Lesson | Deck | Letters and signs | What the learner can do after it |
|---|---|---|---|
| `vowels` | Vowels | the 12 everyday vowel letters; ఋ (recognition); క with its inherent a | read a vowel letter; hear short vs long; knows the a is always said |
| `vowel-signs` | Vowel signs | the 11 signs on క; the talakaṭṭu | read any consonant + vowel sign once the consonant is known |
| `consonants-1` | Consonants 1: k, ch and ṭ rows | క–ఙ, చ–ఞ, ట–ణ | read the first three rows; hear plain vs aspirated; tell retroflex; knows ch / j are often ts / dz |
| `consonants-2` | Consonants 2: t and p rows, and the rest | త–న, ప–మ, య ర ల వ శ ష స హ ళ (ఱ shown as old) | read every modern letter; tell dental from retroflex, ల from ళ |
| `conjuncts` | Conjuncts and sunna | doubled letters, vattulu, ్ర, క్ష, ం, ్ (pollu), ః (recognition) | read whole words, including English loans |

- **How many script lessons:** five, as the roadmap plans. Six were considered (splitting vattulu from sunna), but the sunna rule is short and the course reuses both in every later lesson.
- **Which conjuncts a beginner must read** (taught in `conjuncts`, every later lesson may use them): the doubled letters (క్క గ్గ చ్చ జ్జ ట్ట డ్డ త్త ద్ద న్న ప్ప మ్మ ల్ల ళ్ళ య్య వ్వ ర్ర); the common pairs ్ర (ప్రేమ, శ్రీ), ్య (ధన్యవాదాలు, బ్యాంకు), ్వ (స్వాగతం), ్ల (ఇంట్లో), ్త (పుస్తకం), స్త / స్క (వస్తాను, నమస్కారం), ష్ట (ఇష్టం), క్ష (క్షమించండి), జ్ఞ (recognition: జ్ఞానం). Rare three-letter clusters (స్త్ర) only in recognition. A conjunct that only Sanskrit words use is glossed in the card note the first time it appears.
- **The rare letters to recognise only:** ఙ ఞ (chart only), ఋ ృ (కృష్ణ, సంస్కృతి), ః (దుఃఖం), ఱ (old books and some names). The generator throws on ఱ, ఁ, ౘ ౙ ౚ ఴ, ఌ ౡ ౠ, so they cannot enter stored text by accident.
- The consonants follow the chart order (the rows make aspiration and retroflex / dental visible as a grid) [curriculum convention: the roadmap's grouping; Telugu primers such as the CIIL primer, which appeared in search and was not opened].
- **If the letter-drill machine is generalised** (request 10), the five decks gain "hear a syllable, tap its letter" drills (త / ట, ల / ళ, ఎ / ఏ) and "build the word from letters". Nothing in the lessons changes.

### 10.2 What a typed answer is

**Default: word tiles** (`tilesDefault: true`). Telugu writes spaces between words, so tiles are words as written: a noun with its case ending is one tile (ఇంట్లో, బడికి, నాకు), నుంచి / మీద / దగ్గర / కోసం are separate tiles, a verb is one tile (వెళ్తాను, చేస్తున్నాను), చేయడం and లేదు are two.

**Typing is optional**, through the existing switch:
- **Telugu script from a phone keyboard.** Gboard and iOS offer a Telugu keyboard [inference: neither page was opened, §11]; phonetic (transliterating) keyboards such as Gboard's Telugu with English-letter input also produce Telugu script. The answer arrives as Telugu, compared after NFC.
- **Romanisation on an English keyboard:** `pronAnswers: true`, `pronAnswersKeepSpaces: true`, `pronAnswersScript: "[ఀ-౿]"`, `romaAlmost: true`, `pronAnswersWarn: true` (the en-pa / en-ur settings). `gen.js` respells every Telugu `fi` / `ty` answer with its spaces kept and adds the romanisation to the accepted answers; typed Latin text equal to it is **exact**, and text equal to it after `romaLoose` is **"almost"**.

**What must and must not fold (romanised input):**

| Fold to "almost" | Measured today (§10.5) | Must stay wrong |
|---|---|---|
| missing macrons (a / ā, e / ē, o / ō, i / ī, u / ū): *bagunnanu*, *emiti*, *roju* | yes | a length twin when it is another word (*vellanu* for వెళ్ళను "I won't go" when the answer is వెళ్ళాను "I went"): request 2 |
| missing dots (ṭ ḍ ṇ ḷ → t d n l): *rendu*, *vallu*, *intlo* | yes | |

| c / ch / chh (*manci*, *cala*) | yes | |
| th / dh typed for dental t / d (*velthanu*, *thelugu*), the usual Indian spelling | **no** (wrong) | request 5 |
| s for sh (*istam*, *chesanu*) | **no** (wrong) | request 5 (optional) |

### 10.3 Unicode normalisation

- **Stored text:** NFC, Telugu letters only, spaces between words.
- **The two-part vowel sign ై** (U+0C48) has a canonical decomposition U+0C46 + U+0C56; NFC recomposes it (checked in node; r12a: "It is normal to use the precomposed version"). The app's `pracNorm` and the generator both normalise to NFC, so either input matches (measured, §10.5). U+0C55 (a partial glyph, "does not normally occur in Telugu text", r12a) and a bare U+0C56 throw in the generator.
- **ZWNJ / ZWJ.** Telugu writes English loans with a visible pollu before a suffix using ZWNJ (the brief's own డాక్టర్ని carries U+200C between ్ and ని, checked in node). The validator rejects U+200B–200F in stored text, and `pracNorm` strips ZWNJ / ZWJ from input. **Rule:** stored text has no ZWNJ: డాక్టర్ని (the font draws ర with a న vattu; the reading is the same, *ḍākṭarni*). A learner who types the ZWNJ form matches exactly (measured).
- **Nukta** (U+0C3C, Unicode 14) is rare in real text; the course does not use it. Loans with z and f are written with జ and ఫ (ఫోన్ *fōn*: its stem is in `FSTEM`).
- **ళ్ల vs ళ్ళ:** print and keyboards often write ళ్ల for the doubled ḷḷ (వెళ్లాను). The course stores ళ్ళ; the generator reads ళ్ల as ḷḷ with a warning; the matcher marks ళ్ల wrong today (measured): request 1 folds it as **exact**.
- **Sunna vs a nasal conjunct** (అంత / అన్త, పండు / పణ్డు): both spellings occur in older print; "almost" (request 1).
- **Archaic letters:** ఱ ఁ ౘ ౙ never in stored text (the generator throws); a learner who types ఱ for ర gets "almost" (request 1).
- **Digits:** neither Western nor Telugu digits (౦–౯, which modern text rarely uses, r12a) appear in Telugu strings: numbers are words (§2.8). Real-life prices are in Western digits; lessons say so in English and show ₹50 only in English text.
- **Never "almost" in Telugu script:** vowel length (ి / ీ, ె / ే, ొ / ో, plain / ా: వెళ్ళను / వెళ్ళాను), dental / retroflex (త / ట, ల / ళ), single / doubled (గది / గద్ది), endings (-ఆను / -ఆరు, -ఆడు / -ఇంది).

### 10.4 The accent bar

`keys`: `ం ్` (sunna and pollu): the two signs learners most often cannot find; a Telugu keyboard has the rest. The bar inserts them after the letter at the caret; NFC handles order. `accents: true` turns missing macrons and dots in romanised answers into "almost".

**The length twin is the one serious hole.** For a whole class of verbs the past and the negative differ only in one vowel's length, in every person [inference for the class; §11]: verbs whose stem ends in a doubled consonant or a cluster, and the -ించు verbs (వెళ్ళాను / వెళ్ళను, చెప్పాను / చెప్పను, పెట్టాను / పెట్టను, క్షమించాను / క్షమించను; వెళ్ళాడు / వెళ్ళడు, వెళ్ళారు / వెళ్ళరు). ### 10.6 Fonts and line height

- The app has rules for `[lang=hi]`, `[lang=th]`, `[lang=ur]` and `[lang=pa]` target text and **nothing for Telugu** (searched `src/app.html` for "Telugu" in CSS and `lang=te`: no hit). - Telugu stacks vowel signs above the letter (ి ీ ె ే ొ ో, the tick) and vattulu below it, sometimes two deep (స్త్ర), and the ై has a part below: tiles at least 52 px tall, and no `overflow: hidden` on target text.
- **The pron line** uses only precomposed letters (ā ī ū ē ō ṭ ḍ ṇ ḷ ṅ ñ): the body font must contain them (DM Sans was not checked, §11).

### 10.7 `targetScript` and punctuation

- `targetScript`: `[ఀ-౥]` (U+0C00–0C65): Telugu letters and signs, **excluding** the Telugu digits (U+0C66–0C6F) and the fraction signs (U+0C78–0C7F). `pronAnswersScript` is the whole block (`[ఀ-౿]`), which only decides which answers get a romanised twin.
- **Punctuation:** modern Telugu uses ASCII punctuation (r12a). Statements end with **.**, questions with ?, as in English; **no danda** in course text. `pracNorm` strips . ? ! anyway, so answers and tiles may carry or omit them.

- **Learners:** the US figure is a news report of an ACS analysis; the Census table was not opened; no survey of motives was found.

**The roadmap and the brief**
- **Present before past** (the brief's change): stage 3 is now `like`, `future`, `continuous`, `past`, `past-neg`, `and-then`; `future` moves from stage 4 into stage 3 and is titled "Habitual and future" (§8).
- **`vowel-signs` moves from 4th to 2nd** (a separate change, §8).
- `profile.json` carries fields beyond the brief's shape, as en-pa and en-ur do: `tilesDefault`, `pronAnswers`, `pronAnswersKeepSpaces`, `pronAnswersScript`, `romaAlmost`, `voiceMatch`, `pronAnswersWarn`.
- The brief's own example డాక్టర్ని contains a ZWNJ (U+200C), which the validator rejects; the course writes డాక్టర్ని (§10.3).
- The brief asks for "Western digits in prices": real prices are in Western digits, and the course says so, but Telugu strings write numbers as words, because the generator, the voice and the matcher then agree (§2.8, §10.3).

**Not verified at all**
- How the Azure voice reads lone letters, ts / dz, length pairs and English loans (request 9).
- That learners read ē / ō and the dots easily; no study compares Telugu romanisations for learners.
- Which romanisation habits Telugu typists really use (*th* for dental t, *ee* for ī): request 5 should be checked against a sample of real typing before it is built.
- That the app's body font renders ē ō ḷ ṅ cleanly.
