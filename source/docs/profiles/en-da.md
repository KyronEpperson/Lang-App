# English speakers learning Danish: course profile

Course `en-da`: Danish for English speakers. Every explanation, prompt, `why`, `hint`, meaning and card note is in English. Answers, examples and card targets are in Danish. Machine-readable twin: `tools/target/da/profile.json`. The pronunciation aid is generated by `tools/target/da/respell.js` (§6). Writers never hand-write a `pron`.

**Evidence tags.**
- **[research-backed]**: an empirical study that was opened. The study is named. **(second-hand)** means the finding was read in another source's summary, not in the original.
- **[curriculum convention]**: what a textbook or a course does.
- **[practitioner opinion]**: a teacher's or a site's stated view, with no data behind it.
- **[inference]**: this profile's own reasoning. No native speaker has checked it.

Untagged rules in §6 to §10 are design decisions, not findings.

## 1. Summary

- **Course:** `en-da`, Standard Danish, Copenhagen pronunciation, for adult beginners, 26 lessons in 4 stages and 18 decks in 5 groups. All explanation is in US English.
- **Register:** *du* everywhere a person is addressed. *De* is recognition only. Every English "you" is cued "(to one person)" or "(to several people)", because the split is *du* vs *I*, not politeness (§2.3).
- **Biggest traps:** soft *d*, silent *d* and swallowed endings; the stød, which the aid does not mark; *en* / *et* and the definite ending; verb second and where *ikke* sits (§4).
- **Standing rule (§7):** every practice set re-tests (a) common gender vs neuter and (b) verb second and the place of *ikke*. Not verb endings: the present is one *-r* for every person.
- **Pron (§6):** required, generated. It is good at soft *d* (`dh`), silent *d*, and swallowed *-e / -er / -en / -et*. It does not mark the stød. Vowels are a coarse cover. *hun* and *hund* come out the same.
- **Lessons (§8):** all 25 roadmap items, reordered (*en-et* before pronouns; *inversion* up next to *negation*), plus one added lesson, `traps`. Compliments is pointed at from `likes`, not from a second new lesson.
- **Typing (§10):** `accents: false`, keys `æ ø å é`. A missing *æ*, *ø*, *å* or *é* is wrong, not "almost", because the app would fold *å* to *a* and *æ* to *ae*. No app change is required for the course to run.
- **Evidence:** phonetics from Wikipedia's Danish phonology page and from Schachtenhaufen's schwa paper; grammar errors from DanskGrammatik's mistakes page and from Holmes & Lundskær-Nielsen's grammar preview; the course order from the UBC DANI 100 syllabus. No native speaker has checked this profile (§11).

## 2. Variant, register and voice

### 2.1 Variant: Standard Danish, Copenhagen

The roadmap sets "Standard Danish (Denmark, Copenhagen pronunciation)" and the voice `da-DK`. Decisions that follow:

- **Pronunciation** is the careful Copenhagen form a `da-DK` voice aims at, not fast connected speech and not Jutland. Wikipedia's Danish phonology page says /p t k/ are aspirated and that Copenhagen /t/ is affricated [tsʰ], while some non-Copenhagen standard speech has aspiration without the affrication. /b d g/ are voiceless and unaspirated. Syllable-final /r/ is [ɐ̯]; syllable-initial /r/ is a uvular fricative or, more often, an approximant. The aid keeps onset *r* as the letter `r` and drops a coda *r* (§6). It does not write `ts` for every *t*: that would lie in clusters. `soft-d` teaches the puff and the Copenhagen *t*.
- **Spelling** follows the current Retskrivningsordbogen. The digital dictionary's own guide (ro.dsn.dk, "Vejledning til indhold") describes it as the word list of *rigssprog*. sproget.dk's July 2023 note says the 5th edition, out in November 2024, drops the recommendation to omit the comma before a subordinate clause, and that omitting it stays legal. One house rule is in §2.5.
- **Vocabulary** is the Denmark word. A card may note a place-name spelling (*Aarhus*, *Aalborg*, *Aabenraa* use *Aa*; *å* in a place name is never against the rules, sproget.dk "Bogstavet å"). No celebrity names. Prices are in *kroner*. Cities used in examples: København, Aarhus, Aalborg, Odense.

### 2.2 What the variant changes in the roadmap

Nothing in the roadmap is a non-Denmark form. The reorder in §8 is for English-speaking beginners, not for the variant.

### 2.3 Forms of "you"

Wiktionary's Danish pronoun table (on the *mig* entry, opened) labels *De / Dem / Deres* as "formal (uncommon)". *du / dig* is "modern / informal". Plural "you" is *I / jer / jeres*, and that *I* is always a capital.

- **Produce *du*** with strangers, shop staff, a doctor, a teacher, and friends. Politeness is *tak*, *gerne*, *vil du være sød at*, *værsgo*, *selv tak*. There is no everyday "please". DanskGrammatik's mistakes page says *venligst* is correct but stiff in a café, and that it belongs on signs and in letters. *Vær venlig og…* at the table sounds like an order. The café line is *Jeg vil gerne have en kaffe, tak.*
- ***De*** is recognition only: one check question in `greetings` ("which one is the stiff, old-fashioned you?"), never a typed answer, unless a prompt says "(formal letter)". *De*, *Dem* and *Deres* are always capital in model answers.
- **Plural *I*** is taught in `pronouns` and produced when the prompt says "(to several people)". *I* is capital; *i* is the preposition "in".
- **Every English "you"** carries a cue. "(to one person)" means *du*. "(to several people)" means *I*. The cue is not about politeness. This is the opposite of the German course's *Sie*-first rule, and that is the right call for Danish.

### 2.4 Voice of the English text

US English. "Apartment", "fall" only if a season is needed (it is not in this course), "truck" is not needed. Glosses say "a" and "the" even though Danish often has no separate word for "the".

### 2.5 Spelling house rule

**Write the comma before *at*, *fordi*, *hvis* and *når*.** Both systems are legal. sproget.dk (3 July 2023, Andrea Stengaard) says the 2024 dictionary withdraws the recommendation to leave that comma out, and that leaving it out is still allowed if a text is consistent. The course writes it because the comma is where the word order changes (*at han ikke kan*, not *at han kan ikke*). Learners will see texts without it. Say that once, in `negation`.

The rest of the house style, so writers do not invent a second one:

- Ordinary words use *å*, not *aa*. *å* replaced *aa* in the 1948 reform (sproget.dk, "Bogstavet å"). *aa* at the start of *Aarhus*, *Aalborg* and *Aabenraa* is the local spelling; *å* is never wrong there. Personal names are spelled as the bearer spells them (§ 61.3, as sproget.dk reports it).
- Days, months, languages and nationalities take a small letter: *tirsdag*, *februar*, *dansk*, *en englænder*. The country has a capital: *England*. [practitioner opinion] DanskGrammatik's mistakes page, which says it has not been checked by a native speaker.
- Nouns are not capitalised. Only the first word of a sentence, *I*, *De / Dem / Deres*, and proper names take a capital.
- *én* and *ét* keep the acute only when the number "one" has to be told apart from the article. Elsewhere write *en* and *et*.
- Adverbs of manner are written with *-t* (*dejligt*). The 2024 change to the adverb rule was not opened as its own page; do not cite it as a new law (§11).

## 3. Glossary

Use these English terms, each once. Do not swap in a synonym.

| Term | Use it for | Do not also say |
|---|---|---|
| common gender | nouns that take *en*. About 75 per cent of nouns (Holmes & Lundskær-Nielsen, *Danish: A Comprehensive Grammar*, preview). | masculine, feminine, "the en-gender" |
| neuter | nouns that take *et* | "the et-gender", "intetkøn" in a lesson |
| en-word / et-word | the same split, when the article is the point | "common noun" (that means a different thing in English) |
| indefinite article | *en* and *et* | "a-word" |
| definite ending | the suffix *-en / -et / -ne* (*bilen*, *huset*) | "the-word", "definite article" as if it were a separate word |
| verb second | the conjugated verb is the second element of a statement | "verb-second rule" and "V2" in the same lesson; pick verb second |
| second element | a whole phrase counts as one (*i morgen tager jeg*) | "second word" |
| inversion | subject after the verb when something else is first | a new term for the same fact in the same paragraph |
| main clause / subordinate clause | where *ikke* sits: after the verb, or before it | "dependent clause" |
| soft d | *d* after a vowel (*mad*, *gade*). Danish *blødt d* | "th", as the name of the sound |
| stød | the catch that separates *hund* from *hun* | "glottal stop" as if it were a full stop the learner should punch |
| swallowed ending | unstressed *-e, -er, -en, -et* | "silent e" for all four |
| false friend | *gift*, *fabrik*, *eventuelt* | "false cognate" |
| modal verb | *vil, kan, skal, må* plus an infinitive with no *at* | "helping verb" (English *do* is the thing Danish does not have) |

*han* and *hun* are "he" and "she". They are not the genders of nouns. Holmes's preview says a common-gender noun for a person takes *han* or *hun*, not *den*, when the person is known. Say that once, in `pronouns`.

The present tense is one form in *-r* for every person. Do not set up a six-row person table. There is no case system to teach. Do not use the word "case" for letters or for nouns.

## 4. Error zones of English speakers, ranked

Each pair is the wrong English-shaped sentence, then the form the course teaches. Grammar pairs are from DanskGrammatik's mistakes page unless another source is named. That page says it has not been checked by a native speaker, and that its audio is a computer voice. Treat it as [practitioner opinion], not as a study.

1. **Soft *d*, silent *d*, swallowed endings, stød.** *mad* said with an English *d*; *hund* said as *hun*. Right: soft *d* in *mad* and *gade*; *d* silent in *land*, *kold*, *hund*; the stød is the only difference between *hun* and *hund*. Wikipedia: soft *d* is a velarized laminal alveolar approximant, often heard as [l], and only rarely a fricative. DanskGrammatik's page compares it to a weak "th" and calls that comparison an approximation. The course does not. The aid writes `dh`, not `th` (§6).
2. **Verb second.** *I morgen jeg tager toget.* Right: *I morgen tager jeg toget.* A subordinate clause in front is the first element: *Når jeg kommer hjem, laver jeg kaffe*, not *…, jeg laver kaffe*. [practitioner opinion]
3. ***ikke* and no *do*.** *Jeg gør ikke spise.* *Jeg altid cykler.* *Mads siger, at han kan ikke komme.* Right: *Jeg spiser ikke.* *Jeg cykler altid.* *…at han ikke kan komme.* *ikke* goes after the verb in a main clause and before the verb in a subordinate clause. Yes/no questions start with the verb: *Arbejder du?*, not *Gør du arbejder?* [practitioner opinion]
4. ***en* / *et* and the definite ending.** *en hus.* *Ligger toilet ude?* *Det er min bilen.* Right: *et hus.* *toilettet.* *min bil* or *bilen*, never both. Holmes: learn each noun with its gender; the definite singular ending is *-(e)n* or *-(e)t*; the plural definite is *-ne*. [curriculum convention] for the terms; the wrong sentences are [practitioner opinion].
5. **Jobs, nationalities, and adjective endings.** *Jeg er en amerikaner.* *den stor have.* *en dejlig vejr.* Right: *Jeg er amerikaner* (the article comes back with an adjective: *en dygtig tømrer*). *den store have.* *dejligt vejr* (*vejr* is neuter and takes no article). [practitioner opinion]
6. **One present, no *-ing*, modal without *at*, strong past.** *Jeg arbejde.* *Jeg er spiser.* *Vi vil at købe.* *I går drikkede vi.* Right: *Jeg arbejder* (every person). *Jeg spiser* or *Jeg sidder og spiser.* *Vi vil gerne købe.* *I går drak vi.* [practitioner opinion]
7. **Prepositions copied from English.** *Jeg ringer dig.* *Vi venter for bussen.* *glade med.* *afhænger på.* Right: *ringer til*, *venter på*, *glade for*, *afhænger af*. [practitioner opinion]
8. **False friends.** *Eventuelt fik vi lejligheden* for "eventually". *en fabrik* for cloth. *tak for din gift* for a present. Right: *til sidst* or *endelig*; *stof*; *en gave*. *gift* is "married" or "poison". [practitioner opinion]
9. **Politeness.** *en te, venligst* at the counter. Right: *Jeg vil gerne have en te, tak.* [practitioner opinion]
10. **Capitals.** *en Tirsdag i Februar*, *taler Dansk*. Right: *tirsdag*, *februar*, *dansk*. [practitioner opinion]
11. **Numbers 50–90.** The roadmap names *halvtreds, tres, halvfjerds, firs, halvfems* as a hard spot. The historical arithmetic (2½ × 20 and the rest) was not re-opened (§11). Teach the words as chunks. Do not derive them in the lesson.

Not ranked, because the aid shows them and they are not a grammar error: *jeg / mig / dig* as "yay / my / die" instead of the diphthong. Wiktionary: *jeg* [jɑ̈(j)], *mig* [mɑj], *dig* [dɑj].

## 5. Learners: who, why, what first

UBC DANI 100 (2022W, opened) is an elementary course with no prerequisite: "intended for students who have no knowledge of Danish." Students with native or near-native Danish, Norwegian or Swedish are sent to the intermediate courses instead. The textbook named on the syllabus is Puls 1. The weekly order is the backbone of the roadmap: alphabet and introductions, vowels, negation and SVA, nationalities, questions and pronouns, numbers, indefinite nouns, time and the definite singular, inversion, weekdays, family, past tense at the end of term. Pronunciation is taught beside the grammar (vowels in week 2; soft *d*, silent *d* and *-er* in week 3; the uvular *r* and reduction in week 12). [curriculum convention]

Who the US adult is, beyond that university course, was not found in an enrollment study that was opened. Treat the next sentence as [inference]: the likely learner has a partner or family in Denmark, a term in Copenhagen, or family history, and is studying alone. The course is built for that person: *du* from the first greeting, prices in kroner, a café order, the numbers that appear on a receipt.

What they need first, from the errors in §4 and from UBC's own early weeks: the three extra letters and the soft *d* before any sentence they will say twice; then *en* / *et*, because every noun from then on carries it; then verb second and *ikke*, which UBC already pairs in week 3. Noun gender in the UBC week list comes in week 6. This course moves it earlier (§8). That is [inference], not what UBC does.

## 6. Pronunciation aid (`pron`)

**How good it can be, in one place.**

The aid is a careful Copenhagen citation form written in English letters. It is good at the three things the roadmap calls hardest in the spelling:

- **Soft *d*** is `dh`. That is not English "th" and not a hard *d*. The tongue stays down. Learners often hear [l] (Wikipedia, citing the descriptions it summarises). `dh` is a cover so the letter *d* is not read as English *d*.
- **Silent *d*** in *nd, ld, rd* is dropped (*land* `lan`, *kold* `kawl`). *rd* then falls together with a plain coda *r*, which is also dropped.
- **Swallowed endings:** unstressed *e* is `uh`, *-er* is `uh` (the *r* is not an English "er"), *-en* is `uhn`, *-et* is `uhdh` (the *t* is soft *d*). A lone *r* before that *e* is dropped. An *r* inside a cluster is kept (*venstre* `VEHN-struh`, *bedre* `BEH-dhruh`).

It does **not** mark the stød. Wikipedia: the stød is creaky voice on some stressed syllables with a long vowel or a short vowel plus a sonorant, and it is not predictable from the spelling alone (historical monosyllables, the definite *-et*, loanwords with final stress). A mark the rules cannot get right would be worse than no mark. *hun* and *hund* are both `hoon`. *mor* and *mord* are both `moh`. *man* and *mand* are both `man`. The difference is the stød, and `soft-d` teaches those pairs by ear. *far* `fa` and *får* `faw` stay different, because *å* is a different vowel, not a stød.

It is **rough on vowels**. One symbol per letter. Length is mostly collapsed. Short *i* is written `eh` and short *o* is written `aw` only when the spelling rule thinks the vowel is short, and that rule is the part this profile trusts least (§6.5, §11).

It is a **citation form**. Schachtenhaufen (opened; DanPASS, 27 speakers of standard Copenhagen Danish) reports schwa assimilation in roughly half of all cases and total schwa loss in roughly a quarter, more in some positions than others, and the loss depends on the next word. A final schwa after a consonant is usually kept in a careful isolated pronunciation (*lille*). The aid keeps `uh`. It does not try to predict fast-speech loss. DanskGrammatik's page says fast *havde* drops both *v* and *d* ("ha-e"). The aid says `HA-dhuh`: *v* already gone, soft *d* still there.

**Options weighed.**

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| (a) English-letter respelling, generated | Readable on day one. Same shape as the Spanish and German tools. Can show soft *d*, silent *d*, and swallowed endings, which are the point. | Cannot show the stød, the exact vowel, the Copenhagen affricated *t*, or fast-speech loss. Some pairs collapse. |
| (b) IPA | Exact, including the stød. | A US adult beginner cannot read it [inference], and a generated IPA that guesses the stød would still be wrong. |
| (c) No aid | The voice can carry the sound. | The spelling is the trap. *mad*, *gade*, *huset*, *hund* are misread on day one. |

**Decision: (a).** `pron: "required"`. `node tools/target/da/respell.js "<text>"` prints `<text> -> <pron>`. The module exports `respell` and `pronPattern`. The same spelling always gives the same `pron`.

**How a word is analysed**, in order:

1. The word *at* is special, because it is two words. Wiktionary: the conjunction "that" is [æd̥] or [æ]; the infinitive marker is [ʌ], a homophone of *og*, and the full [æd̥] is the phrase-initial or formal reading. Before *du, dig, jeg, mig, han, ham, hun, hende, vi, os, de, dem, i, jer, man, den, det, der, en, et, som, hvis, når, fordi* the aid says `a`. Otherwise it says `aw` (*at spise*). Other "that"-clauses (*at huset er stort*) are wrongly `aw`. Do not build a card whose point is that second reading (§6.5).
2. The word list `LEX` (49 entries). Whole words only. Endings are not derived, because a Danish ending changes the sound.
3. A number compound *en/to/tre/… + og + tyve/tredive/…* is split, and the *og* is `aw` (*femogtyve* `fehm-aw-TEW-wuh`, *toogtyve* `toh-aw-TEW-wuh`). *sprog*, *toget* and *også* are not split.
4. *be-* is unstressed only on a short list (*betale, begynde, …*). *bedre* and *bede* stay whole.
5. *-tion* is `shohn` and takes the stress (*station* `sta-SHOHN`).
6. Otherwise the first syllable is stressed. A word of four or more syllables that got this default writes `stress unchecked (default first syllable): …` on stderr. Stdout stays `<text> -> <pron>`.

**Homographs** get one reading, except *at*. Do not ask the aid to distinguish *så* "saw" from *så* "so": both are `saw`.

### 6.1 The key

**Format.** Lowercase ASCII. One space between words. A hyphen between syllables. In a word of two or more syllables, exactly one syllable is in CAPITALS. A one-syllable word is all lowercase. No punctuation, no *æ ø å*, no digits. Write numbers as words.

**Vowels.** One symbol per letter. Long and short share it, except *i* and *o*.

| Letter | Aid | Example | What it is asking the learner to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | a | tak `tak`, gade `GA-dhuh` | The "a" of *cat* or *bed*, not the "ah" of *father*. Dark *a* in *tak* and the front *a* in *gade* are the same symbol on purpose. |
| e stressed | eh | hedder `HEH-dhuh` | The vowel of *bed*. |
| e unstressed | uh | gade `GA-dhuh`, taler `TA-luh` | A weak "uh". Not English "er". |
| i long | ee | bil `beel`, min `meen` | The vowel of *see*. |
| i short | eh | ikke `EH-guh`, lille `LEH-luh` | Copenhagen short *i* is the *e* of *bed*. This is the coarse rule, and it is wrong when the length guess is wrong (§6.5). |
| o long | oh | stor `stoh`, bor `boh` | The vowel of *go*, without a strong glide. |
| o short | aw | komme `KAW-muh`, ost `awst`, kold `kawl` | The vowel of *caught* / *lot*. Same symbol as *å*. |
| u | oo | hus `hoos`, hun `hoon` | The vowel of *food*, both lengths. |
| y | ew | syv would be the same pattern | "ee" with rounded lips. The *w* is not the glide of English *few*. `vowels` must say that. |
| æ | ae | mælk `maelk`, være `VAE-uh` | The vowel of *bed*, spelled *ae* so it is not read as *å*. |
| ø | oe | øl `oel`, rød `roedh` | "ay" with rounded lips. Not "ur" and not "er". |
| å | aw | får `faw`, gå `gaw` | Same as short *o*. Length is collapsed. |
| ej, aj | ai | hej `hai`, nej `nai` | The vowel of *eye*. |
| øj | oy | højre `HOY-uh` | The vowel of *boy*. |
| é | eh | (only in *én, ét, idé, café*) | Same as stressed *e*. The acute is spelling, not a new sound. |

**Consonants.**

| Spelling | Aid | Example |
|---|---|---|
| d after a vowel, and dd | dh | mad `madh`, gade `GA-dhuh`, sidde `SEH-dhuh` |
| d in nd, ld | dropped; the vowel is short | land `lan`, kold `kawl`, finde `FEH-nuh` |
| d in rd | dropped with the r | mord `moh` (same as mor) |
| d before t or s | dropped | godt `gawt`, plads `plas`, fedt `feht` |
| coda r, and a lone r before unstressed e | dropped | bor `boh`, taler `TA-luh`, store `STOH-uh` |
| r in a cluster before unstressed e | kept | venstre `VEHN-struh`, bedre `BEH-dhruh` |
| onset r | r | rød `roedh` | The letter only. The sound is in the throat. `vowels` and `soft-d` say so. |
| hj, hv at the start | y, v | hjem `yehm`, hvad `vadh` |
| j | y | ja `ya` |
| g after a front vowel, before a vowel or at the end of the word | y | pige `PEE-yuh` |
| g after a back vowel, in the same spot | w | have `HA-wuh` |
| kk, pp, tt between vowels | g, b, d | ikke `EH-guh`, drikker `DREH-guh`, datter `DA-duh` | No puff of air. Not a different letter in the spelling. |
| ng | ng | ringe `REHNG-uh` |
| sj, and -tion | sh, shohn | station `sta-SHOHN` |
| other letters | themselves | tak `tak` |

Single *p t k* stay *p t k*. The lesson, not the aid, says the puff of air and the Copenhagen *t*.

Vowels: `ee ew eh oh oo aw ae oe uh ai oy a i`, and the same in capitals. Consonants: `dh ng sh` and `b d f g h k l m n p r s t v w y z`. No *j, c, q, x*, no *æ ø å*. One capital syllable in a longer word; a one-syllable word is lowercase.

It accepts every worked example below and every sample card in §9. It rejects most raw Danish (*øl, jeg, gade, huset*). It does **not** reject every raw word. *mad*, *land* and *bil* match the pattern and are the wrong pron (`madh`, `lan`, `beel`). *tak*, *han* and *dansk* match because they are already legal respellings. The pattern cannot prove a `pron` was generated. The generator can.

### 6.4 The generator

`tools/target/da/respell.js`. No dependencies.

- `node tools/target/da/respell.js "Huset er rødt."` prints one line, `Huset er rødt. -> HOO-suhdh eh roedh`.
- `node tools/target/da/respell.js --pattern` prints the regex.

- A digit, or a character that is not a Danish letter, throws. Write numbers as words.

### 6.5 Words it gets wrong, or refuses to distinguish

**Same string on purpose, because the stød is not marked.** *hun* = *hund* = `hoon`. *mor* = *mord* = `moh`. *man* = *mand* = `man`. *der* and *det* are both `deh` (*der* loses its *r*; *det* loses its *t*). Do not use the aid to tell these apart. *far* / *får* and *har* / *hår* stay different.

**The word *at*.** Infinitive `aw`, conjunction before a pronoun `a`. *at huset er stort* is wrongly `aw`.

**Vowel length, the weak spot.** Short *i* → `eh` and short *o* → `aw` only when a doubled letter, *nd/ld*, or a final *p t k s f* closes the vowel. A single *l, m, n, b* does not. So *job* comes out `yohb` and is short in real speech [inference]. *ost* is `awst` but *osten* is `OHS-tuhn`, because the cluster was split. *formiddag* is `FOH-meh-dhaw`: the *o* should be the *aw* of *ost*, and the *g* of *dag* is not a *w*. *til* is in the word list as `tehl` on the pattern of *vil*, which Wiktionary gives as [ve(l)]; the *til* page itself was not opened.

**Final *g* and the days.** *dag* is listed as `da`. *bog, tog, sprog* are listed with `w` (*baw, taw, spraw*). Those are [inference], not a page that was opened. Day names still run through the rules: *tirsdag* `TEE-sdaw`, *mandag* `MA-naw`, *fredag* `FREH-dhaw`, *lørdag* `LOE-raw`, *søndag* `SOE-naw`. The *g* of *dag* is the silent one. Trust the voice for the days.

**Stress the rules do not know.** First syllable, unless the word is in the list, is a *be-* verb, or ends in *-tion*. stderr warns from four syllables up (*amerikaner, eventuelt, gymnasium, stationen, formiddag* is only three, so it does not warn, and it is still wrong). *februar, minut, kvarter, toilet, forældre* are not in the list. *københavn* is listed as `koe-buhn-HAWN` (stress on the last syllable). That stress is the single listing this profile is least willing to defend (§11).

**Endings the rules over-split.** Past *-ede*: *boede* `BOH-uh-dhuh`, *arbejdede* `A-bai-dhuh-dhuh`. *sagde* `SAG-duh` keeps a hard *g* and a *d*; fast speech does not. *brødet* `BROE-dhuhdh` stacks two soft d's. *ligeud* `LEE-yuh-oodh` is three syllables.

**Teens and a few numbers.** *tretten* `TREH-duhn`, *sytten* `SEW-duhn`, *seksten* `SEHK-stuhn`, *fjorten* `FYOH-tuhn` are the rule, not a dictionary. *halvtreds* `hal-TREHS`, *tres* `trehs`, *halvfjerds* `hal-FYEHS`, *firs* `feers`, *halvfems* `hal-FEHMS`, *fyrre* `FOE-uh`, *tolv* `tawl` are in the list so the lesson can say them. The list is [inference]. *halv* stays `halv`; the *v* is often silent, and the aid still writes it.

**Fast speech the aid will not copy.** Schwa loss (§6, Schachtenhaufen). *havde* as "ha-e". *v* after a long *u* (Wikipedia: /v/ is often dropped there). *have* is `HA-wuh`, with the *w* still written.

**Word-list items that are cited, and the ones that are not.** Cited from a page that was opened: *og* and infinitive *at* (Wiktionary *at*, homophones, [ʌ]); *det* [de]; *jeg, mig, dig*; *vil* [ve(l)]. Not opened, and therefore [inference]: *sig* `sai` (same pattern as *mig* and *dig*), *til, hvor, kom, som, om, havde, meget, dag, bog, tog, sprog, også, gik*, the greetings, the city names, the number list, *smørrebrød, rugbrød, kanelsnegl, bedstemor, bedstefar*.

## 7. Standing practice rule

**Rule: gender, and verb second with *ikke*.** Every practice set keeps re-testing two strands:

- **(a) Common gender and neuter.** *en* vs *et*; the definite ending; the adjective *-t* (neuter) and *-e* (definite or plural); *min / mit / mine* when the noun's gender is the point.
- **(b) Verb second and the place of *ikke*.** The verb is the second element. Something else in front puts the subject after the verb. *ikke* (and *altid, aldrig*) goes after the verb in a main clause and before the verb in a subordinate clause. A yes/no question starts with the verb.

**Why these two.** [inference], from §4 and from UBC week 3, which already teaches negation and SVA together, and from Holmes's statement that gender is on three quarters of the nouns and has to be learned with each one. Both show up in almost every sentence after lesson 6 (*et hus*, *i dag arbejder jeg*), so the rule can be met before the course has case, which Danish does not have, and before it has person endings, which the present does not have.

**Not the standing rule:** the *-r* itself, false friends, prepositions, the stød, numbers. They are recycled as neighbours. A `why` that only says "the present ends in -r" does not count.

**Count** (`minOrder: 2`, `minType: 2`): at least two `order` items and two `type` items hinge on the rule, and their `why` matches the keyword regex.

**What counts as hinging:**

- An `order` item hinges when the `why` matches and the tiles turn on *en/et*, a definite ending, an adjective ending, *ikke* in the wrong slot, or the subject on the wrong side of the verb.
- A `type` item hinges when the `why` matches and the answer is an *en*-word or an *et*-word, a definite form, an agreeing adjective, a fronted phrase, or a clause with *ikke* in the position the clause requires.
- A possessive hinge has to say the gender: "hus is neuter: mit hus." The sentence "min agrees with the noun" does **not** match the keywords. That was tested.

**Lessons where this is thinnest:**

- `alphabet`, `vowels`, `soft-d`: the deck nouns are *en*-words and *et*-words, and the `why` says so (*æble* is an et-word). Verb second can be one short sentence already (*det er et æble*) if the prompt starts with something other than the subject, or it waits; two order items can be gender and two type items can be gender until `negation`. From `negation` on, one of each strand is the expectation. The validator only counts keyword hits, not which strand. Writers still cover both strands from `negation` onward.
- `numbers`: *en krone* / *et minut* if a unit is gendered, plus *halvtreds kroner koster det* with the verb second. The number word itself is not the hinge.

**Keywords** (`standingRule.keywords`, flags `i`):

```
\bcommon gender\b|\bneuter\b|\ben-word\b|\bet-word\b|\bdefinite ending\b|\bindefinite article\b|\badjective ending\b|\bverb second\b|\bsecond element\b|\binversion\b|\bikke (?:goes|comes|sits|before|after)\b|\bmain clause\b|\bsubordinate clause\b
```

Use "common gender", "neuter", "en-word", "et-word", "definite ending", "indefinite article", "adjective ending", "verb second", "second element", "inversion", "ikke goes / comes / sits / before / after", "main clause", "subordinate clause".

These are not keywords, and they were tested so that they stay out: bare "ikke", bare "plural", bare "common", "the verb ending", "soft d", "stød", "du", "halv tre".

## 8. Lessons (final order)

- `en-et` moves to the start of stage 2, before `pronouns`. UBC teaches indefinite nouns in week 6, after numbers. What each lesson is for:

1. `alphabet`. The 29 letters, *æ ø å* at the end, *é* for *én / ét / idé / café*. How to read the aid (§6.1), in one screen: capitals are stress, `dh` is soft d, `uh` is a swallowed e. Trap: reading *æ* as *ae*, *ø* as "er", *å* as *aa* or as *a*.
2. `vowels`. The key in §6.1. *y* is "ee" with rounded lips, and the *w* of `ew` is not a glide. Short *i* heard as *e* (*ikke*). *far* vs *får*. Trap: one English value per letter.
3. `soft-d`. Soft *d* (`dh`, not th), silent *d* in *nd / ld / rd*, swallowed *-e -er -en -et*, doubled *kk pp tt* with no puff, Copenhagen *t* as a puff the aid does not spell. The stød: *hun* / *hund*, *mor* / *mord*, heard, not marked. Trap: a hard *d* in *mad*; ignoring the catch.
4. `greetings`. *hej* all day. *godmorgen, goddag, godaften, godnat* for the clock. *tak, værsgo, selv tak, farvel, undskyld*. *du* to everyone; *De* recognised and not produced (§2.3). Trap: *venligst* at the counter; *De* to a stranger.
5. `intro`. *Jeg hedder…, Jeg kommer fra…, Jeg bor i…, Jeg er…* Articles on the nouns are chunks until lesson 6. Trap: *Jeg er en…* for a nationality (fixed properly in `nationality`).
6. `en-et`. Two genders, about 75 per cent common (Holmes). No reliable rule; a few tendencies may be mentioned as tendencies (Holmes's preview lists suffixes). Every new noun from here on is stored with *en* or *et*. Trap: *en hus*.
7. `pronouns`. *jeg du han hun vi I de*, *mig dig sig*, *den det*. *de* is "they" (`dee`), *det* is "it/that" (`deh`). *I* capital, *i* "in". *De* again, still recognition. Trap: *mig* read as "mig" with a hard g; *de* and *det* confused, which the aid also confuses (§6.5).
8. `present`. One *-r* for every person. No *-ing*: *jeg spiser*, *jeg sidder og spiser*. Trap: *jeg arbejde*; *jeg er spiser*.
9. `negation`. *ikke* after the verb in a main clause, before it after *at / fordi / hvis / når*. The comma is written (§2.5). No *gør*. *altid* and *aldrig* sit with *ikke*. Trap: *jeg ikke spiser*; *at han kan ikke*.
10. `inversion`. *I dag arbejder jeg.* The verb is the second element, not the second word. Trap: *I dag jeg arbejder*.
11. `questions`. *hvad, hvor, hvem, hvornår, hvordan*, and verb-first yes/no. No *do*. Trap: *Hvor du bor?* *Gør du…?*
12. `nationality`. *Jeg er amerikaner. Jeg taler engelsk.* No article. Small letters. The article returns with an adjective. Trap: *Jeg er en amerikaner. Jeg taler Engelsk.*
13. `numbers`. 0–100 as chunks, especially 50–90. *kroner*. *en og tyve* is not how 21 is said: it is *enogtyve*, and the *og* in the middle sounds like the word *og*. Trap: reading 25 as "five and twenty" in the English order, or pronouncing *og* as "ohg".
14. `plural`. *-er, -e*, no ending, a few with a vowel change (*barn / børn*, *mand / mænd*). The indefinite plural has no article. Trap: *en biler*; a regular English *-s*.
15. `definite`. *bilen, huset, børnene*. Never *min bilen*. *livet, toilettet* for things English leaves bare. Trap: a separate word for "the"; the double marking.
16. `time`. *Den er tre. halv tre* is 2:30, not 3:30. *kvart over, kvart i*. Add *om morgenen* or *om aftenen* when the 12-hour phrase is ambiguous. The 24-hour clock on a timetable. The *v* of *halv* is often silent; the aid still writes it (§6.5). Trap: *halv tre* read as 3:30.
17. `days`. *mandag* through *søndag*, small letters. *om morgenen, om aftenen*. The aid is rough on *-dag* (§6.5); the voice is the authority. Trap: *Mandag*.
18. `modals`. *vil, kan, skal, må* plus a bare infinitive. *vil* is "want", not the future. *skal* is obligation or a plan. Trap: *vil at købe*; *jeg vil* for "I will" as a future.
19. `cafe`. *Jeg vil gerne have…, tak. Hvad koster det?* *en øl, en kaffe, rugbrød, smørrebrød, en kanelsnegl*. No *venligst*. Trap: the stiff please.
20. `family`. *mor, far, søster, bror, barn, børn, børnene*. Plurals from lesson 14 in a real set. Trap: *forældrene* stress, which the aid gets wrong (§6.5); teach the word by ear.
21. `possessives`. *min mit mine* agree with the noun, not with the owner. *mit hus*, *min bil*, *mine børn*. Never the definite ending as well. Trap: *min bilen*; *min* for a neuter noun.
22. `adjectives`. *en stor bil, et stort hus, den store bil, store huse*. After *er*, no ending: *bilen er stor*. Trap: *et stor hus*; an ending after *er*.
23. `likes`. *Jeg kan godt lide…, Jeg elsker…* This lesson also holds the Compliments deck: *det smager godt, godt klaret, hvor flot*, and the answers *tak, selv tak, tak skal du have*. Trap: *jeg kan lide* with the *godt* missing; answering a compliment by deflecting in an English way the lesson has not taught. Do not invent a Danish deflection. *tak* is the answer.
24. `directions`. *til højre, til venstre, ligeud, hvor er…?* Trap: *til venstre* with the *r* pronounced as English "er". The aid keeps the cluster *r* (`VEHN-struh`) and drops the ending.
25. `past`. Regular *-ede* (*arbejdede, boede*) and the short list *var, havde, kom, gik, fik, så, drak, spiste*. The aid over-splits *-ede* (§6.5). Trap: *drikkede*; *jeg har arbejdet* for a spoken past (the perfect is not this lesson).
26. `traps`. The false friends and calques of §4 in full sentences: *gift / gave, fabrik / stof, eventuelt / til sidst, en tømrer* with no article, *venter på, ringer til*. Trap: the English meaning of a Danish look-alike.

Writer notes, for every lesson:

- The practice set meets §7.
- The last two items may bring back a neighbour: *en/et*, *ikke*, a soft *d*.
- Every "you" has the cue in §2.3.
- Pairs the validator folds together (*får / far*, *én / en*, *ét / et*) are `!strict` even though `accents` is false (§10).
- Do not put *hun* and *hund* in one typed item as if the spelling were the only difference. The stød is the difference, and typing cannot hear it. Use them in `soft-d` as a listening check.

## 10. Typing and script

**Script.** Latin, plus *æ ø å* and the acute on *é* (*én, ét, idé, café*). `targetScript`: `[A-Za-zÆØÅæøåÉé]`. Every Danish answer contains at least one of these.

**Accent bar** (`keys`): `æ ø å é`. Capitals are not on the bar. The matcher lower-cases before it compares, so a capital is never the thing that fails.

**`accents: false`.** This is the opposite of the German course, and it is deliberate.

What the app does today (`src/app.html`, `foldAcc` and `accLoose`, read, not changed):

- `accLoose()` is true only when the course's `accents` flag is true. With `accents: false`, a missing letter is wrong, not "almost".
- If the flag were true, `foldAcc` would map *æ/Æ* to *ae*, and it would strip the ring from *å/Å* (the ring is a combining mark after NFD), so *får* would fold to *far*, *gå* to *ga*, *år* to *ar*. *é* would fold to *e*, so *én* would fold to *en* and *ét* to *et*. *ø/Ø* would not fold: *øl* stays *øl*.
- That fold is the wrong default for Danish. *får* and *far* are different words. *én* and *en* are the number and the article. *mælk* typed *maelk* is a different spelling, not a slip the course wants to accept. German can fold *ä* to *a* because the umlaut is often a typing slip. Danish *å* is a different letter, and the fold would hide it.

So the course sets `accents: false`. A missing *æ*, *ø*, *å* or *é* is a wrong answer. The accent bar is how the learner types the real letter. Writers do not list *ae, o, a, e* as accepted alternatives.

**What the validator does anyway.** `tools/target/validate.js` `fold` strips NFD marks and does not map *æ* to *ae*. Checked in node: *får* and *far* fold together; *én* and *en* fold together; *øl* and *ol* do not; *mælk* and *maelk* do not. A fill distractor that folds together with the answer has to be `!strict`, or the validator rejects the item. That includes *får / far*, *går / gar* if both are offered, *én / en*, *ét / et*. It does not include *øl / ol* or *mælk / maelk*. Mark those *æ* and *ø* pairs `!strict` too when the letter is the point, so a future flag change cannot turn them into "almost".

**App changes, in order, if anyone builds them later. The course does not need them to ship.**

1. **Do not strip the ring from *å* for Danish**, the way *ñ* is protected. Without this, `accents: true` would grade *far* as "almost" for *får*. The course does not turn the flag on.
2. **Do not map *æ* to *ae* for Danish.** *ae* is not how Danes write *æ* in ordinary words. Without this, the same flag would accept *maelk*. The course keeps the flag off.
3. **Optional: do not fold *é* on *én / en* and *ét / et*.** Same reason. The course keeps the flag off, and writers mark those items `!strict` for the validator.

If none of the three is built, nothing in the course changes. `accents` stays false. The keys stay on the bar.

**Capitals.** The matcher ignores them. The course still teaches them: small letters for days, months, languages and nationalities; capital *I*, *De*, *Dem*, *Deres*, and names; no capital on nouns. A typed answer is never failed for case.

**Apostrophes and hyphens.** Danish A1 barely uses them. The matcher strips a hyphen typed as a space and an inner apostrophe left out, as "almost", for every course. Do not build a typed item on a hyphen.

**Punctuation.** The engine strips `. , ! ?` and quotes. Writers still write the comma before *at / fordi / hvis / når* in model answers (§2.5). A typed answer need not include it.

**What a typed answer must include.** The subject pronoun (*jeg bor*, not a bare *bor*). *en* or *et* when the English has "a" and Danish has an article. The definite ending when the English has "the" and Danish uses the ending. The *-r*. *ikke* in the right slot. The verb in second place when the prompt starts with a time or a place. *æ, ø, å, é* as spelled.

**What it need not include.** Capitals. Final punctuation. The comma. *tak* unless the prompt says "thanks" or "please". *De*. A stød, which cannot be typed.

## 11. Gaps and unverified claims

No native speaker has checked this profile. Check these first:

1. The vowel-length rule, short *i* as `eh` and short *o* as `aw`. It is the decision this profile is least sure of. *ikke* and *komme* come out right. *formiddag*, *osten* against *ost*, and *job* (`yohb`) show the rule failing. A native pass over §6.3 and the §9 cards will find more.
2. *københavn* stressed on the last syllable (`koe-buhn-HAWN`). The listing is a choice, not a page that was opened.
3. Every `LEX` line marked [inference] in §6.5, especially the numbers 50–90, the greetings, *havde* as `HA-dhuh` rather than the fast "ha-e", and *sig*.
4. *halv tre* = 2:30. It is how the course will teach the phrase. The sproget.dk clock page was not re-opened for this draft.
5. *du* as the only produced "you". Wiktionary labels *De* uncommon. A survey of who still says *De* was not opened.
6. The 2024 adverb *-t* rule. Not opened. The house style writes *dejligt* anyway.
7. US learner numbers. No enrollment page was opened. §5's "partner, Copenhagen, family history" is [inference].
8. DanskGrammatik's mistakes page, the source of most of §4, says itself that a native speaker has not checked it and that the audio is synthetic.

**Also unverified, and not used as facts:** the historical arithmetic behind *halvtreds*; exact café prices; whether *goddag* is the afternoon greeting or just a stiff all-day *goddag* (the app needs four clock strings, so *Goddag* is the afternoon slot; *hej* is what the lesson actually teaches).
