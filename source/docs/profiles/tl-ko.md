# Tagalog (Filipino) speakers learning Korean: pair profile

Course `tl-ko` is Korean for speakers of Tagalog (Filipino). It is a standard course, assembled from two parts:
- the fixed Korean half `tools/halves/target/ko/` (33 lessons, 641 cards, frozen v1; see `docs/HALVES-SPEC.md`);
- a native half `tools/halves/native/tl/ko/`, written in Filipino.

This profile is the brief the Filipino writers follow. It:
- reuses the structure and the shared Philippine research of `docs/profiles/ceb-ko.md` (the hand-tuned Cebuano course);
- decides the house rules for the native half;
- states precisely what is **different** for a Tagalog speaker;
- gives the reading guide for the romanisation in Filipino;
- lists, lesson by lesson, what must be rewritten rather than translated.

**Evidence tags** (as in `ceb-ko.md`):
- **[research-backed]**: an empirical study opened for this profile, with its sample named.
  - **(Filipino L1)** means the study's learners are described as Filipino or Tagalog speakers. Unlike in ceb-ko, that is **direct** evidence for this course, with the caveats given in each case.
- **[curriculum convention]**: what reference grammars, dictionaries, spelling guides, schools and courses do.
- **[practitioner opinion]**: a teacher's, journalist's, blog's or guide's stated view, with no data behind it.
- **[inference]**: this profile's own reasoning.
  - **Every Filipino sentence written in this profile is [inference]**: its author is not a native speaker.
  - §10 lists what a Tagalog-speaking reviewer must check first.
- **[carried]**: a claim taken from `ceb-ko.md` or `docs/profiles/en-tl.md` whose source was **not** re-opened for this profile. These claims are listed again in §11.

Untagged rules in §3 and §6 to §9 are design decisions, not findings.

**How much is known.** More than for Cebuano, but still little:
- **Writing.** One error analysis of written Korean by 16 UP Diliman students, who are "at least proficient in both Filipino and English" (Manrique 2022).
- **Speech.** One acoustic study of 14 Filipina marriage migrants in Korea (Jo, Kim & Kim 2008). It attributes their errors to **Tagalog** phonology by name.
- **Teacher view.** A UP Korean teacher says "Pronunciation is quite difficult. It has to be discussed every semester, even in advanced courses". He also says multilingual Filipino students "tend to be more sensitive to sounds" ([UP Linguistics news, de Chavez](https://linguistics.upd.edu.ph/news/de-chavez-on-why-korean-is-a-super-hard-language/)) [practitioner opinion].
- **Gaps.** Nothing found measures Tagalog speakers' Korean particles in speech, or their honorifics.

## 1. Summary

1. **Explanations:** everyday educated Manila Filipino in its natural **Taglish** teaching register, addressed with ***ikaw / mo***. Standard spelling, no accent marks. **No *po*** in prose, glosses or interface (§3).
2. **One house rule for terms:** grammar terms are **English**, from a closed list (§3.4), used as nouns inside Filipino sentences (*ang particle, mga ending*). Filipino school terms (*pangngalan, pandiwa*) are not used. The interface names are those of the new Tagalog pack: **Ngayon, Aralin, Pagsasanay, Review, Pag-unlad** (§3.5).
3. **What carries over from Cebuano unchanged:**
   - Philippine learners and EPS-TOPIK;
   - markers before the noun vs particles after it;
   - verb first vs verb last;
   - *sa* covering 에 and 에서 (research-backed, Filipino L1);
   - unaspirated stops;
   - two number systems;
   - *kuya / ate* vs 형 / 오빠 / 누나 / 언니;
   - *mas … kaysa*, *pinaka-*;
   - the 좋아하다 + 이/가 trap from *gusto* (research-backed, Filipino L1).
4. **What is different for Tagalog (§4, §5):**
   - ***po / ho / opo*** is a real politeness marker: a strong bridge to 요 / -습니다 that Cebuano lacks, but **not the same thing** (§4.8);
   - ***kay*** (to a person) vs ***sa*** (to a place) prefigures 에게 vs 에;
   - ***may / mayroon* vs *nasa***: Tagalog splits what 있어요 joins, while ***wala*** = 없어요 covers both;
   - *ito / iyan / iyon* = 이 / 그 / 저;
   - *din / rin* = 도, and the *d / r* switch is itself a "vowel or not" rule;
   - *lang* = 만;
   - **five vowels**: *e / i* are kept apart, so ㅔ / ㅣ is not the trap it is for Cebuano, but *o / u* stay acoustically close (research-backed, small samples) (§5.2);
   - the linker ***na / -ng***;
   - aspect (*kumain / kumakain / kakain*) vs Korean tense;
   - Spanish numbers for clock time (*alas-dos*), **and** both Spanish and native numbers for age (§4.9).
5. **Strongest traps, ranked (§5):**
   1. aspirated vs tense vs plain stops;
   2. ㅗ / ㅜ and ㅓ;
   3. particles after the noun, and the wrong particle;
   4. *sa* → 에 / 에서;
   5. "*po* = 요" misconceptions (요 left off with peers, put in the wrong place, or used as the only respect device);
   6. verb last;
   7. hours in native numbers;
   8. counters.
6. **Romanisation:** Revised Romanization. §6 gives a Filipino reading guide: one vowel for *eo, eu, ae*; no catch (glottal stop) and no stress; *j* like the *dy* of *dyip*; *k t p ch* with a puff; *kk tt pp* tight; final *l* never *r*.
7. **Localisation:** keep the "I'm from the US" glosses exact. The lead adds 필리핀에서 왔어요 / 마닐라에서 왔어요 to the built-in course before export (§9).
8. **Confidence:** moderate for sounds and particles (two Filipino-L1 studies), low for everything else. §10 has 20 review questions.

## 2. Learners: who, why, what first

### 2.1 Who learns Korean from Tagalog

- **Workers bound for Korea under the Employment Permit System (EPS).**
  - The EPS is "a government-to-government hiring program between the Philippines and Korea".
  - Its test is the "Employment Permit System-Test on Proficiency in Korean (EPS-Topik)".
  - In March 2025 the Department of Migrant Workers urged Korea-bound workers to train only in TESDA-accredited Korean courses, after reports of illegal training centres ([SunStar 2025](https://www.sunstar.com.ph/manila/korea-bound-ofws-urged-to-learn-korean-in-tesda-approved-institutions)) [practitioner opinion: press report of a government advisory].
  - The test's format and the number of takers were not found (§11).
  - Manufacturing-quota figures (13.98% of new workers in 2015) are [carried] from ceb-ko.
  - Most applicants from Luzon, and many from elsewhere, read Filipino, so this course is the default Korean course for Philippine EPS applicants who do not pick ceb-ko [inference].
- **School students.**
  - DepEd's Special Program in Foreign Language taught Korean in "69 public secondary schools across 13 regions", "benefiting 4,810 learners", with "168 teachers" trained, in SY 2024–2025. The partnership was renewed in March 2026 ([Manila Bulletin 2026](https://mb.com.ph/2026/03/04/deped-renews-partnership-with-south-korea-to-expand-korean-language-program-in-public-high-schools)).
  - The pilot began in ten Metro Manila public high schools, among them Makati High School and Las Piñas National High School, after a June 2017 agreement ([Inquirer 2018](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/1054028/deped-10-public-high-schools-in-metro-manila-to-teach-korean-language)).
  - These are Tagalog-region schools, so this course fits them directly [inference].
- **K-drama and K-pop fans.** Filipino Hallyu fans in a focus group mostly "rely on informal methods such as peer discussions, digital resources, and incidental exposure", and "most do not consider their skills sufficient for practical communication" ([Leon, Caugma & Dreisbach 2025](https://periodical.uthm.edu.my/index.php/dils/article/view/20069), abstract only; sample size not given) [research-backed (Filipino L1), abstract only].
- **Spouses and families in Korea.**
  - The one Filipino phonetics study sampled 14 Filipina marriage migrants (mean age 31.64) ([Jo, Kim & Kim 2008](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO200833338919695.pdf)).
  - The Filipino community in Korea was about 63,000 in 2015, and Busan churches hold "Tagalog masses" ([Wikipedia, *Filipinos in South Korea*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filipinos_in_South_Korea)).
- **University students** (UP Diliman and others), the population of Manrique (2022).

### 2.2 What they need first [inference]

1. **Reading Hangul and the romanisation correctly** from day one (§6).
2. **The 요 default and the respect system.** A Tagalog speaker already believes respect is grammatical, because of *po*. The job is to re-map that belief (§4.8), not to create it as for Cebuano.
3. **있어요 / 없어요, 주세요, numbers, counters and money**: the survival core.
4. **Particles placed after the noun.**
5. For EPS-bound learners, the workplace vocabulary EPS-TOPIK tests is **not** in this 33-lesson course (§9.4).

### 2.3 Bridge languages and the house rule for terms

**Facts.**
- Taglish is "code-switching and/or code-mixing in the use of Tagalog and English".
  - It began in Metro Manila and is now the de facto lingua franca of the "urbanized and/or educated middle class".
  - English verbs become Tagalog verbs with affixes (*magda-drive*, *i-explain*).
  - Prescriptivists discourage it in schools ([Wikipedia, *Taglish*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taglish)).
- English and Filipino are both media of instruction; technical subjects are taught in English textbooks [carried from ceb-ko §2.3]. Filipino class teaches Filipino grammar terms (*pangngalan, pandiwa, pang-uri*) [inference: common knowledge, not opened].
- Unlike in Cebu, Tagalog is not a sensitive bridge here: it **is** the learner's language. The comparison language that needs care is English: the learner reads it well, but the course must not explain Korean through English grammar.

**House rule (one rule, no exceptions):**
- **Prose is Filipino**, in the Taglish register of a good Manila teacher. Every explanation, `why`, `hint`, gloss, card meaning and note is a Filipino sentence.
- **Grammar terms are English, from the closed list in §3.4.** They are written in English spelling and used as nouns inside Filipino (*ang verb*, *sa particle*, *mga ending*).
  - They take Filipino markers and the linker (*ang polite na ending*).
  - They never take Filipino affixes (*i-particle* ✗, *naka-honorific* ✗).
- **Ordinary Taglish is allowed** where a Manila teacher would really use it: *okay, actually, so, pero, tapos*. The closed list governs **terms**, not every English word. Each lesson should still read as Filipino with English terms, not English with Filipino words [inference].
- **No Filipino grammar terms** (*pangngalan, pandiwa, panlapi, pang-angkop*), and no invented ones.

Why [inference]:
- Korean courses and Korean grammar books available in the Philippines are in English.
- The learner met *noun / verb* in English textbooks.
- The Filipino terms are school words that many adults have half forgotten.
- A closed list keeps 1,000+ rows consistent, as ceb-ko and the es-ko review found.

The only change from ceb-ko is that Taglish discourse words are allowed, because Taglish **is** the register of Manila teaching and of the app's own Tagalog interface. Cebuano's "no other English" rule protected a stricter Bisaya register.

## 3. Variety, register, spelling and terms of the Filipino text

### 3.1 Variety and register

- **Variety:** everyday educated Manila Filipino, as in `en-tl.md` §2.1 [carried].
  - Not the formal written register (*Ako ay…* in every sentence, purist coinages).
  - Not street slang or *jejemon*.
- **Register:** a friendly teacher talking to an adult.
  - Address the learner with ***ikaw / ka / mo / iyo*** (singular).
  - **No *po*, no *kayo*** for the learner. The app speaks to the learner as a friendly peer, as the Tagalog interface does (`tools/i18n/tl/NOTES.md` §1).
  - Commands use the plain command form (*Basahin, Sabihin, Tandaan, Pakinggan*), softened with *lang* or *muna* where a teacher would (*Basahin mo muna ito*).
- **Spelling:**
  - standard KWF spelling, **no accent marks**;
  - *ng* vs *nang* used correctly;
  - *d / r* by the preceding sound (*rin, rito, raw* after a vowel);
  - *kumusta*;
  - loans in the spelling Manila people write (*jeep, cellphone, CR*) ([carried from en-tl.md §2.2]);
  - no apostrophe contractions in explanations (*ano ang*, not *ano'ng*); they are allowed only inside quoted speech.
- ***Pilipinas*, *Pilipino / Filipino*, *Koreano / Koreana*** (person), *Korea*, *Seoul*, *Maynila* in prose, matching the Korean 마닐라.
- **The language name** in prose is ***Koreano*** (*Nag-aaral ako ng Koreano*), which is also what the interface shows. The course name in `course.json` is proposed as `"name": "Koreano"` [inference; question 2 in §10].

### 3.2 Glosses of Korean lines: one policy

- Glosses are natural, short Filipino for what the Korean line means in context.
- **No *po* in glosses, and no title added** (*Kuya, Ate, Sir, Ma'am*) that the Korean line does not contain.
  - This is the main departure from what a native would write. A Tagalog speaker would naturally gloss 감사합니다 as *Salamat po*.
  - The course does not, because *po* in every polite gloss would teach "요 = *po*", which is wrong in both directions (§4.8).
- **Politeness is carried only by the tag** the English half already has, and nothing else:
  - **(formal)** for -습니다;
  - **(polite)** for the 요-form;
  - **(casual)** for 반말;
  - **(honorific)** for -시- and the special verbs.
  - A gloss has a tag exactly where the English gloss has one.
- Korean lines that address the listener with -세요 or 주세요 are glossed with the Filipino command form plus *paki-* or *pakiusap* where the English has "please" (*Paupo kayo*? no: *Umupo ka, pakiusap*). The `polite` lesson explains that the real Korean line is polite **without** *po* [inference; the exact gloss style is question 6 in §10].
- Names, places and "the US" stay as they are in the English gloss (§9).

### 3.3 Ten sample sentences of explanation prose (all [inference], for the native check)

1. Sa Koreano, laging nasa dulo ng sentence ang verb.
2. Ang object marker na 을/를 ay dumidikit sa likod ng noun, hindi sa harap. Sa Tagalog, nauuna ang marker (*sa bahay*); sa Koreano, sumusunod ang particle (집에).
3. Kapag hindi ka sigurado, gamitin ang 요. Mas mabuti nang sobrang magalang kaysa bastos.
4. Hindi pareho ang 요 at ang *po*. Ang *po* ay salitang idinadagdag mo; ang 요 ay bahagi ng verb mismo.
5. Parang *ito, iyan, iyon*: ang 이 ay malapit sa akin, ang 그 ay malapit sa iyo, ang 저 ay malayo sa ating dalawa.
6. Ang 있어요 ay *may* at *nasa* nang sabay: may bagay, at nasa isang lugar.
7. Huwag ilagay ang 뭐 sa simula. Sa Tagalog, sinasabi nating *Ano ito?*, pero sa Koreano, 이거 뭐예요?
8. May malakas na hangin ang ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, ㅊ. Ilagay ang kamay mo sa harap ng bibig: dapat maramdaman mo ang hangin.
9. Ang 오빠 ay para lang sa babae na tumatawag sa mas matandang lalaki. Kung lalaki ka, 형 ang gamitin mo.
10. Ang galing! I-review ulit ang mga card na ito bukas para hindi mo makalimutan.

(The Hangul in these samples is illustrative. In real rows, the Hangul set must equal the English row's, §3.6.)

### 3.6 Constraints from the halves format that shape the writing

These are unchanged from `ceb-ko.md` §3.6:
- **Hangul set-equality** (spec §6). Every native string must contain exactly the English string's set of Hangul runs.

  - A lesson that needs extra Hangul carries `"_waive":["hangul"]` and a `_note` on its `lessons.jsonl` title row. That applies to the reading guide in `hangul` (it uses ㄹ and the tense letters) and to a localised example in `meet`.

- **Deck labels** are progress keys.
  - Translate them once, keep them unique, never "My phrases".

- **Glosses:**
  - dialogue glosses keep the single ` / `;
  - blanks `__` stay;
  - no braces in glosses;
  - `q p why hint` are at most 400 characters.
- **The `on…=` pattern.** In lesson bodies never write a space + `on` + letters + `=`. In Filipino this bites with *ong* or *onse*: write *onse: 11*.

## 4. What a Tagalog speaker already knows

Each item says where the bridge holds, where it misleads, and how the course uses it.
- Tagalog facts are from the opened [Wikiwand *Tagalog grammar*](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Tagalog_grammar) page, Kess (1981) and the number sources in §4.9, plus [carried] `en-tl.md`.
- Korean facts are from the English half.
- **Where Cebuano and Tagalog agree, the ceb-ko text is reused; the differences are marked "≠ ceb".**

### 4.1 Markers before the noun, particles after it

- **Tagalog** has three sets of markers ([Wikiwand, *Tagalog grammar*](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Tagalog_grammar)) [curriculum convention]:
  - common nouns take *ang* (direct), *ng* (indirect) and *sa* (oblique);
  - personal names take *si / ni / kay*, plural *sina / nina / kina*.
- **Holds:** the learner already thinks "a small word shows the noun's job".
- **Misleads, three ways** (as in ceb-ko) [inference]:
  1. **Position.** Tagalog markers come **before** the noun (*sa bahay*); Korean particles come **after** it (집에). The English half's list already has "에 집 ✗".
  2. ***ang* is not 이/가 or 은/는.**
     - Which noun gets *ang* depends on the verb's focus: *Kumain ako ng mangga* (the eater gets *ang*-form *ako*) vs *Kinain ko ang mangga* (the mango gets *ang*).
     - Korean marks the eaten thing with 을/를 either way.
     - So no "*ang* = X" rule is taught. The only comparison made is that 은/는 overlaps with *ang* in plain statements (*Guro si Pedro* / 피터 씨는 선생님이에요).
  3. **No personal markers in Korean.** Names take the same particles as things (민수 씨가, 민수 씨를).
- ***ng* is three things.** It marks the doer in a non-*ang* role, an indefinite object (*Bumili ako ng saging* ≈ 바나나를 사요), and an owner (*bahay ng nanay* ≈ 엄마의 집). Korean splits these into 이/가, 을/를 and 의. Only the object use is a bridge [inference].
- **≠ ceb: *kay* vs *sa* already split person from place.**
  - Tagalog says *sa bahay* (to the house) but *kay Mina* (to Mina).
  - Korean says 집에 but 민아 씨에게 / 한테.
  - So the learner already has the distinction that Korean particles make with 에 vs 에게 [inference; Cebuano *kang* does the same job, but ceb-ko did not use it]. 에게 is outside the 33 lessons except in passing; one sentence in `particles` is enough.

### 4.2 Choosing a form by "ends in a vowel or not": a stronger bridge than in Cebuano

- **Tagalog does this in three places** (≠ ceb, which has two):
  - the linker is *na* after a consonant other than n, and *-ng* after a vowel or n ([Wikiwand, *Tagalog grammar*](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Tagalog_grammar)): *magandang bahay*, *mabait na bata*;
  - *din / rin*, *daw / raw*, *dito / rito* switch to *r* after a vowel [curriculum convention, standard spelling];
  - *at* is contracted to *'t* after a vowel [inference].
- **Korean:** 은/는, 이/가, 을/를, 이에요/예요, 으로/로 and -을/-ㄹ all choose by the same test.
- **Use it** [inference]: open `nouns` with "Alam mo na ito: sa Tagalog, *maganda* + linker = *magandang*, at *ako rin* pero *siya din*. Ganyan din sa Koreano: tingnan kung nagtatapos sa vowel o sa consonant ang salita."
- **Trap:** the direction does not match. Tagalog's short form follows a vowel (*-ng*, *rin*); Korean's vowel form is sometimes the short one (는, 를) and sometimes not (가 after a vowel, 이 after a consonant). The writer must give the Korean pairs as they are, not "like Tagalog" one by one.

### 4.3 Verb-first Tagalog, verb-last Korean

- **Tagalog:** "the verb always remains in the initial position, the order of noun phrase complements that follows is flexible"; *ay*-inversion (topic first, then *ay*) exists, but is seen as formal or literary ([Wikiwand, *Tagalog grammar*](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Tagalog_grammar)) [curriculum convention].
- **Korean:** verb last (저는 밥을 먹어요).
- **Use it:** rewrite `sov` as "Tagalog: verb una; Koreano: verb sa dulo; English: sa gitna".
- ***ay* is a half-bridge** [inference]. *Ako ay estudyante* puts the topic first, like 저는 학생이에요. But *ay* is not 이에요 (en-tl.md: "ay is not 'is'" [carried]), and spoken Tagalog rarely uses it. Mention it once in `sov`, as recognition only.
- **Little words in second place.** *po, din / rin, lang, na, pa, ba* cluster after the first word in a fixed order ([Wikiwand](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Tagalog_grammar)). Korean particles stay glued to their noun. Expect learners to float 도 and 만 like *rin* and *lang* [inference].

### 4.4 *may / mayroon*, *nasa*, *wala* and the missing copula: 있어요 / 없어요 and 이에요 (≠ ceb)

Cebuano *naa* covers "be somewhere" and "have / exist". Tagalog splits them: *may / mayroon* for having and existing, and *nasa* for location. Only the negative *wala* covers both, as 없어요 does [curriculum convention for *may / mayroon / wala* ([Wikiwand](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Tagalog_grammar): *walâ* is "the opposite of may and mayroón ('there is')", e.g. *Walâ akóng pera*); the *nasa* row is [inference]].

| Tagalog | Korean | Job |
|---|---|---|
| *Estudyante ako.* (no verb) | 학생이에요 | A = B |
| *Nasa paaralan ako.* | 학교에 있어요 | being somewhere |
| *May pusa ako.* / *Mayroon akong pusa.* | 고양이가 있어요 | having |
| *May paaralan dito.* | 여기에 학교가 있어요 | existing |
| *Wala akong oras.* / *Walang oras.* | 시간이 없어요 | not having, not existing |
| *Wala siya sa bahay.* | 집에 없어요 | not being somewhere |
| *Hindi ako estudyante.* | 학생이 아니에요 | A ≠ B |

- **Holds:** "no verb for A = B" and *hindi* for A ≠ B; *wala* = 없어요 for every use.
- **Rewrite `isseoyo` around this table:** "Ang 있어요 ay *may* at *nasa* nang sabay; ang 없어요 ay *wala*."
- **Misleads:** *wala* also negates the completed aspect in some speech (*Wala pa siyang dumating*? no: the common form is *Hindi pa siya dumarating*). Do not teach a *wala* + verb trap for Tagalog: that was a Cebuano trap (*wala* + *mo-*) [inference; ≠ ceb]. The Tagalog-side trap is aspect (§4.11).

### 4.5 Three-way demonstratives

- *ito / iyan / iyon* and *dito / diyan / doon* are grouped by distance from the speaker and the addressee ([Wikiwand](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Tagalog_grammar)). They map one-to-one onto 이 / 그 / 저 and 여기 / 거기 / 저기 [inference].
- **Small trap:** Tagalog links the demonstrative with the linker or puts it after the noun (*itong bahay*, *ang bahay na ito*). Korean puts 이 / 그 / 저 directly before the noun with nothing between (이 집).

### 4.6 Negation

| Tagalog | Korean | Holds / misleads |
|---|---|---|
| *wala* (none) | 없어요 | holds |
| *hindi* + noun (*Hindi ako Amerikano*) | 아니에요 | holds |

### 4.8 *po / ho* and *opo*: the strongest new bridge, and why it is not 요 (≠ ceb)

**Facts** [practitioner opinion / descriptive essay, Kess 1981 ([*WPLC* 1(2)](https://journals.uvic.ca/index.php/WPLC/article/view/5039/1946))]:
- *Po* and *ho* are enclitics that "usually appear after the first full word or phrase in the sentence".
- *opo* / *oho* are the respectful "yes".
- They co-occur "in principle, though not necessarily in each instance" with the plural *kayo* used for one person.
- *po* is "usually considered more formal than *ho*, some speakers prefer one, some the other".
- Combinations with *ikaw* "are rare and are usually sociolinguistically marked" (prayer, sarcasm).
- The respect form can be inserted or omitted "without any noticeable grammatical or lexical change in the content of the sentence".

Cebuano has no *po* at all (ceb-ko §4.8 [carried]). This is the main structural difference between the two courses.

**Where *po* is a real bridge** [inference]:
1. **The idea that respect is obligatory and audible.** A Tagalog speaker already feels that talking to an older stranger without a respect marker is rude. The Korean 요 rule ("요 with everyone you are not close to") lands on prepared ground.
2. ***opo / oo* = 네 / 응.** The respectful vs plain "yes" pair maps closely: *Opo* ≈ 네 (or 예), *Oo* ≈ 응. Use it in `polite` and `texting`.
3. **Respect on fragments.** *Kape po* (ordering) and Korean 커피요 both put the respect marker on a bare noun phrase. Spoken Korean 요 attaches to fragments (저요, 커피요, 내일요?), much as *po* attaches to the first phrase. This is the most natural way to show what 요 "feels like".
4. ***Mano po*, *Tao po*** show that set phrases carry *po*, as 안녕하세요 and 감사합니다 carry 요 / -습니다.

**Where it breaks: tell the learner exactly these three things** [inference]:
1. **요 is part of the verb; *po* is an extra word.**
   - *Po* can be dropped and the sentence stays grammatical (Kess).
   - 가요 → 가 changes the verb form itself, and a Korean sentence with no ending is a different level, not "the same minus a word".
   - A 요-form sentence never puts 요 after the first word (저요 학생이에요 ✗); it goes at the end, on the verb (저는 학생이에요).
2. **요 is for "not close", *po* is for "older or higher".**
   - Tagalog drops *po* with a stranger of your own age or younger.
   - Korean keeps 요 with any adult you are not close to, including peers and people younger than you at work or in a shop.
   - Expect a Tagalog speaker to use 반말 with a same-age Korean classmate or a young shop assistant "because no *po* is needed": the commonest predicted error [inference].
3. **Korean has two more respect devices that *po* does not cover.**
   - **-습니다** is about the **situation** (announcements, the workplace, first meetings), not the listener's age. Tagalog marks that with vocabulary and *kayo*, not with a different *po*.
   - **-시-** honours the **person doing the action**, who may not be the listener (할머니가 가세요). *Po* only ever honours the listener. 저는 가세요 ✗ is the "*po* about oneself" error. Tagalog has no parallel, because *po* never attaches to the speaker's own action as a compliment.
- **And the pronoun.** The polite Tagalog "you" is *kayo* / *ninyo* / *inyo*. Korean has no polite "you" to switch to: it **drops** "you" or uses name + 씨 or a title. 당신 is not *kayo* (`pronouns-questions`).

**Use it:** `polite` opens with *po* as the bridge. Three lines:

> *Alam mo na ang* po *at* opo. *Ganyan din ang 요 sa Koreano, pero may tatlong pagkakaiba: (1) bahagi ito ng verb, hindi salitang idinadagdag; (2) ginagamit ito sa lahat ng hindi mo kaclose, kahit kaedad mo; (3) may dalawa pang paraan ng paggalang: -습니다 para sa pormal na sitwasyon, at -시- para sa taong gumagawa ng kilos.*

The glossary forbids "po-form" as a term (§3.4), and glosses never add *po* (§3.2).

### 4.9 Two number systems: a strong parallel with exact breaks

- **Tagalog:** Tagalog, English and Spanish-derived numbers "are often used interchangeably" ([learningtagalog, *Numbers in use*](https://learningtagalog.com/grammar/other_describing_words/expressing_quantity_or_distribution/numbers_in_use.html)) [practitioner opinion]. By use:
  - **Money:** "Numerals from Spanish are usually used in counting money" ([Wiktionary, *Appendix: Tagalog numbers*](https://en.wiktionary.com/wiki/Appendix:Tagalog_numbers)) [reference work]. learningtagalog lists *dalawampung piso, twenty pesos, bente pesos* side by side. Hundreds are native (*isang daan*), not *siyento* ([tagalog.com, *Spanish loan numbers 2*](https://tagalog.com/lessons/filipino-spanish-loan-numbers-2.php)).
  - **Clock time:** Spanish with *alas-* "more common" than native ordinals (Wiktionary, which hyphenates *alas-dos*). learningtagalog writes *alas tres* unhyphenated; tagalog.com writes *Alas-kuwatro*. The prose writes ***alas-dos*** (two of three sources, and en-tl.md's choice [carried]).
  - **Age: both.**
    - tagalog.com: Spanish numbers are used "when saying one's age" (*Kinse anyos, Beynte anyos, Beynte siyete anyos*).
    - learningtagalog gives native *tatlong taon* for age.
    - Wiktionary: "There are two ways of reckoning age", native *isang taon* and Spanish *uno anyos*, without saying which is preferred.
    - en-tl.md teaches native for age [carried]. The task brief that commissioned this profile names *bente anyos*.
    - **Resolution:** both are normal; the course names both (question 15 in §10).
  - **Counting things and durations:** native (*limang minuto, limang oras*) (learningtagalog).
- **Korean:** native numbers for counting things, hours and age; Sino-Korean for money, minutes, dates, phone numbers and floors (English `numbers` lesson).

| Use | Tagalog | Korean | Bridge |
|---|---|---|---|
| counting things | native (*dalawang saging*) | native (두 개) | **holds** |
| money | Spanish / native / English (*bente pesos*) | Sino-Korean (오만 원) | **holds** for the Spanish habit ("the borrowed system for money") |
| minutes | Spanish in clock times (*alas-tres y medya*, *alas-dos kinse*); native for durations (*limang minuto*) | Sino-Korean (삼십 분) for both | partly holds |
| **hours (clock)** | Spanish (*alas-tres*) | **native** (세 시) | **breaks**: 삼 시 ✗ |
| **age** | Spanish (*bente anyos*) **or** native (*dalawampung taon*) | **native** (스무 살) | **breaks for *anyos*, holds for *taon*** |
| large numbers | native hundreds and thousands (*libo*); groups of 1,000 | Sino-Korean; groups of 10,000 (만) | holds for the system, breaks for grouping |

- **Use it** [inference]: `numbers` opens with "Alam mo nang gumamit ng dalawang klase ng numero". Then come the two breaks in bold:
  - "Sa oras: *alas-tres* = 세 시, numerong Koreano, hindi 삼 시."
  - "Sa edad: kung *bente anyos* ang sinasabi mo, mag-ingat: 스무 살, hindi 이십 살. Kung *dalawampung taon*, tama ang kutob mo."
- **Number + linker vs counters.** *Dalawang tasa ng kape* is number + linker + measure word + *ng* + noun. 커피 두 잔 is noun + number + counter. *Ilan?* / *Ilang piraso?* ≈ 몇 개? [inference].

### 4.10 Other bridges (all [inference] unless tagged)

- **Verbs do not change for the person**, in Tagalog either: *Kumain ako / ka / siya*. Korean 먹어요 is the same for everyone.
- **Describing words are predicates** without "is": *Mainit ang kape* / 커피가 뜨거워요.
- **Noun + "do" verbs:** *mag-* + noun or English loan (*magtrabaho*, *mag-shopping*, *magda-drive* ([Wikipedia, *Taglish*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taglish))) ≈ 하다 verbs (공부하다, 쇼핑하다).
- **The linker and noun modifiers:** *magandang bahay* and 크-ㄴ 가방 both glue a sound onto the describing word before the noun.
  - **Trap:** Tagalog also puts the description **after** the noun with the linker (*bahay na maganda*, *ang pagkaing gusto ko*).
  - Korean never does (제가 좋아하는 음식).
  - Tagalog relative clauses are just linker + clause, so "walang *na* pagkatapos ng noun" is the rule to give.
- **Comparison:** *mas* ≈ 더; *kaysa (sa)* ≈ 보다 (but *kaysa* comes **before** the noun and 보다 **after** it); *pinaka-* ≈ 제일 (*pinakamasarap* / 제일 맛있어요). *Pinaka-* is confirmed as the superlative prefix ([Wikiwand](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Tagalog_grammar)); *kaysa* is [carried] from ceb-ko and en-tl.md.
- **"Let's":** *Tara!* / *Kain tayo!* ≈ casual -자 (먹자, 가자). *Ano kaya kung…?* ≈ -(으)ㄹ까요?. *Gusto mo bang…?* ≈ -(으)ㄹ래요? (`plans`).
- **Can / can't:** *maka-* / *kaya* ≈ -(으)ㄹ 수 있어요; *hindi maka-* ≈ 못 / -(으)ㄹ 수 없어요 (`cant`).
- **Must / may:** *kailangan* (before the verb) ≈ -아/어야 해요 (after the stem). *Puwede ba akong…?* ≈ -아/어도 돼요? (`have-to`, `requests`).
- **Pronouns:**
  - *siya* is he and she, and Korean also avoids a gendered "he / she".
  - *kami / tayo* (exclusive / inclusive) both become 우리.
  - 우리 also means "my" for family (우리 엄마), close to Tagalog *aming / ating*.
- **Loanwords:** Philippine English replaces /f/ and /v/ with [p] and [b] [carried from ceb-ko], so 커피 *keopi* sounds natural. Spelling loans in Hangul is the hard part (Manrique: 드레시 for 드레스, 디지탈 for 디지털).

### 4.11 Aspect, not tense: *kumain / kumakain / kakain* (≠ ceb)

- **Tagalog:** *kumain* (completed), *kumakain* (progressive), *kakain* (contemplative, "will eat") ([Wikiwand](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Tagalog_grammar)) [curriculum convention]. en-tl.md names them "completed / ongoing / future (contemplated)" [carried].
- **Korean:** past 먹었어요 / present 먹어요 / future 먹을 거예요.
- **Holds in the plain cases:**
  - *Kumain ako* ≈ 먹었어요;
  - *Kumakain ako* ≈ 먹어요 (or 먹고 있어요, outside this course);
  - *Kakain ako mamaya* ≈ 먹을 거예요.
  The rough match is useful for `past` and `future`.
- **Misleads in three places** [inference]:
  1. ***Hindi pa ako kumakain*** (not yet + ongoing form) = 아직 안 먹었어요 (**past**). A learner who maps form to form says 아직 안 먹어요 ✗.
  2. ***Kakain na ako*** ("I'm about to eat") uses the contemplated form with *na*. Korean uses the present or 먹을게요, not a plain future.
  3. **Tagalog completed ≠ "past only".** *Kakakain ko lang* ("I just ate") is a recent-perfective form. Korean simply uses 먹었어요 (plus 방금 if needed).
- **Use it:** in `past`, say "Ang Koreano ay may tense: kailan nangyari. Ang Tagalog ay may aspect: tapos na ba o hindi pa." Then show the *hindi pa* trap with the English half's own 안 갔어요 example. The English body has no 아직, so it gets no new Hangul without a waiver.

## 5. Error zones of Tagalog speakers in Korean, ranked

**How the list is ranked** [inference]: (1) items with Filipino-learner data; (2) items in nearly every sentence; (3) lesson-local traps. Studies cited here **are** about Filipino / Tagalog-speaking learners. Their limits are stated each time.

### 5.1 Aspirated vs tense vs plain stops

- **Wrong → right:**
  - 탈 *tal* said like 딸 *ttal*;
  - 커피 with no puff;
  - 불 / 뿔 / 풀 not told apart.
- [research-backed (Filipino L1)] Jo, Kim & Kim (2008): 14 Filipina marriage migrants vs 10 Korean women ([PDF](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO200833338919695.pdf)).
  - **Aspirated stops: the Filipino VOT was much shorter than the Korean** (ms): ㅍ 37.55 vs 58.89; ㅌ 26.88 vs 58.45; ㅋ 34.90 vs 55.83; ㅊ 48.59 vs 82.28.
  - **Tense stops: the Filipino VOT was longer** (ms): ㅃ 47.50 vs 17.51; ㄸ 30.52 vs 10.47; ㄲ 38.03 vs 19.41; ㅉ 42.85 vs 28.26.
  - **ㅌ errors:** 9 of 14 produced a tense ㄸ and 3 a plain ㄷ. The paper reports "92.9%", although 12 of 14 is 85.7%.
  - The authors attribute the pattern to Tagalog consonants lacking aspiration. The paper gives the consonant count as 16 in one section and 15 in another.
  - This fetch labelled each value with its group, and the direction agrees with the reading ceb-ko §5.1 settled on.
  - **Safe teaching claim:** "walang buga ng hangin ang k, t, p mo; dalawang magkaibang bagay ang kailangan ng Koreano: malakas na buga para sa ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅊ, at mariing walang-hangin para sa ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ".
- [carried] The five-L1 arXiv study and Yoo & Kang (2018), cited in ceb-ko, found three-way stop errors in every group studied. They have no Filipino learners and were not re-opened.
- **Design consequence:** as ceb-ko. Two instructions, no sound equivalence, minimal-pair cards ko.c0045 to ko.c0048 with Filipino notes.

### 5.2 Vowels: ㅗ / ㅜ close, ㅓ too open; ㅔ / ㅣ not a trap (≠ ceb)

- **Wrong → right:**
  - 소 *so* / 수 *su* said alike;
  - 서울 *Seoul* read "se-ul" or "so-ul";
  - 어 said like 아 or 오;
  - 그 *geu* read "ge-u".
- **Tagalog has five vowel phonemes**, /i u ɛ ɔ a/ ([Wikipedia, *Tagalog phonology*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagalog_phonology)) [curriculum convention].
  - The same page says [e] and [ɛ] "were formerly an allophone of /i/".
  - A 2025 thesis: "Originally, Tagalog had three vowel phonemes – /a/, /i/, and /u/, until /e/ and /o/ were introduced" ([Manaog 2025, BA thesis, University of Amsterdam](https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/archive/2025/2025-BA-IndyManaog.pdf)) [curriculum convention as cited there].
  - Cebuano treats *e / i* and *o / u* as largely free variants (ceb-ko §5.2 [carried]).
  - **So the Cebuano argument (*kumusta / komusta*) does not carry over as it stands.**
- ***e / i* are kept apart.** Manaog cites Delos Reyes et al. (2009) for "a noticeable acoustic contrast between [e] and [i]". Jo et al. (2008) found **no** significant Filipino-vs-Korean difference for ㅔ. ㅔ / ㅣ (게 / 기) is therefore **not** ranked as a trap for Tagalog speakers [research-backed (Filipino L1) for ㅔ; inference for the ranking].
- ***o / u* remain close** [research-backed (Filipino L1), small samples]:
  - Manaog (6 Metro Manila bilinguals) reports that "[o] and [u] were not far apart in terms of frequency values". She hedges that a merger "is not yet certain".
  - Jo et al. found the Filipino ㅗ significantly different from the Korean on **both formants**: F1 378 vs 480 Hz, F2 1,101 vs 1,346. A lower F1 is a **higher**, more ㅜ-like vowel [inference from the acoustics].
  - Both point the same way. **ㅗ / ㅜ is the top vowel trap** for Tagalog speakers too, for a different reason than in Cebuano: not spelling variation, but a narrow phonetic gap.
- **ㅓ:** the Filipino ㅓ had a higher F1 than the Korean (711 vs 564 Hz), meaning more open and closer to ㅏ (Jo et al.). Its F2 was not reported as different. ㅏ differed on F2 (1,523 vs 1,782). Guide: "ㅓ is not *a*" [research-backed (Filipino L1)].
- **ㅡ, ㅜ, ㅔ** showed no significant difference in Jo et al. ㅡ has no Tagalog counterpart [inference], but it ranks low.
- **ㅐ / ㅔ** sound the same in modern Seoul speech (cards ko.c0056, ko.c0007). Jo et al. report a significant ㅐ F1 difference, but their text and appendix give different Filipino values (534 vs 452), so it is not used.

### 5.3 Particles after the noun, and the wrong particle

- **Wrong → right:** 에 집 → 집에; 커피가 좋아해요 → 커피를 좋아해요; 시간가 있어요 → 시간이 있어요; 저은 → 저는.
- [research-backed (Filipino L1)] Manrique (2022) ([PDF](https://linguistics.upd.edu.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/14-Error-Analysis-of-Written-Compositions-of-Filipino-Learners-of-Korean-as-a-Foreign-Language.pdf)):
  - **Sample:** 16 students, 38 documents, 644 sentences. 383 sentences (59.47%) had at least one error, and there were 809 errors in all.
  - **By type:** selection 491, omission 156, addition 138, ordering 24.
  - "만화가 너무 좋아해요" should take 를. The paper links this to Filipino *gusto*, where the liked thing "typically take[s] the phrase marker ang", and says the correct Korean marking mirrors English.
  - 이/가 vs 은/는 confusion "persist[s]" across levels.
  - **Limits:** written work only; learners "at least proficient in both Filipino and English"; levels assigned by course hours; per-level totals inconsistent between two tables (457 vs 454).
- [inference] Particle position (before vs after) is not in the study. It is inferred from §4.1.

### 5.4 에 vs 에서: one *sa* for both

- **Wrong → right:** 카페에 공부해요 → 카페에서 공부해요; 집에서 있어요 → 집에 있어요; 장례식장에 해요 → 장례식장에서 해요 (Manrique's example).
- [research-backed (Filipino L1)] Manrique (2022): the particles are confused partly because "In Filipino, these are all expressed by locative phrase marker sa".
- **Design consequence:** rewrite `particles` around *sa*: "Isang *sa* sa Tagalog, dalawa sa Koreano: 에 kapag pupunta ka o nandoon ka; 에서 kapag may ginagawa ka roon o galing ka roon." Add one line on *kay* → 에게 (§4.1).

### 5.5 "*po* = 요" errors (≠ ceb)

- **Wrong → right** [inference: predicted from §4.8, not measured]:
  - 반말 to a same-age stranger or a young shop assistant ("no *po* needed") → 요-form;
  - 요 placed after the first word (저요 학생이에요) → 저는 학생이에요;
  - 저는 가세요 → 저는 가요 (honorific on oneself, as if -시- were *po*);
  - 당신 for the polite "you", from *kayo* → name + 씨 or a title;
  - -습니다 avoided with an older but friendly boss "because *po* is enough" → workplace -습니다 in set phrases.
- [research-backed (Filipino L1)] Manrique (2022) reports register errors in writing: polite 저 in plain-style writing, spoken forms in formal writing, and -니까 for -므로. These are not *po* errors as such, but they show register is a live error area for Filipino learners.
- Ranked above word order because it occurs in every spoken sentence and the *po* belief makes it feel solved.

### 5.6 Word order: verb last, question words in place

- **Wrong → right:**
  - 먹어요 밥을 → 밥을 먹어요;
  - 뭐 이거예요? → 이거 뭐예요? (from *Ano ito?*);
  - 어디 화장실이에요? → 화장실 어디예요? (from *Saan ang CR?*).
- [research-backed (Filipino L1)] Ordering errors were only 24 of 809 in Manrique (2022), in written work.
- [inference] Low in writing, but the question-word habit is strong: Tagalog fronts every question word (*Ano, Saan, Sino, Kailan, Bakit, Ilan, Magkano*).

### 5.7 Hours (and *anyos* ages) in native numbers; 만

- **Wrong → right:** 삼 시 → 세 시 (from *alas-tres*); 이십 살 → 스무 살 (from *bente anyos*); 오십천 원 → 오만 원.
- [inference] from §4.9. The age error applies only to speakers who say *anyos*; *dalawampung taon* speakers are helped by their habit.

### 5.8 Counters and their order

- **Wrong → right:** 두 커피 → 커피 두 잔 (from *dalawang kape*); 세 개 사람 → 사람 세 명.
- [inference] Tagalog puts the number + linker first.

### 5.9 Final consonants, ㄹ, linking without a glottal stop

- **Wrong → right:** 물 *mul* said "mur" or with a dark *l*; 한국어 said with a glottal stop before 어 → *hangugeo*; final 받침 released (밥 "ba-pə").
- [research-backed (Filipino L1)] Jo et al. (2008): Filipino speakers sometimes replaced onset /l/ with /r/ and distorted final /l/ toward a dark [ɫ]. No percentages are given.
- [curriculum convention] Tagalog stress is distinctive (*baba* 'father' / *babá* 'piggyback' / *babà* 'chin' / *babâ* 'go down'). The glottal stop is "normally unwritten at the end of a word" and occurs between vowels (*Taal*). Final stops are "unreleased or nasally released phrase-finally", which helps with Korean finals ([Wikipedia, *Tagalog phonology*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagalog_phonology)).
- [inference] A Tagalog reader may insert a catch before a Korean vowel-initial syllable, which blocks linking (연음), and may read RR hyphens (*Kii-yeyo*) as a catch.
- **≠ ceb, a small help:** Tagalog final stops are often unreleased, so 밥 is less likely to get a puff than an English speaker's "bap" [inference from the Wikipedia note].

### 5.10 Spelling loanwords and spacing (writing)

- [research-backed (Filipino L1)] Manrique (2022) reports:
  - missing spaces (살고싶어요 → 살고 싶어요; 3년동안 → 3년 동안);
  - extra spaces (돌아 오지 → 돌아오지);
  - loanword misspellings (드레시 → 드레스; 디지탈 → 디지털).
- Low priority for this course.

### 5.11 Stress

- [curriculum convention] "Stress is a distinctive feature in Tagalog" ([Wikipedia, *Tagalog phonology*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagalog_phonology)).
- [inference] Seoul Korean has no word stress, so the guide says "pare-pareho ang diin ng lahat ng syllable".

## 6. Pronunciation aid: how a Tagalog reader reads the romanisation

**What the Korean half prints.**
- Every Korean line carries Revised Romanization (RR) in `rom`, shared by every course and not editable (spec §5).
- RR spells pronunciation: the English `sound-changes` lesson says "The romanization in this app is written the way words sound". So 감사합니다 appears as *gamsahamnida*.

**Where a Tagalog reader goes wrong** [inference]:
1. **Vowel pairs.** Reads **eo, eu, ae, oe, ui** as two vowels, possibly with a catch between them. Tagalog vowel pairs are two syllables (*maaga* = ma-a-ga; *Taal* with a catch).
2. ***o* and *u*.** Treats them as close (§5.2). *e* and *i* are fine.
3. **Stops.** Reads *k t p* as his own unaspirated stops (§5.1), and doubled *kk tt pp* as long.
4. ***j*.** Reads it as the *h* of Spanish names (*Jose*), or as English *j*. Filipino spelling writes the Korean sound *dy* (*dyip*). en-tl.md teaches *jeep / dyip* [carried].
5. **Catch.** Adds a glottal stop before vowel-initial syllables, and reads RR hyphens as one.
6. **Final *l*, stress.** Says final ***l*** as *r*, and stresses one syllable.

Some RR sequences **are** two syllables (*kapeeseo* = 카페에서, ka-pe-e-seo). The guide names the single-vowel digraphs and says every other pair is two syllables.

**Placement.** As in ceb-ko §6:
- the full guide goes in the `hangul` body (waiver needed);
- `sound-changes` repeats the parts on linking, finals and "no catch";
- the three Hangul deck descriptions repeat their parts;
- card notes for single jamo (ko.c0001 to ko.c0060) use the same comparisons.

### 6.1 The reading guide, in Filipino (ready to paste; every line [inference], native check §10)

> **Paano basahin ang romanization**
>
> Ang letrang Latin sa ilalim ng bawat linyang Koreano ay ang romanization. Ginawa ito para sa mga nagbabasa ng English, pero mababasa mo ito na parang Tagalog, maliban sa mga letrang ito.
>
> **Mga vowel**
> - **a, i, e**: gaya sa Tagalog. Ang **e** (ㅔ) at **ae** (ㅐ) ay iisa ang tunog, gaya ng e sa *kape*.
> - **o** (ㅗ): bilugan nang husto ang labi at ibaba nang kaunti ang panga. **u** (ㅜ): bilog din ang labi pero mas sarado ang bibig. Magkaiba ang *so* at *su* sa Koreano: magkaibang salita. Huwag silang pagpalitin.
> - **eo** (ㅓ): **isang vowel lang**, hindi e-o. Buksan ang bibig na parang o, pero huwag bilugan ang labi. Hindi ito *a*. *Seoul* = "seo-ul", dalawang syllable, hindi "se-ul" at hindi "so-ul".
> - **eu** (ㅡ): **isang vowel lang**, hindi e-u. Sabihin ang u habang nakangiti, patag ang labi.
> - **ui** (ㅢ): eu at i nang mabilis, isang syllable.
> - **oe** (ㅚ): parang "we".
> - **ya, yeo, yo, yu, ye, wa, wo, wi, wae, we**: isang syllable bawat isa.
> - Ang ibang dalawang magkasunod na vowel ay dalawang syllable: *kapeeseo* = ka-pe-e-seo.
>
> **Mga consonant**
> - **k, t, p, ch** (ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅊ): ibuga ang hangin. Ilagay ang kamay sa harap ng bibig: dapat maramdaman mo ang hangin. Walang hangin ang *k, t, p* ng Tagalog, kaya kailangan mong dagdagan.
> - **kk, tt, pp, ss, jj** (ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ): **hindi mahaba** ang tunog. Mariin ang lalamunan, walang hangin, mas mariin pa kaysa sa *k, t, p* ng Tagalog.
> - **g, d, b, j** sa simula ng salita (ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅈ): malambot, kaunting hangin lang, nasa pagitan ng g at k, ng d at t, ng b at p. Sa pagitan ng dalawang vowel, tunay na g, d, b, j.
> - **j**: parang *dy* sa *dyip*. Hindi parang j sa *Jose*.
> - **ch**: parang *ts* sa *tsinelas*, pero may hangin.
> - **s**: gaya sa Tagalog; bago ang i, puwedeng "sh": *si* = "shi".
> - **h**: laging binibigkas.
> - **r** sa pagitan ng dalawang vowel (ㄹ): parang r ng Tagalog. Sa dulo ng syllable, **l**, hindi r: *mul* (tubig), hindi "mur". **ll** = mahabang l.
> - **ng**: parang ng sa *ngayon*, pero sa Koreano, sa dulo lang ng syllable ito lumalabas.
> - **f, v, z**: wala sa Koreano. Ang *coffee* ay *keopi* sa Koreano.
>
> **Sa dulo ng syllable**
> - **k, t, p** sa dulo: isara ang bibig o ang dila, pero **huwag ibuga ang hangin**. *bap* (kanin): isara ang labi sa p, at tumigil. Ganito rin ang ginagawa mo sa dulo ng *sulat* o *anak*.
> - Pito lang ang tunog na puwede sa dulo: k, n, t, l, m, p, ng.
>
> **Tatlong tuntuning iba sa Tagalog**
> 1. **Walang impit (glottal stop).** Lumilipat ang final consonant sa susunod na vowel: *hangugeo* = han-gu-geo, hindi "han-guk-'eo". Hindi impit ang gitling sa romanization (*Kii-yeyo*).
> 2. **Walang diin.** Pare-pareho ang diin ng lahat ng syllable. Iba ito sa Tagalog, kung saan nagbabago ang kahulugan ng *baba* depende sa diin.
> 3. **Tunog ang isinusulat ng romanization**, hindi ang Hangul: ang 감사합니다 ay *gamsahamnida*. Kapag magkaiba ang Hangul at ang romanization, sundin ang romanization sa pagsasalita at ang Hangul sa pagsusulat.

- 영어 할 수 있어요? (ko.c0091, ko.g014) and 혹시 영어 메뉴 있어요? (ko.s0280, ko.p040, requests.02) are especially useful to Filipinos: keep them and gloss them as they are.

## 10. Review plan: 20 questions for a Tagalog speaker

Ask for "OK", or for the natural version.

1. Why are you learning Korean: EPS work, school (SPFL), K-drama / K-pop, family, or something else?
2. Do you call the language *Koreano*, *Korean*, or *wikang Koreano*?
3. Read the ten sample sentences in §3.3. Are they natural for a Manila teacher? Too much English, or too little?
4. Are English grammar terms inside Filipino (*ang verb*, *ang particle*, *ang object marker*) easier than *pandiwa, pangngalan*?
5. §4.8: is "the 요 is part of the verb, *po* is a word you add" clear? Is the three-difference box in `polite` right?
6. §3.2: glosses without *po* (*Salamat* for 감사합니다 (formal)). Acceptable, or does it feel rude enough to confuse?
7. §4.8: do you drop *po* with a stranger your own age? With a young shop assistant?
8. §6.1 vowels: can you hear *so* vs *su*? Do *o* and *u* sound close in your own speech (*kumusta*, *puno*)?
9. §6.1: say *Seoul* and *geu*. Did you say two vowels before reading the guide?
10. §6.1 consonants: is *ibuga ang hangin* the right phrase for a puff? Is *mariin, walang hangin* clear for ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ?
11. §6.1: is *j* like the *dy* of *dyip*, and *ch* like the *ts* of *tsinelas*, right for your speech?
12. §6.1: are "Walang impit" and "Walang diin" clear?
13. §4.4 table: are *Nasa paaralan ako*, *May pusa ako*, *May paaralan dito*, *Wala akong oras*, *Wala siya sa bahay* all natural?
14. §9.1: for 미국에서 왔어요, which is natural: *Taga-Amerika ako* or *Galing ako sa Amerika*?
15. §4.9: how do you say your age, *bente singko anyos* or *dalawampu't limang taon*? And the hour, *alas-tres* or *three o'clock*?
16. §4.11: is *Hindi pa ako kumakain* the natural "I haven't eaten yet"?
17. §4.1: does "*kay* is for people, *sa* for places, like 에게 and 에" make sense?
18. §8.2 cards 7 and 9: *Kape lang ang iniinom ko*, *Hindi ako pumunta kasi busy ako*: natural?
19. §7 lesson 32: is *baka* or *sakali* a fair match for 혹시 ("by any chance")?
20. §7 lesson 26: are *una, tapos, pagkatapos, kaya, pero, sa huli* natural story connectors?

## 11. Gaps and unverified claims

**No native check yet.** Every Filipino sentence, gloss, word choice and spelling in §3.3, §4, §6.1, §7, §8 and §9 is [inference] by a non-speaker.

**Evidence limits (sources opened)**
- **Manrique 2022:**
  - 16 students, written work only;
  - L1 not stated as one label ("at least proficient in both Filipino and English");
  - levels assigned by course hours;
  - per-level error totals inconsistent between Tables 3 and 4 (457 vs 454);
  - EDI given as 1.25 and 1.26.
- **Jo, Kim & Kim 2008:**
  - 14 Filipina marriage migrants;
  - reports 92.9% where its counts give 85.7%;
  - gives the Tagalog consonant count as both 16 and 15;
  - its ㅐ F1 differs between text and appendix;
  - its significance levels differ between text (p<0.05) and appendix (p<0.01).
  - Only the directions are used with confidence. The ㅗ-toward-ㅜ reading of its formants is this profile's inference.
- **Manaog 2025:** a BA thesis with 6 speakers, 4 of them women. The author herself hedges the *o / u* merger. Delos Reyes et al. (2009), the source of the *e / i* contrast, was cited there and not opened.
- **Kess 1981:** a descriptive essay without speaker data.
- **Leon et al. 2025:** abstract only; sample size unknown.
- **Number usage:** the three sources disagree on age (native vs Spanish) and on hyphenating *alas-*. The course states both age forms (§4.9).

**Claims carried, not re-opened** [carried]:
- From ceb-ko: the EPS manufacturing quota figures (Rappler 2015), DFA 2021 arrivals, English as medium of instruction (Wikipedia *Philippine English*), Philippine English /f/ → [p], the Yoo & Kang 2018 and arXiv 2023 findings, and the RR summary (Wikipedia *Revised Romanization*).
- From en-tl.md: Manila register, loan spelling (*jeep, CR*), *ay* is not "is", the aspect terms, *hindi / wala / huwag*, *kaysa*, and the *kuya / ate / sir / ma'am* usage.

**Pages that could not be opened**

- en.wikipedia *Tagalog language* (blocked by the fetch allowlist). The three-vowel history is cited from Manaog 2025 and the Wikipedia phonology page instead.

**Not found:**
- any study of Tagalog speakers' spoken Korean particles or honorifics;
