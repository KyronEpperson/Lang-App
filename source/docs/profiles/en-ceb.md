# English speakers learning Cebuano: course profile

Course `en-ceb`: Cebuano (Bisaya) for English speakers. Every explanation, prompt, `why`, `hint`, meaning and card note is in English. Answers, examples and card targets are in Cebuano. Machine-readable twin: `tools/target/ceb/profile.json`, generated from the same data as §6 to §9, so the two files agree. Pronunciation generator: `tools/target/ceb/respell.js` (§6).

(The roadmap used is the `ceb` entry of `packs/roadmaps.js`, merged from `docs/roadmaps/ceb.json`, whose `basis`, `src`, `note`, `hardest`, `voice` and `script` fields were read first. The Tagalog profile `docs/profiles/en-tl.md` did not exist when this profile was written, so the two were not aligned.)

**Evidence tags.**
- **[research-backed]**: an empirical study opened for this profile. The study is named and its sample given. **(abstract only)** says only the abstract was read.
- **[curriculum convention]**: what syllabuses, textbooks, dictionaries, spelling guides and courses do.
- **[practitioner opinion]**: a teacher's, school's, blog's or guide's stated view, with no data behind it.
- **[inference]**: this profile's own reasoning, **including the author's own knowledge of Cebuano**, which is weaker than for the major languages (see §6 for a measured example). Every claim of that kind that a writer will copy into the course is listed for a native check in §11.

Untagged rules in §6 to §10 are design decisions, not findings.

## 1. Summary

- **Variant and voice:** everyday Cebuano of Cebu City, polite register, no Tagalog and no *po*; `ceb-PH`, for which no device or Azure voice was found, so the `pron` line carries pronunciation (§2.6).
- **Spelling:** one house spelling per word, after KWF 2022 / *Bisaya* rules (*u* early, *o* in the last syllable; *i* except *babaye*-type words; *mo- / ni-*; ***og* for the marker, *ug* for "and"**, a change from the roadmap); alternates (*mu-, ug, gyod, jud*) as "almost" through a requested fold (§2.2, §10).
- **Biggest traps (§4):** the pronoun sets and markers (*ako / ko / nako / akong / kanako*, *si / ang / ni / sa / og*); the main word first with short words in second place (*Gigutom ko*); verb focus and future / past forms; *dili / wala*; unwritten stress and catch; the linker *nga*.
- **Standing rule (§7):** every set re-tests **the right set and marker** (from `mo` on: who gets *ang*) and **the main word first, short words in second place**; keyword regex tested on 56 lines.
- **Pronunciation aid (§6):** English-letter respelling generated by `respell.js`, required everywhere and visible all course: `BAH-tahq`, `bah-LAI`, `mah-ah-yohng boon-tahg`. Stress and final catch come only from a list (`LEX`, 172 forms) of forms with IPA or written marks in a source, shown in CAPITALS and `q`; every other word (and every derived form) is written all lowercase, with no stress claim, and warns, because the author's own recall got 2 of 7 non-numeral words right.
- **Lessons (§8):** 28: all 26 roadmap items in order (renamed for clarity) plus `feelings` and `requests`.
- **Decks (§9):** 18 in 5 groups, including "Traps for English speakers" and "Short forms and Cebu talk".
- **Typing (§10):** Latin, no accents, no script stage; first engine need: `lib.js` accepts only two-letter course codes, so `ceb` cannot build until that changes (§10.1).

## 2. Variant, register and voice

### 2.1 Variant: everyday Cebuano of Cebu City

- The roadmap names "Cebuano of Cebu (Cebu City), everyday modern spelling", basis Bunye and Yap, *Cebuano for Beginners* (1971), and the build plan keeps it [curriculum convention]. This profile keeps it.
- **Target register:** the polite everyday speech of Metro Cebu: shops, jeepneys, carinderias (small eateries), offices, family meals. It is not the "deep" literary Bisaya of poetry and the Bible (*lalom nga Binisaya*), and not street slang or reversed words (*yapok* for *kapoy*, "tired"; [practitioner opinion: Chiongbian-Khu, *Loserdom*](https://loserdom.substack.com/p/learning-bisaya)).
- **No spoken standard exists** [practitioner opinion]: the Binisaya.com article on Cebuano dialectology says "there is yet no standard for spoken Cebuano"; the written de facto standard is the southern Cebu (Sialo) usage of the Church and the press ([Binisaya.com](https://binisaya.com/node/372/1290)). Cebu City speech is the most widely heard form in media and the Visayas–Mindanao cities [inference].
- **Metro Cebu shortenings** [curriculum convention: Wiktionary lists *balay* as /baˈlaj/ in standard Cebuano and /ˈbaj/ in "Metro Cebu, Bohol, Leyte" ([Wiktionary: balay](https://en.wiktionary.com/wiki/balay))]. The course teaches the full forms (*balay*, *wala*, *dili*) in answers and gives the short spoken forms in notes and in the deck "Short forms and Cebu talk" (§2.4).
- **English mixing** [practitioner opinion]: Cebu City speech mixes in a lot of English (Chiongbian-Khu; Whitmore, a US missionary, contrasts *Pwede ba ka mobalik sa sunod?* with *Okay ra ba if mobalik lang ka sa next time?* ([Medium](https://medium.com/@jamesmichaelwhitmore/becoming-bistak-an-english-speakers-journey-with-and-analysis-of-the-cebuano-language-culture-699ea646c526))). Decision: Cebuano strings use Cebuano words where an everyday one exists; established English loans that Cebuanos really use are allowed in their Cebuano spelling (*dyip*, *traysikel*, *selpon*) or, for very common unadapted words, not at all in answers (a learner cannot know whether to type *bill* or *kuwenta*). Card notes may say "you will also hear the English word".
- **Tagalog** is not taught. Notes may warn where a Tagalog word is a trap (*hindi* is not Cebuano; *po* is not used) [practitioner opinion: a long-term expat in Cebu warns that teachers sometimes teach Tagalog as Cebuano, and that in Cebu "you will be treated better if you speak English than Tagalog" ([Live in the Philippines](https://liveinthephilippines.com/acculturation-language))].

### 2.2 Spelling policy: one rule

**Rule: every Cebuano word is written in one house spelling, the one given by the Komisyon sa Wikang Filipino's *Gabay sa Ortograpiyang Sinugbuánong Binisayâ* (KWF 2022), which follows the *Bisaya* magazine rules; where the guide is silent, the Cebu press (SunStar *Superbalita*) decides; no accent marks are written.** [curriculum convention]

What that gives (each point from the source opened):
- **u / o:** in native roots, *u* in the first and middle syllables and *o* in the last syllable: *buntag, kumusta, tubig, maayo, dako, ugma* (KWF §7.1; Wiktionary's Cebuano spelling appendix, rule 1, after the *Bisaya* rules: [Appendix: Cebuano spellings](https://en.wiktionary.com/wiki/Appendix:Cebuano_spellings)).
- **i / e:** *i* in all native words except *babaye* (woman), *pangadye* (pray), *dayeg* (praise) and *sikwate* (hot chocolate); *e* in loans (*kape, mesa, Lunes*) (KWF §7.1; Wiktionary rule 2).
- **Affixed words keep the root's vowels** (KWF §7.5: *himò → himoon*; Wiktionary rule 3: "O is kept in affixed forms … unlike Tagalog"). The course follows that for *himoon* (KWF's own example) and the many roots that simply add an ending (*paliton, palitan*). Whether it extends to *lutoon* (common: *lutuon*) is [inference] and listed in §11. Forms that drop a vowel (*kan-on* from *kaon*, *imnon* from *inom*; KWF's own *pil-a*, *wad-un*) are separate words, taught as such and never folded.
- **Prefixes:** *mo-* (will), *ni-* and *mi-* (did), *mag- / nag-*, *gi-*, *maka- / naka-*: KWF writes *mouyon, nipalit, nagtrabaho, gitugway*; *Superbalita* headlines write *mobalik, mokontra, mipatawag, midunggab* (fetched 2026-11-19). Bunye and Yap (1971) write *mu-*; the roadmap note already chose *mo-*. The course writes *mo-*.
- **og and ug:** *og* marks an indefinite object ("a / some": *Mopalit ko og saging*); *ug* is "and" (*ako ug ang akong mga igsoon*) (KWF §14; *Superbalita*: *Way ipalit og diaper*, *Damgo ug Hubad*). **This is a change from the roadmap and the brief, which write *ug* for both**; older books (Bunye and Yap, Wolff) do too. The split follows both authorities opened and lets a `why` line tell the marker from "and".
- **mga, nga, -ng:** *mga* (plural, said "manga"); *nga* links a describing word, and becomes *-ng* joined to a word that ends in a vowel (*dakong balay*), (KWF §13). After a word ending in *-n*, *nga* stays a separate word in the course (*balay nga gamay*); KWF's note that *nga* drops after *-ng* / *-m* is taught for recognition only.
- **Hyphen:** between a consonant and a vowel where the glottal catch falls (*kan-on, luy-a, kanus-a, mag-ampo, Taga-Amerika*) (KWF §10.1), and between the two halves of a repeated word (*hinay-hinay, lukso-lukso*) (KWF §7.7).
- **Short forms joined, no apostrophe:** the *Bisaya* rules drop the apostrophe and join the short form to the word before it: *lainlaing dapit, dakog kaon* (Wiktionary rule 4). So *unsay* (*unsa ang*), *walay* (*wala ang / wala og*), *naay*, *Nong* (for *Manong*), *Day* (for *Inday*). The course never writes an apostrophe in Cebuano.
- **Accent marks:** none. KWF §9 gives the accents for pronunciation only and says "Dili kini angay gamiton sa pagsuwat" (they should not be used in writing). The pronunciation aid (§6) carries stress and the catch instead.
- **Spanish and English loans** keep their vowels, with Cebuano consonants: *trabaho, kuwarta, eskuwelahan, dyip, otso* (Wiktionary's loanword tables, after Kilaton's *Mga Lagda sa Sinugboanong Panitik*).

**Alternates learners will see** (all accepted as "almost" by the requested fold, §10; never listed as accepted answers):

| House spelling | Also seen | Where |
|---|---|---|

### 2.4 Numbers: three systems

[curriculum convention] Wikipedia's article on Cebuano numerals says the Spanish-derived numbers are "mainly used for … counting large numbers, currency, solar time", and the native ones "for counting small numbers and basic measurement" ([Cebuano numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cebuano_numerals)). The roadmap's `numbers` item says the same.
- **Cebuano** (*usa, duha, tulo, upat, lima, unom, pito, walo, siyam, napulo*) for counting things, people, days, with *ka*: *usa ka saging, duha ka adlaw*. Produced in the course up to 10; *kawhaan* (20) and *usa ka gatos* (100) for recognition.
- **Spanish** for clock time (*alas tres, alas otso y medya*) and for prices (*singko pesos, baynte, kuwarenta*). Produced: 1–12 and the tens to 100 (*baynte, traynta, kuwarenta, singkuwenta, sesenta, setenta, otsenta, nobenta, siyen*).
- **English** numbers are common for prices and phone numbers in Cebu City [inference]; notes say so, but answers use Spanish for money and time so that one form is right.

### 2.5 Politeness without *po*; forms of address

- Cebuano has no *po* / *opo* (Tagalog) [inference, widely stated; Bunye and Yap's dialogues have none]. Politeness comes from **palihug** (please), **salamat / daghang salamat**, **the softeners *lang, sa, ba, unta***, a title, and the *-a / -i* command forms used with *palihug* (§8 `requests`).
- **"You":** *ikaw / ka* is used with everyone, including elders; respect is shown by a title, not by a different pronoun [inference; Bunye and Yap use *Kumusta ka, Mr. Smith?*].
- **Titles taught** [inference, to be checked natively (§11)]:

| Title | To whom | Course use |
|---|---|---|
| **Manong / Nong** | an older man; drivers, guards, vendors | taught in `greetings`; *Salamat, Nong.* |
| **Manang / Nang** | an older woman; vendors, helpers | taught in `greetings` |
| **Dong** (from *Dodong*) | a boy or young man | recognition |
| **Day** (from *Inday*) | a girl or young woman | recognition |
| **Kuya / Ate** | older brother / sister; young men and women in service jobs | heard everywhere in Cebu now (Tagalog origin) [inference]; recognition, accepted |
| **Sir / Ma'am** | customers, teachers, officials | staff say them constantly; learners hear them, need not say them |
| **Tiya / Tiyo**, **Lola / Lolo** | aunt / uncle, grandparents, and older family friends | taught in `family` |

- **Every English prompt that contains "you" to an older stranger carries a cue** ("(to an older man)" → *Nong*), so one answer is natural.

### 2.6 Voice

- **Tag:** `ttsLang: "ceb-PH"` (the roadmap's voice field). Devices: no mainstream phone or browser voice for Cebuano was found [inference: neither Apple nor Google lists one in the material opened; not verified, §11], so **most learners will have no device voice**.
- **Azure:** the Speech service language page lists Filipino (`fil-PH`) for speech-to-text and custom speech, and third-party voice lists name Azure `fil-PH` neural voices (Angelo, Blessica; seen in search results only, not opened), but **no Cebuano locale appears anywhere** on the language-support pages opened ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/ai-services/speech-service/language-support); [Azure China mirror](https://docs.azure.cn/zh-cn/ai-services/speech-service/language-support?tabs=stt-tts)). So recorded clips cannot be made with Azure in Cebuano.
- **Project preference:** the app's owner chose Azure voices for recorded audio (over ElevenLabs, which he found likely to get expensive) and wants native-sounding audio; for Cebuano that choice has no voice, so it needs the owner's decision (another engine, a native recording, or none).
- **Decision (needs the lead, §10):** keep `ceb-PH`. Do **not** fall back silently to a Filipino voice: a Tagalog voice reads Cebuano letters plausibly but puts Tagalog stress on shared words, which is exactly the thing the aid tries to teach [inference]. The course must work with no audio: the `pron` line is required on every card and example (§6), and the no-voice message should say "No Cebuano voice on this device: use the pronunciation line under each card." If the lead can make clips with another engine that has Cebuano (none verified here), they should be checked by a native before shipping.

### 2.7 Voice of the English text

- Plain, friendly US English with US spelling. A `why` is one sentence.
- Cebuano inside English text: in lesson bodies in `<b>`; in plain-text fields bare. Never add English endings to Cebuano words.
- Sample lines:
  1. "Cebuano puts the main word first: *Maayo ang balay*, literally 'good the house'."
  2. "*ko* is the short 'I' and comes second in the sentence: *Gigutom ko*."
  3. "Nice work! Review these cards tomorrow so they stick."

## 3. Glossary

Plain names used in English-language Cebuano materials [curriculum convention: Bunye and Yap use "topic", "marker", "Actor Focus", "Goal Focus", "ligature", "ako-class / nako-class pronouns"], made plainer for adults with no grammar [inference]. One term per idea, always the same:

| Term to use | Do not use | Note |
|---|---|---|
| noun, verb, adjective, question word | | |
| describing word (adjective) | modifier, stative | "describing word" in bodies, "adjective" allowed in `why` |
| **marker** (*ang, si, sa, ni, og, kang*) | case marker, determiner, article | **standing-rule keyword**; call *mga* "the plural word", never a marker |
| **the focus**; "the *ang* word" (the noun or pronoun marked by *ang* / *si*, or an *ako*-set pronoun) | topic, subject, absolutive, trigger | **standing-rule keyword** (§7). Gloss once: "the person or thing the sentence is about" |
| ***ako*-set** (*ako / ko, ikaw / ka, siya, kami / mi, kita / ta, kamo, sila*) | absolutive pronouns, set 1 | **standing-rule keyword** |
| ***nako*-set** (*nako / ko, nimo / mo, niya, namo, nato, ninyo, nila*) | ergative / genitive pronouns | **standing-rule keyword**; "after the noun" for owners |
| ***akong*-set** (*akong, imong, iyang, among, atong, inyong, ilang*) | preposed possessives | **standing-rule keyword**; "before the noun" |
| ***kanako*-set** (*kanako, kanimo, kaniya…*) | oblique / dative pronouns | "to me, for me"; *kang* + name |
| **short pronoun** (*ko, ka, mo, ta, mi*) | clitic, enclitic | **standing-rule keyword** |
| **second place** ("short words go in second place") | Wackernagel position, clitic climbing | **standing-rule keyword** |
| **the main word comes first** (*Maestra si Ana*: "teacher (is) Ana") | predicate-initial, VSO | **standing-rule keyword** "comes first" |
| **linker** (*nga*, *-ng*) | ligature, relativizer | |
| **particle** (*man, lang, ra, ba, diay, sab, pa, na, gyud*) | discourse marker, enclitic | "small word" in bodies |
| **root** (*palit* in *mopalit*) | base, stem | |
| **prefix** (*mo-, ni-, mag-, gi-…*), **ending** (*-on, -an, -a, -i*) | affix (except once as a gloss) | |
| **doer-focus verb** (*mo- / ni-*, *mag- / nag-*: the doer is the focus) | actor focus, active voice | **standing-rule keyword** "doer" |
| **thing-focus verb** (*-on / gi-*: the thing is the focus) | goal focus, object focus, patient focus, passive | **standing-rule keyword**; never call it "passive" |

## 4. Error zones of English speakers, ranked

**How the list is ranked** [inference]:
1. Items that occur in nearly every sentence and can be checked in typed practice.
2. Then sounds the spelling hides (stress, the catch).
3. Then lesson-local and vocabulary traps.

**The evidence is thin.** No error study of English-speaking learners of Cebuano was found. Bunye and Yap (1971), written for US learners, name the sound problems (unaspirated *p*, the glottal stop "not easily heard by an English speaker", initial *ng*) and treat focus, markers and pronoun sets as the core grammar. Learner accounts (Chiongbian-Khu: verb forms "the biggest challenge"; Whitmore, a US missionary; expats on *Live in the Philippines*) describe difficulty but not error frequencies. **The ranking below is [inference]**, built on those sources and on what the grammar forces in every sentence; every wrong → right pair is the author's and is listed for the native check (§11).

1. **Choosing the right pronoun set and marker**
   - Wrong → right:
     - *ako balay* → *akong balay* or *balay nako* (my house)
     - *Ang Pedro maestro.* → *Maestro si Pedro.* (Pedro is a teacher: names take *si*)
     - *Gipalit ako ang saging.* → *Gipalit nako ang saging.* (I bought the banana: the doer of a *gi-* verb takes the *nako*-set)
     - *ang balay sa Pedro* → *ang balay ni Pedro* (Pedro's house: names take *ni*)
     - *para ako* → *para nako* or *para kanako* (for me)
     - *Unsa imo ngalan?* → *Unsa ang imong ngalan?* / *Unsay imong ngalan?* (the *akong*-set always has *-ng*; *Kinsa imong ngalan?* is also heard [inference], so it is never used as a distractor)
   - Why: English has one "I / me / my" choice per position; Cebuano has four pronoun sets and five markers, and the choice depends on the verb's focus, not on "subject / object" [inference; Bunye and Yap Lessons 4, 8, 11 set out the sets].
   - Design consequence: strand (a) of the standing rule (§7).

2. **Word order: the main word first, short words in second place**
   - Wrong → right:
     - *Ako gigutom.* → *Gigutom ko.* (I'm hungry)
     - *Ka asa?* / *Ikaw asa?* → *Asa ka?* (Where are you [going]?)
     - *Dili ganahan ko.* → *Dili ko ganahan.* (I don't like it: the short pronoun moves up to right after *dili*)
     - *Siya maestra.* (as a plain statement) → *Maestra siya.* (She's a teacher)
     - *Mopalit og tubig ko.* → *Mopalit ko og tubig.* (I'll buy water)
   - Why: English puts the subject first; Cebuano puts the comment first (Bunye and Yap: "The predicate usually precedes the topic"; "*Ka* never appears initially in a sentence"). Short pronouns and particles (*ko, ka, man, ba, lang, pa, na*) cluster right after the first word [inference for the second-place detail].
   - Design consequence: strand (b) of the standing rule (§7).

3. **Verb focus and the future / past forms**
   - Wrong → right:
     - *Mokaon ko ang mangga.* → *Kaonon nako ang mangga.* or *Mokaon ko og mangga.* (a definite "the mango" prefers a thing-focus verb; a doer-focus verb takes *og* + an indefinite thing)
     - *Mokaon ko gahapon.* → *Nikaon ko gahapon.* (I ate yesterday: past form)
     - *Nikaon ko ugma.* → *Mokaon ko ugma.* (future form)
     - *Gipalit nako ugma.* → *Paliton nako ugma.* (I'll buy it tomorrow)
   - [practitioner opinion] Chiongbian-Khu: the *palit* family (*mopalit, paliton, ipalit, palitan, makapalit*) is "the biggest challenge", and her teacher called the choice "a matter of feel". Bunye and Yap define Actor and Goal Focus by which noun is the topic.
   - Design consequence: the focus choice is part of the standing rule from `mo` on ("who gets *ang*"), §7.

4. **dili or wala**
   - Wrong → right:
     - *Dili tubig.* (meaning "there's no water") → *Walay tubig.*
     - *Dili ko moadto gahapon.* → *Wala ko moadto gahapon.* (I didn't go yesterday)
     - *Wala ko moadto ugma.* → *Dili ko moadto ugma.* (I won't go tomorrow)
     - *Wala ko nikaon.* → *Wala ko mokaon.* (after *wala* the verb takes the future form; *ni-* after *wala* is heard in speech but is not taught) [inference]
     - *Wala ko Amerikano.* → *Dili ko Amerikano.* (I'm not American)
   - Why: English has one "not". The roadmap names this as one of its four hardest points. Bunye and Yap: "non-existence … is signalled by the negative form *wala*".
   - Design consequence: the preferred mixed neighbour in the last two items of every set from `negation` on.

5. **Sounds the spelling hides: stress and the catch**
   - Wrong → right: *balay* said BAH-lai → `bah-LAI`; *bata* (child) said with no catch → `BAH-tahq`; *maayo* said as two syllables "mai-yo" → `mah-AH-yoh` (three syllables, with a catch); *mga* said "m-ga" → `mah-NGAH`; *ngano* said "nano" → `NGAH-noh`; *p, t, k* with an English puff of air.
   - [curriculum convention] Bunye and Yap: "/p/ is not aspirated"; "The glottal stop /'/ is not easily heard by an English speaker"; "/ŋ/ written as ng occurs initially in words". Wiktionary's Cebuano convention: "Cebuano uses stress and/or final glottal stops to distinguish homographs" ([Wiktionary: About Cebuano](https://www.wiktionary.com/wiki/Wiktionary:About_Cebuano)), e.g. *bata* /ˈbataʔ/ "child" vs *bata* /ˈbata/ "nightgown" ([Wiktionary: bata](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bata)).
   - English speakers reduce unstressed vowels ("about"); Cebuano keeps every vowel full [inference].
   - Design consequence: the aid shows stress and the catch on every card (§6).

6. **Leaving out the linker *nga / -ng***
   - Wrong → right: *dako balay* → *dakong balay* (a big house); *balay dako* → *balay nga dako*; *gwapa babaye* → *gwapang babaye*.
   - [curriculum convention] KWF §13; Bunye and Yap: "The ligature *nga*, written as -ng after a vowel, connects words or phrases".

7. **Numbers: *ka*, and which system**
   - Wrong → right: *duha saging* → *duha ka saging* (two bananas); *alas tulo* → *alas tres* (three o'clock); *tulo pesos* in a shop → *tres pesos* (both are understood, but prices are said in Spanish) [curriculum convention: Wikipedia, §2.4].

8. **The wrong "we"**
   - Wrong → right: *Taga-Amerika kita.* (to a Cebuano, meaning "my friends and I") → *Taga-Amerika mi.* / *Taga-Amerika kami.* *Kita* includes the listener; *kami* leaves them out [curriculum convention: Wiktionary pronoun table lists *kita* "first person plural inclusive", *kami* "exclusive"].

9. **Calques**
   - Wrong → right:
     - *Asa ka gikan?* for "Where are you from (originally)?" → *Taga-asa ka?* (*Asa ka gikan?* = "Where have you just come from?")
     - *Unsa ka?* for "How are you?" → *Kumusta ka?*
     - *Ako adunay baynte singko anyos.* → *Baynte singko anyos na ko.* (I'm 25)
     - *Ako ganahan sa kape.* → *Ganahan ko og kape.* (I like coffee)
   - All [inference].

10. **Greeting times and address**
    - Wrong → right: *Maayong buntag* at 2 p.m. → *Maayong hapon*; *Maayong gabii* at 5 p.m. → *Maayong hapon*; a bare *Salamat* to an older vendor → *Salamat, Nang*; *po* added from Tagalog → nothing (Cebuano has no *po*).
    - [inference] Hour boundaries: *buntag* until about 11, *udto* around noon, *hapon* until dusk, *gabii* after dark.

11. **False friends and look-alikes** [inference, every pair for the native check, §11]
    - *para* = "for" and also "Stop!" to a jeepney driver; *bayad* = fare, payment (said when passing your fare); *libre* = free of charge, also "my treat"; *sige* = "OK, go ahead", also "bye"; *pan* = bread; *sala* = living room, and also "fault, sin"; *kuwarto* = room (not "quarter"); *traffic* = a traffic jam; *brownout* = a power cut; *ambot* = "I don't know".
    - Sound-alike pairs inside Cebuano: *bata* (child) / *bata* (nightgown) (Wiktionary, above); *wala* (none) / *wala* (left side), told apart by stress [Wiktionary lists both *walâ* and *walà* among Cebuano words; which is which was not confirmed, §11].
    - Design consequence: the "Traps" deck (§9).

12. **Tagalog leaking in**
    - Wrong → right: *hindi* → *dili*; *po* → (nothing); *ano* → *unsa*; *saan* → *asa*; *kain* → *kaon*.
    - [practitioner opinion] expats report teachers who taught Tagalog as Cebuano (Live in the Philippines). Relevant to heritage learners who grew up hearing Tagalog TV [inference].

## 5. Learners: who, why, what first

**Who** [inference unless tagged]
- **Heritage learners**: Filipino Americans with Visayan or Mindanao family. Chiongbian-Khu, a Filipino who already speaks Tagalog, learned Cebuano because "Tagalog does not go very far in Cebu" and to follow her mother's family [practitioner opinion]. US census counts of Cebuano speakers were not opened (§11).
- **Partners and in-laws** of Cebuanos, and **retirees and expats** in Cebu, Bohol, Dumaguete, Davao and Cagayan de Oro. An expat who learned over four years: locals "don't speak English often, especially if they are in a friendly discussion", and speaking Cebuano makes you "part of the family" [practitioner opinion: Live in the Philippines].
- **Missionaries and volunteers**: a US Latter-day Saint missionary describes six weeks of "accelerated basic Cebuano" at the Utah training center before two years in the Cebu Mission [practitioner opinion: Whitmore].
- **Travellers and remote workers**: Cebu, Bohol, Siargao, Moalboal, Oslob, Malapascua (diving).
- Nearly all are true beginners. Heritage learners often understand more than they can say, and may know Tagalog (§4.12).

**Why they study** [inference]: family first, then living there, then service and work, then travel. No survey was found.

**What they need first** [inference]
1. **Greetings, thanks, please and titles** (*Maayong buntag, Salamat, Nong, Nang*): every exchange starts there.
2. **The feel of the particles** (*man, lang, ba, diay*): Cebuano without them sounds blunt (Whitmore notes the directness "can often be misinterpreted for anger"); `particles` stays third, as in Bunye and Yap.
3. **Saying who they are** and asking back (*Taga-Amerika ko. Taga-asa ka?*).
4. **"I'm hungry / tired / full"** (*Gigutom ko, Kapoy ko, Busog na ko*): constant at family meals. Added as `feelings`.
5. **Prices in Spanish numbers** and buying at a market or sari-sari store (*Tagpila?*).
6. **Food and drink**, **jeepneys and directions** (*Para!*, *Bayad*).
7. **Doer-focus verbs before thing-focus verbs**, as every course does.

**Check of the roadmap against a beginner syllabus** [curriculum convention]
- The roadmap follows Bunye and Yap's order (greetings and particles → demonstratives and question words → pronouns → *taga-* and place markers → actor-focus verbs → *ni / sa* and *nako*-class → adjectives → existentials and negation → time and numbers → want / need → goal focus → comparatives). Its own `note` says that cutting 45 lessons to 26 A1 lessons was the researcher's judgment.
- Language Links' *Magbinisaya Kita* course 1 (named in the roadmap's `src`) covers non-verbal sentences, "there is / has", "want / like" and "need" first: the same core (not opened for this profile).
- **Changes for US adult learners** (§8): two lessons added (`feelings`, `requests`); `negation` stays after the first verbs but `aduna` already teaches *wala* for "there isn't"; nothing removed.

## 6. Pronunciation aid (`pron`)

**Evidence.**
- [curriculum convention] Cebuano spelling is regular for consonants and writes three vowel sounds with five letters (*a*; *i / e*; *u / o*: KWF §7.1). It **does not write stress or the glottal catch**, and both distinguish words: Wiktionary's Cebuano guidelines say "Cebuano uses stress and/or final glottal stops to distinguish homographs" and mark them on headwords (acute = stress off the next-to-last syllable; grave = final catch; circumflex = final stress and catch), the same three marks KWF §9 gives for pronunciation (*pahilís, paiwà, pakupyâ*).
- [curriculum convention] Bunye and Yap (1971), for US learners: the glottal stop "is not easily heard by an English speaker"; *p* is unaspirated; *ng* starts words.
- **The author's own recall of Cebuano stress is not reliable.** Before checking sources, the author froze guesses for 63 words, then checked what sources could show. **17 of them have IPA or a written mark in a source**: the author got **12 right, but 10 of those were the numerals** (Tagalog cognates); of the other 7, only *akó* and *kamí* were right, and *palít, karón, ugmâ, bugás* and *walâ* (/waˈlaʔ/) were wrong. *balay* (/baˈlaj/), seen just before freezing, would also have been wrong. A further 18 words appear only unmarked in word lists, which proves nothing (the same lists leave *usa, tulo, dako* unmarked although better sources mark them), so they are not scored. Details: §6.4.
- No study comparing respelling, IPA and no aid for English-speaking learners of Cebuano was found. The choice is [inference], as in the earlier courses.

**Options weighed.**

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| (a) English-letter respelling, generated from the spelling plus a checked word list (`LEX`) | Same format as `en-es` and `en-id`; engine, lint, validator and `repron.js` need nothing new. The same text always gets the same `pron`. Syllables, *ng*, *mga*, the catch inside words (spelling hyphens, vowel + vowel), *ts*, *dy*, glides are fully regular and generated exactly. One place to fix a word. | Stress and the final catch must come from `LEX`; an unlisted word is printed all lowercase (no stress shown) with a warning, so most of the course's vocabulary starts as "unchecked" until the lead checks it. |
| (b) Hand-typed by writers in a strict format | No word list to build. | Writers' recall is likely no better than the author's (2 of 7 non-numeral words, above); four writers would disagree with each other, and the lint only compares identical strings. |
| (c) IPA | Exact. | US adult self-learners cannot read it [inference]. |
| (d) No aid | Spelling is regular. | Leaves stress and the catch, which the spelling never shows, to guesswork on every word, and most learners will have no voice (§2.6). |

**Decision: (a), `pron: "required"`, generated by `tools/target/ceb/respell.js`.**
- `node tools/target/ceb/respell.js "<text>"` prints `<text> -> <pron>`; `require('./respell.js').respell(text)` returns the string. A writer never types a `pron`.
- **`LEX` holds only words with IPA or a written mark in a source** (172 now, each source in §6.4 and `STRESS-AUDIT.md`). No word is added from memory or from an unmarked list.
- **A word not in `LEX`** of two or more syllables is written all lowercase with hyphens between syllables and no final `q` (*dili* → `dee-lee`): the aid makes no claim about its stress or a final catch, rather than show a capitalised guess that is right about as often as a coin flip. stderr gets `respell: check stress / final catch in «word»`. Writers collect these (`repron.js` collects the words in « »), report them, and the lead checks them in batches (Wolff's dictionary, searchable at [bohol.ph/wced.php](https://bohol.ph/wced.php); Wiktionary entries with IPA or a marked headword), then adds them to `LEX` and runs `node tools/target/repron.js ceb`.
- **Prefixed forms** (*mo- mi- ni- mag- nag- gi- maka- naka- ma- na- ka- pa- pag- taga- i- ig-*) of a listed root are matched to the root **with a warning** ("derived from palit"), but because that prefixes do not move stress is [inference] (§11), and a look-alike can fool the match (*tulog* "sleep" would be read as *tuló* + *-g*), a derived form is written all lowercase like any unchecked word (*mopalit* → `moh-pah-leet`) until the form itself is listed. **Suffixed forms** (*-on, -an, -a, -i*) are never derived, because the ending moves the stress and can drop a vowel (*kaon → kan-on*); each is listed or warns.
- **Joined short forms**: a listed word + linker *-ng*, or + *-y* / *-g*, probably keeps the word's syllables and stress and loses the final catch (*dakô* → *dakong*; *walâ* → *walay*, matching Wiktionary's *waláy*), but that is [inference], so these too are written all lowercase with a "derived" warning (*dakong* `dah-kohng`, *walay* `wah-lai`) until the form itself is listed.
- **Homographs** (*wala* none / left, *bata* child / nightgown, *kita* we / see) always warn. The lint allows one `pron` per spelling, so the aid shows the commoner reading (*wala* = `wah-LAHQ` "none", *bata* = `BAH-tahq` "child"); a card for the other reading must say in its note that the aid does not fit it, until the engine allows a per-card override (§10.1).
- **Card front**: the Cebuano text, with the `pron` line under it and the meaning on the back, as in the other courses. **The `pron` line stays visible for the whole course by default** (no auto-blur after stage 1, unlike `es`), because stress and the catch can never be read from the spelling and most learners have no voice; the learner may hide it in settings. No aid in practice items (PRACTICE-SPEC).

**What the respelling does not show, on purpose** (each goes in `sounds` and card notes):
- the catch at the start of a word that begins with a vowel (*ako*), which every such word has and English speakers also do before a stressed vowel ("uh-oh");
- unaspirated *p t k* (Bunye and Yap); the tap *r* (`r`);
- that *i / e* and *u / o* are each one sound with a range (the aid writes the spelled letter: `ee` / `eh`, `oo` / `oh`);
- vowel length (Wolff's analysis treats stress partly as length; not shown).

**What lesson 1 (`sounds`) must teach, so the aid can be read:**
1. Three vowel sounds, five letters: *a* `ah`; *i* `ee` (and *e*, mostly in loans, `eh`); *u* `oo` (and *o*, in last syllables, `oh`). Keep every vowel full.
2. CAPITALS = the stressed syllable. Stress is often on the last syllable (*balay* `bah-LAI`), and moving it can change the word. A word in all lowercase (`dee-lee`) has a stress the course has not yet checked: listen, and do not guess from English.
3. The catch: a syllable in the aid that starts with a vowel (`mah-AH-yoh`, `seep-OHN`) starts with a catch, as in "uh-oh". A `q` at the end of a word is a catch there (*bata* `BAH-tahq`): stop the breath; do not add a k.
4. *ng* as in "singer", also at the start of a word (*ngano*); *mga* is said "manga".
5. *ay* `ai` (as in "Thai"), *aw* `ow` (as in "how"), *uy* `ooy`, *iw* `eew`, *oy* `oy`.
6. *ts* `ch` (*otso*), *dy* `j` (*dyip*), *g* always hard (`gh` before `ee` / `eh`), *r* tapped.
7. Listen when a voice exists; otherwise trust the aid over English habits.

### 6.1 The key (exact format)

**Format**
- Lowercase ASCII letters; words separated by single spaces, syllables inside a word by hyphens.
- **Capitals appear only where the stress has been checked against a source** (a `LEX` form, §6.4): such a word of two or more syllables has exactly one syllable in CAPITALS. A word whose stress is not checked is all lowercase, with hyphens between syllables and no final `q` (*dili* → `dee-lee`). One-syllable words are lowercase (*ang* → `ahng`, *ko* → `koh`).
- No punctuation, accent marks, apostrophes or digits. Write numbers as words.
- A hyphen between two copies of a word (*hinay-hinay*) makes two words in the aid: `hee-nai hee-nai` (each half is respelled as a word; *hinay* is unchecked, so lowercase). A spelling hyphen before a vowel (*kanus-a, mag-ampo, Taga-Amerika*) stays inside one word: it is a syllable break with a catch.

**Vowels**

| Cebuano | Aid | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a | ah | *salamat* → `sah-LAH-maht` |
| i | ee | *tubig* → `TOO-beeg` |
| e | eh | *kape* → `kah-peh` |
| u | oo | *buntag* → `boon-tahg` |
| o | oh | *maayo* → `mah-AH-yoh` |
| ay | ai | *balay* → `bah-LAI` |
| aw | ow | *adlaw* → `ahd-low` (stress not yet checked) |
| uy / oy / iw / ey | ooy / oy / eew / ay | *luy-a* → `looy-ah` |
| two vowels side by side | separate syllables; the second starts with a catch | *kaon* → `KAH-ohn`; *gabii* → `gah-BEE-ee` |

**Consonants** (only those that differ; all others as written: b d h k l m n p r s t w y)

| Cebuano | Aid | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ng | ng (also at the start of a word) | *ngalan* → `NGAH-lahn` |
| mga | mah-ngah (whole-word exception; stress unchecked) | *mga bata* → `mah-ngah BAH-tahq` |
| g | g; gh before ee / eh | *gikan* → `ghee-kahn` |
| ts + vowel | ch | *otso* → `oh-choh` |
| dy + vowel | j | *dyip* → `jeep` |
| hyphen before a vowel | syllable break (catch) | *sip-on* → `seep-OHN` |
| final catch (from `LEX`) | q after the last vowel | *bata* → `BAH-tahq`; *dako* → `dah-KOHQ` |
| c, f, j, q, v, x, z | (not in house spelling; foreign names only, the generator stops with an error for c q f v x z) | |

**Syllable breaks**
- One consonant goes with the next vowel; *ng* counts as one consonant.
- Two consonants split (*bun-tag*), except *pr br tr dr kr gr pl bl kl gl* and the loan clusters *ky gw kw sw by py dw tw*, which stay together (*trabaho* → `trah-BAH-hoh`).
- A spelling hyphen marks the break exactly.
- A final *y / w* joins the vowel before it as a glide (*balay*, *adlaw*).

### 6.2 `pronPattern`

```
^(?!.*[cC](?![hH]))(?!.*(?<![aA])[iI])(?!.*[aA](?![hHiIyY]))(?!.*(?<![eE])[eE](?![hHeE]))(?!.*(?<![oO])[oO](?![hHoOyYwW]))(?!.*[qQ](?=[a-zA-Z]))(?!.*[jJ](?![aeoAEO]))(?:[abcdeghijklmnopqrstwy]+|(?:[abcdeghijklmnopqrstwy]+-)+[ABCDEGHIJKLMNOPQRSTWY]+(?:-[abcdeghijklmnopqrstwy]+)*|[ABCDEGHIJKLMNOPQRSTWY]+(?:-[abcdeghijklmnopqrstwy]+)+)(?: (?:[abcdeghijklmnopqrstwy]+|(?:[abcdeghijklmnopqrstwy]+-)+[ABCDEGHIJKLMNOPQRSTWY]+(?:-[abcdeghijklmnopqrstwy]+)*|[ABCDEGHIJKLMNOPQRSTWY]+(?:-[abcdeghijklmnopqrstwy]+)+))*$
```

The Indonesian pattern with the letter set changed (no *f, u, v, x, z*; *j* only before a vowel). It enforces:
- only the key's letters; no accents, apostrophes, digits or punctuation; single spaces;
- *c* only in *ch*; *i* only after *a* (`ai`); no *u* at all (Cebuano *u* is `oo`); *a* only as `ah / ai / ay`; *e* only as `eh / ee`; *o* only as `oh / oo / ow / oy`; *q* only at the end of a syllable;
- every multi-syllable word is either all lowercase (stress unchecked) or has exactly one CAPITAL syllable (stress checked); one-syllable words lowercase; no empty syllables (no doubled, leading or trailing hyphen) and no mixed case inside a syllable.

Because an all-lowercase word is now well formed, the pattern can no longer tell a missing capital from an unchecked word; only the generator decides which words get capitals.

Compiled with the `u` flag. Tested (node): it accepts the 22 worked examples below and the 90 sample cards of §9, and rejects the raw Cebuano of 111 of those 112 strings. The one that passes as it stands is the card *kay* (because), whose raw spelling is also a well-formed one-syllable aid string; any one-word raw string with only *a*, *y* and consonants (*baybay*) would pass too, so the pattern alone cannot prove a `pron` was generated. It cannot catch a wrong but well-formed stress; only `LEX` and a native check can.

### 6.4 The generator and its word list

`tools/target/ceb/respell.js` (about 170 lines, no dependencies).
- **Usage:** `node tools/target/ceb/respell.js "Maayong buntag!"` → `Maayong buntag! -> mah-ah-yohng boon-tahg` (both words unchecked, so lowercase). Several arguments give several lines. Words with digits or *c q f v x z* throw an error (write numbers as words; foreign names stay in English prompts).
- **`LEX` notation** (Wiktionary / KWF marks): no mark = next-to-last stress; acute = stress on that vowel; grave on the last vowel = final catch with next-to-last stress; circumflex = final stress and catch. `EXC` holds whole words given as is (*mga*).
- **Sources of the 172 `LEX` forms** (all opened 2026-11-19). The first 48 (now 47, see *dinhî* below) came from the profile's own check:
  - Wiktionary IPA: *baláy* /baˈlaj/, *maayo* /maˈʔajo/, *batà* /ˈbataʔ/, *gabii* /ɡaˈbiʔi/, *akó* /ʔaˈko/, *walâ* /waˈlaʔ/ (also /waˈla/).
  - Wiktionary marked headwords: *dakô*, *túbig*, *ugmâ*; *Cebú* (whole-word exception `seh-BOO`, since *c* is not in the house letters); *Sugbò* (the example in the documentation of Wiktionary's Cebuano IPA template).
  - Wikipedia, *Cebuano numerals*: *usá duhá tuló upát limá unóm pitó waló siyám napulò* (one Wiktionary list writes *duhâ*, *napulô*, *upat*; Wikipedia's table was preferred). *gabii* is kept as IPA gives it although one list writes *gabií*.
  - Wiktionary appendices (Cebuano–Waray, Cebuano–Hiligaynon, Cebuano–Tagalog relations), marked forms: *bugás karón palít itlóg ulán pulá likód ubó kilát ikóg hagdán putî tungâ pilì yawà lungsód ~~dinhî~~ irô lumà matandâ sip-ón kolór lagós siyudád kamí buhì dagsà gamót*.
  - **Seen only unmarked in those lists** (not evidence by themselves): *salamat buntag hapon udto kumusta dili ikaw kita kamo sila lami init kaon inom amahan inahan tawo dugo bulan payong asawa tuig gamit dagan bukid gikan diha didto usab sunod babaye imo inyo*. ## 7. Standing practice rule

**Rule: the right set and marker, in the right place.** Every lesson's practice set keeps re-testing two strands:
- **(a) Pronoun set and marker.** Names take *si / ni / kang*, other nouns *ang / sa*; an indefinite thing after a doer-focus verb or *naa / wala* takes *og*. The focus (the *ang* word) is an *ako*-set pronoun (*ako / ko, ikaw / ka, siya…*); an owner is *akong*-set before the noun (*akong balay*) or *nako*-set after it (*balay nako*); the doer of a thing-focus or place-focus verb is *nako*-set (*Gipalit nako*); "to / for me" is *kanako*-set. From `mo` on, the strand includes **who gets *ang***: the doer with *mo- / mag-*, the thing with *-on / gi-*, the person or place with *-an*.
- **(b) The main word first, short words in second place.** The comment comes first (*Maestra si Ana*, *Gigutom ko*, *Lami kaayo*); short pronouns (*ko, ka, mo, ta, mi*) and particles (*man, ba, lang, pa, na, sab, gyud*) follow the first word, and move up behind *dili, wala*, a question word or *gusto* (*Dili ko ganahan*, *Asa ka?*).

**Why these two.**
- [inference, from Bunye and Yap and the grammar] They are §4.1 and §4.2. Both occur in essentially every sentence from lesson 1 (*Maayo man ko*, *Ako si Emma*, *Taga-Amerika ko*), which matters because the JSON has no exemption field, and both can be checked mechanically in typed answers.
- **Candidates rejected as the standing rule:** verb focus and aspect (§4.3) cannot be tested before lesson 16 (`mo`); it is folded into strand (a) from there as "who gets *ang*". The linker *nga* (§4.6) appears in only some sentences. *dili / wala* (§4.4) is the preferred mixed neighbour from `negation` on.
- **Against:** no learner-error data for English speakers backs the ranking (§4, §11).

**Count** (`minOrder: 2`, `minType: 2`): in every lesson's set, at least **2 `order` items** and **2 `type` items** hinge on the rule, and their `why` names it with a keyword.

**What counts as hinging** (mechanical, so a reviewer can check it):
- **`order`** hinges if the `why` matches the keywords and **either**:
  - the answer puts a short pronoun or particle in second place where English puts the subject first (*I'm hungry* → *Gigutom ko*; *Where are you going?* → *Asa ka?*), or the comment before the focus (*Ana is a teacher* → *Maestra si Ana*);
  - **or** `x` holds the wrong-set or wrong-marker twin of a tile: *nako* for *ko* (*Gigutom nako* is wrong), *ako* for *akong* (*ako balay*), *ang* for *si* before a name, *sa* for *ni* before a name, *ako* for *nako* after a *gi-* verb.
  - A distractor must be wrong, not merely less common: never *ako* as a distractor for *ko* after a verb (*Gigutom ako* is correct, if emphatic), and never *ug* for *og* (§10).
  - Example: `['or', "I'm hungry", 'Gigutom ko', 'nako', 'The short pronoun ko goes in second place, after the main word: Gigutom ko.']`
- **`type`** hinges if the `why` matches the keywords and the first accepted answer contains **either** a short pronoun after the first word, a name with *si / ni / kang*, an owner in the *akong*- or *nako*-set, a *kanako*-set pronoun, or (from `mo` on) a verb whose focus decides the marker (*Gipalit nako ang saging*).
  - Example: `['ty', 'Pedro is a teacher', 'Maestro si Pedro', 'maestro · Pedro', 'ang', 'Names take si, and the main word comes first: Maestro si Pedro.']` (*ang* cannot rebuild an accepted answer).
- **Accepted answers, more rules:**
  - "I" after a verb or describing word: list both *ko* and *ako* (*Gigutom ko|Gigutom ako*); "you": *ka* and *ikaw* likewise. The short form is `a[0]`.
  - Owners: list both *akong balay* and *balay nako* when the prompt does not cue one; the lesson `nako` cues "(owner after the noun)" when it wants one.
  - Past doer-focus: list both *ni-* and *mi-* forms (*Nikaon ko|Mikaon ko*); *ni-* is `a[0]` (§2.2).
  - *naa / aduna* and *Unsa ang / Unsay*: list both where both are natural (§2.3).

**Lessons where the rule is hardest, with two hinges each** (order / type; all wording [inference] for the native check):
- `sounds`: order "my house" → *akong balay* (x: *ako*); type "my water" → *akong tubig|tubig nako*. why: "akong-set words go before the noun and add -ng: akong balay."
- `greetings`: order "How are you? (to a friend)" → *Kumusta ka* (x: *nimo*); type "I'm fine (reply)" → *Maayo man ko|Maayo man ako|Maayo man*. why: "ka is the short pronoun and goes in second place."
- `this-that`: order "This is my book" → *Akong libro kini* / *Libro nako kini*; why: "The main word comes first: akong libro kini." (accept *Kini akong libro* only if the native check confirms it, §11).
- `numbers`: order "I have two children" → *Duha akong anak* (x: *ako*); type "my three brothers and sisters" → *akong tulo ka igsoon*. why: "akong goes before the noun."
- `time`: order "I'll leave at three" → *Mobiya ko alas tres* (x: *nako*); type "I woke up at six" → *Nimata ko alas sais|Mimata ko alas sais|Nimata ko sa alas sais*. why: "The short pronoun ko goes in second place, after the verb."
- `negation`: order "I don't like it" → *Dili ko ganahan* (x: *nako*); type "I didn't go" → *Wala ko moadto|Wala ako moadto*. why: "The short pronoun moves up to second place, right after dili."
- `on`: order "I bought the bananas" → *Gipalit nako ang saging* (x: *ako*); type "Pedro will eat the mango" → *Kaonon ni Pedro ang mangga*. why: "With a thing-focus verb the doer takes the nako-set and the thing gets ang."

**Per-lesson notes for writers**
- Every lesson's practice set satisfies §7. Sentences marked "(as a phrase)" above are taught whole before their grammar arrives; their exact wording is in §11 for the native check.
- The last two items of each set may mix in a neighbour from an earlier lesson. Preferred neighbours: *dili / wala* (from `negation` on), *si / ang* (from `ako` on), *akong / nako* (from `nako` on), *Spanish / Cebuano numbers* (from `numbers` on).
- `mo`, `mag`, `on`, `an`, `maka`: typed items give the root in the hint (*hint: kaon · saging*) and the model answer has the full form; both *ni-* and *mi-* are accepted for the past of `mo` verbs.
- `on`, `an`: never write "passive" or "was …-ed" in a `why`; say "thing-focus" or "place-focus".
- `time`, `prices`: prompts give the time or price in digits in the English ("at 3:00", "₱20"), and the accepted answer is Spanish (*alas tres*, *baynte pesos*).

## 9. Decks

**18 decks in 5 groups**, 16–18 cards each (about 300 cards in total). Card shape: `target | pron | meaning | note`.
- `target`: Cebuano in the house spelling (§2.2). Nouns often in short phrases that show the standing rule (*akong balay*, *balay nako*), and sentences with the short pronoun in second place (*Gigutom ko*).
- `pron`: generated (§6). Most words will carry a "check stress" warning until the lead's batch check (§6.4); writers keep the generated string.
- `meaning`: short American English; no two cards in a deck share a meaning.
- `note`: one English line, or empty for at most a quarter of a deck. Alternate spellings, short forms, the right title and false-friend warnings go here.

**Groups, in order:**

| Group | Decks |
|---|---|
| First steps | Sounds and spelling · Greetings and courtesy · About me · People and family |
| Everyday words | Numbers and money · Time and days · Food and drink · Shopping and prices · Home and daily life |
| Out and about | Places and directions · Jeepneys and travel · Health and help |
| Conversation | Describing things · Feelings and wants · Questions and little words · Everyday verbs |
| Watch out | Traps for English speakers · Short forms and Cebu talk |

**Deck rules for writers**
- Use a title where a learner meets staff or strangers (shops, jeepneys, directions, health), and say so in the note ("to an older woman: Nang").
- Write prices and times as words (*baynte pesos*, *alas tres*); the note may show the written form (₱20, 3:00).
- No Tagalog, no *po*. English loans only in their Cebuano spelling and only if Cebuanos really say them (*dyip*, *kuryente*).
- **Traps for English speakers** (about 17 cards): look-alikes and double meanings (*para, libre, sala, kuwarto, pan, sige, bayad, traffic, brownout*); the *dili / wala* pairs (4 cards); calques (*Taga-asa ka?* vs *Asa ka gikan?*, age with *anyos*, *Ganahan ko og…*); the stress pairs only after the native check (*wala* none / left, §11).
- **Short forms and Cebu talk** (about 16 cards): *Unsa man?, Diay?, gyud, di, wa, ra, sab / pod / sad, ni / na / to, unsay, naay, walay, Nong, Day, Ambot, Sige, Amping*, each with the full form in the note.

## 10. Typing and script

**Script.** Plain Latin letters, left to right, words separated by spaces (roadmap `script`: "Latin (no extra letters)"). House spelling uses no accent marks (KWF §9) and only the letters *a b d e g h i k l m n o p r s t u w y*, plus *ng* as one sound; *c f j ñ q v x z* appear only in names and unadapted loans. **There is no script stage**: no letter decks, no letter-drill machine, no romanisation answers (`pronAnswers` off). `sounds` is an ordinary lesson with an ordinary deck.

**`targetScript`**: `[A-Za-z]`. Every Cebuano string must contain at least one character from it. **It also matches any English string**, so it cannot catch an English answer in a Cebuano field (same limitation as `id`).

**Accent bar** (`keys`): empty. **`accents: false`.** A learner whose phone inserts an accent (*á*) is marked wrong in the current matcher; the lead may prefer to fold accents anyway (as suggested for `id`), since KWF and dictionaries print *á à â* in pronunciation guides that learners may copy.

**Answer mode**: typing by default (a US keyboard has every letter); tiles as the no-keyboard fallback, as in the other Latin courses. No `tilesDefault`.

**Hyphens are spelling.** Two kinds (§2.2): the catch hyphen (*kan-on, luy-a, bag-o, kanus-a, mag-ampo, Taga-Amerika*) and the repeat hyphen (*hinay-hinay*).
- A hyphen typed as a space is already "almost".
- **Request:** a hyphen left out (*kanon, bago, kanusa, hinayhinay*) graded "almost" with the hyphenated form shown. Natives write *kanusa* and *bago* too [inference]. Until then, writers list only the hyphenated form.
- In `order` items a hyphenated word is one tile.

**Apostrophes.** House spelling has none (*Nong, Day, naay, unsay*). The matcher strips apostrophes at word edges, so *'Nong* already matches *Nong*. **Request:** strip an apostrophe inside a word too and compare (*na'y*, *wa'y*), graded "almost"; note that *na'y* stripped is *nay*, not *naay*, so the pair needs the short-form table below, not just stripping.

**The u / o and i / e alternates of the same word** (§2.2 table: *mukaon / mokaon, ug / og, gyod / gyud, himuon / himoon, kwarta / kuwarta*; not *imnon*, which is a different form, §2.2).
- They are real Cebuano spellings, not mistakes, so they must never be marked plain wrong; but the course teaches one spelling, so they are not "correct" either.
- **Request (priority 4):** a per-course fold, used **only for "almost"**: compare with *o→u* and *e→i* applied to both strings (and *w* dropped between a consonant and a vowel: *kwarta / kuwarta*, *jud → gyud* listed separately); if the folded strings match and the exact strings do not, grade "almost" and show the house form.
- **Collision check** [inference]: in the A1 vocabulary planned, no two different words differ only by *u / o* or *i / e*. The one pair that folds together, *og* (marker) and *ug* ("and"), is the same word in older spelling, so "almost" is right for it. Writers must not use, as a `fill` wrong option or tile distractor, a form that differs from the answer only by *u / o* or *i / e*, because the fold would grade it "almost".

**Short forms and alternates of the same word** (§2.3). **Request:** a per-course pair table graded "almost" with the note "taught form: …": *di → dili, wa → wala, way → walay, anaa → naa, jud / gyod → gyud, pud / sad → pod / sab* (only where the lesson's form is the other one), *mu- → mo-*, *'Nong → Nong*. Until then, writers list only the taught forms, except that ***ni- and mi-*** (both standard, §2.2), ***naa and aduna*** where both mean "there is / have", ***Unsa ang / Unsay***, and ***ko / ako, ka / ikaw*** after a verb are listed as accepted answers in full.

**Punctuation.** Cebuano has no ¿ ¡. The engine strips `. , ! ? ~` and quotes. Authors still write final punctuation in `a[0]`, card targets and examples.

**Case.** Not checked. Authors capitalise the first word of a sentence, names, places, *Amerika*, days and months (*Lunes, Enero*), and titles used in address (*Salamat, Nong*); *Taga-Amerika* keeps the capital after the hyphen.

**What a typed answer must include**
- The marker and pronoun set required (*si / ang*, *ni / sa*, *akong / nako*), and the main word first with short words in second place (the standing rule).
- *ka* between a Cebuano number and its noun; the linker *nga / -ng* where a describing word joins a noun; the hyphen.
- The verb form the lesson teaches (*mo- / ni-*, *-on / gi-*), and *dili* or *wala* as the meaning needs.

**What it need not include**
- *ko / ka* vs *ako / ikaw* after a verb (both listed); *naa / aduna*; *ni- / mi-*.
- Particles the prompt does not ask for (*man, lang, ba*): writers list answers with and without *man* in greetings replies (*Maayo man|Maayo*), and cue particles in the prompt when the item is about them.
- Final punctuation and capitals.

**`fill` and tile fallbacks.** Wrong options should be standing-rule twins wherever possible: the wrong set (*ako / nako / akong / kanako*), the wrong marker (*ang* for *si*, *sa* for *ni*), the English order, *dili / wala*, the wrong focus form (*mopalit / gipalit / paliton*). A distractor must be wrong, not merely casual or an alternate spelling: never *di*, *wa*, *ug*, *mu-*, *mi-* as a "wrong" option.

## 11. Gaps and unverified claims

**No native speaker has checked this profile, and the author's own Cebuano is measurably weak** (2 of 7 non-numeral words right on stress / final catch where a source shows them, §6). Check these first, in this order:
1. **Stress and the final catch of almost every word in the course.** `LEX` has 172 forms with IPA or written marks (157 of the 364 core words of two or more syllables; `tools/target/ceb/STRESS-AUDIT.md` lists the 207 still unverified, for a native speaker). Every other word, including *buntag, dili, asa, pila* (no IPA or conflicting sources), is printed all lowercase with no stress shown, and warns; a next-to-last default was dropped because the author's guesses were right for only 2 of 7 checkable words. Sources disagree on *adlaw / adláw, kan-on / kan-ón, kamot / kamót*, and on which of *walà / walâ* is "left" and which is "none" (Wiktionary IPA gives /waˈlaʔ/ for the negative, used in `LEX`). The lead (or a native with Wolff's dictionary) must clear the warnings in batches before release.
2. **Generator rules that are [inference]:** prefixes do not move stress (*mopalit* → `moh-pah-leet`; derived forms warn); a joined *-ng / -y / -g* keeps the stress and drops the catch (*akong* from *akó*; *dakong*; only *waláy* is attested; all printed lowercase until listed); *mga* = `mah-ngah` (stress unchecked, so lowercase); *ts* = `ch` (*otso*; *litson* may be *lit-son*); *dy* = `j`; place names with vowel pairs (*Mandaue* comes out `mahn-dah-oo-eh`, stress unchecked and the syllables probably wrong; such names need `LEX` or stay in English prompts).
3. **Spelling decisions:** *og* for the marker (a change from the roadmap, following KWF and *Superbalita*); *gyud* kept against KWF's u / o rule; *ni-* as the model past (KWF writes *nipalit*, *Superbalita* *mi-*); *sab* as the taught "also" (Cebu City may prefer *sad* or *pod*); KWF's *himoon* against the common *himuon*, and whether the kept-vowel rule covers *lutoon* (common *lutuon*); Spanish numbers *baynte, traynta, kuwarenta, singkuwenta, siyen* (Wikipedia lists *beynte / baynti, treynta / traynta*, *singku*, *dyis*).
4. **Every wrong → right pair in §4 and every sentence marked "(as a phrase)" in §8**, especially: *Wala ko mokaon* (vs *nikaon* after *wala*), *Dili ko ganahan*, *Maestro si Pedro* (and whether *Si Pedro maestro* is acceptable), *Akong libro kini* / *Kini akong libro*, *Duha akong anak*, *Mobiya ko alas tres*, *Nimata ko alas sais*, *Kaonon ni Pedro ang mangga*, *Hugasan nako ang plato*, *Gisulatan nako si Ana*, *Nagpuyo ko sa Cebu*, *Nalipay ko nga nakaila nimo*, *Unsa nang orasa?*, *Pila mo kabuok?*, *Unsa inyong naa?*, *Lugar lang*, *Asa ni moagi?*, *Pwede pahangyo?*, *Masakit akong tiyan*, *Ganahan ko og kape*, *Gusto ko og kape*, *Naa koy tubig*, *Busog na ko*, *Kapoy kaayo ko*, *Mas dako kini*, *kining balay*, and whether *Kinsa imong ngalan?* is normal.
5. **Words chosen from the author's knowledge** (meaning and current Cebu City use): *pahangyo, sukli, sud-an, habal-habal, botika, kasilyas, liko, kuryente, ambot, libre, sala, tabang, tiyan, bugnaw, tugnaw, magulang, manghod, ig-agaw, kabuok, puso, budbud, danggit*.
6. **Address and register:** *Manong / Manang, Nong / Nang, Dong, Day, Kuya / Ate*, and *ikaw / ka* being fine with elders; the claim that Cebuano has no *po*.
7. **Greeting hours** (*buntag* to about 11, *udto* around noon, *hapon* to dusk, *gabii* after dark). The app's fixed slots use *Maayong hapon* for 12–18 and *Maayong gabii* for both evening and late night (`greet`).
8. **False friends and stress pairs** in §4.11.
9. **Second-place order of several short words** (*Wala pa ko* or *Wala ko pa*; *ko ka* combinations): not taught at A1 beyond one short pronoun, for that reason.
10. **`brand: "Bisaya"`**: what speakers call the language; some prefer *Binisaya* or *Sinugbuanon*. `self` follows the roadmap (*Sinugbuanong Binisaya*).
