# Practice design outweighs any "correct" grammar order

**Summary**

- **No "correct order" exists in the evidence.** The research notes contain no study comparing two whole syllabuses that differ only in item order, and nothing in them studied self-study app learners. What the evidence supplies is constraints: some forms are reliably mastered late, the learner's first language shifts which ones, short explicit explanations help, and recognition practice does not by itself build production.
- **Beginner Korean has a strong textbook convention, and the app matches it at the start** (Hangul, greetings, nouns with 이에요/예요). It departs in four places: six particles taught in one lesson, 을/를 and 에서 taught before any verb conjugation, basic negation at lesson 18 of 21, and casual forms (-자, texting) ahead of core grammar. Splitting the three-tense conjugation lesson is this report's inference, since some curricula also group the tenses.
- **Practice is the larger gap than order.** The per-lesson check is three recognition questions, against the one practice-volume figure in the notes (about 16 items) and findings that recognition practice under-trains production. The app also has drills, dialogues and review whose formats are not described here, so whether they already supply typed production for each grammar point is unknown.
- **English-for-Koreans can follow the conventional A1 to B1 order**, but article difficulty is documented up to advanced level, plural -s and articles are the forms with no Korean equivalent, and speaking trails reading, so those forms need repeated returns and production practice. Obligatory subjects are a predicted difficulty without learner data in the notes.
- **Both recommended orders below are this report's synthesis** of convention and evidence, not findings from the literature. Everything here comes from the notes; listed gaps were not filled from memory.

**Evidence labels used throughout**

- **[R] research-backed**: an empirical study, corpus or meta-analysis, as recorded in the notes. "Contested" is kept where the notes mark it.
- **[C] curriculum convention**: what textbooks, syllabuses and apps do, without direct empirical support.
- **[P] practitioner opinion**: a scholar's, teacher's or reviewer's stated position. **[P-inference]** marks an inference drawn by the note-takers or by this report; none of these was tested in any study in the notes.
- Combined tags: "inferred" after R or C means a design rule the note-takers derived from that kind of source, not a statement in the source; "A + B" means the item rests on both kinds; "conflicting" means the cited studies disagree.

## No study has tested lesson order, so order is mostly convention

- **[R]** The claim of one universal "natural order" has been falsified across first languages. A corpus of 11,893 exam scripts from seven L1 groups found orders more similar within an L1 than between L1s; Korean and Japanese learners deviate mainly on articles and plural -s ([Murakami & Alexopoulou 2016](https://core.ac.uk/download/42340394.pdf)).
- **[R]** That order evidence covers six English morphemes only. It says nothing directly about tenses beyond past -ed, modals, clause types, or any language other than English ([Murakami & Alexopoulou 2016](https://core.ac.uk/download/42340394.pdf)).
- **[R]** A pooled analysis of 12 studies (924 participants) found that salience, semantic complexity, regularity, syntactic category and frequency together explain a large portion of the English morpheme order ([Goldschneider & DeKeyser, ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ718472)). The often-quoted percentage was not verified in the notes.
- **[R, contested]** Pienemann's Teachability Hypothesis says instruction cannot make learners skip developmental stages (question formation, negation, relative clauses). The notes verified it only through a tertiary source ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teachability_Hypothesis)), and a four-learner counter-study reports a successful skip ([Zhang & Lantolf 2015](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/natural-or-artificial-is-the-route-of-l2-development-teachable/)).
- **[R]** For Korean as the target, one dissertation (111 adult learners, 76 English-L1) found the core particles (subject, object, topic, the two locatives) emerge first, later particles show "random variation", and verb endings fit no acquisition hierarchy at all ([Hwang 2002](https://core.ac.uk/download/5095188.pdf)). The author calls the hierarchy tentative.
- **[C]** Syllabuses resemble each other because "it is safer to follow what has been done before"; third person -s is taught very early although it is internalised very late ([Ellis 2006](https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/sli/Readings/Ellis%202006.pdf)). Hwang says her own team's Korean textbook order came from classroom judgment, not acquisition data ([Hwang 2002](https://core.ac.uk/download/5095188.pdf)).
- **[R, contested]** Explicit grammar instruction works. Norris & Ortega's meta-analysis is reported as explicit d = 1.13 versus implicit d = 0.54 (figures as reported by [Ke et al. 2021](https://testplatform.openjournals.nl/dujal/article/view/9361/12390) and [Shin 2010](https://core.ac.uk/download/201198976.pdf); the primary was not read). A later meta-analysis of 54 studies found a large overall effect (g = 1.06) but only "a minor difference" between explicit and implicit ([Kang, Sok & Han 2019, summary](https://www.switchboardta.org/evidence/thirty-five-years-of-isla-on-form-focused-instruction-a-meta-analysis/)). Critics note the tests favour explicit knowledge and 88.5% of the original samples had 30 or fewer participants ([Shin 2010](https://core.ac.uk/download/201198976.pdf)).
- **[R]** Memorised chunks feed grammar: in longitudinal classroom studies, chunks "break down over time and feed into the construction of the productive grammar" ([Myles 2015](https://www.essex.ac.uk/-/media/documents/departments/langling/myles-elct-15.pdf)). The learners were studying French, and their ages and sample sizes were not verified.
- **[P, contested]** Whether to delay grammar until a lexical base exists (Ellis) or start at once (N. Ellis, Lightbown, as reported by Ellis) is a disagreement between scholars, not a finding ([Ellis 2006](https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/sli/Readings/Ellis%202006.pdf)).
- **[P-inference]** Working rules that follow, with their real status:
  - Order by usefulness and frequency first (convention plus indirect support).
  - Separate "introduced" from "mastered", and do not gate progress on accurate production of late-mastered forms.
  - Teach chunks first, then lessons that vary one slot of a known chunk, then the stated rule.
  - Return to L1-contrast forms many times; one lesson does not finish them.

## The Korean order matches convention early and drifts after lesson 6

**The convention being compared against.** **[C]** Full ordered lists were read for Talk To Me In Korean Levels 1-2 ([L1](https://talktomeinkorean.com/learn-korean/level-1), [L2](https://talktomeinkorean.com/curriculum/level-2-korean-grammar)), How to Study Korean Unit 1 ([HTSK](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/home-page/unit-1-lessons-1-25/)), Sogang 1A and 1B ([1A](https://uwm.edu/language-resource-center/?p=992), [1B](https://uwm.edu/language-resource-center/?p=997); edition not stated), Integrated Korean Beginning 1 lessons 1-5 ([IK](https://korean.sitehost.iu.edu/clp/k101.html)), a 30-unit "Seoul Korean Level 1" list of unconfirmed publisher ([list](https://www.koreantopik.com/2021/05/seoul-korean-level-1-online-lessons.html)), and the particle order of four Korean-published series ([Lee 2023](https://oldlib.jejunu.ac.kr/bitstream/2020.oak/24456/2/%ed%95%9c%ea%b5%ad%ec%96%b4%20%ea%b5%90%ec%9e%ac%ec%97%90%20%eb%82%98%ed%83%80%eb%82%9c%20%ec%a1%b0%ec%82%ac%20%ec%96%91%ec%83%81%20%eb%b9%84%ea%b5%90%20%ec%97%b0%ea%b5%ac.pdf)). Korean Grammar in Use Beginning is a reference grouped by function, useful as a coverage checklist, not an order ([contents](https://nuri.hangeul.go.kr/search/media/toc/CAT000000020064)).

- **[C, inferred by the note-takers]** Consensus skeleton: Hangul → 이에요/예요 with 은/는 → 있어요/없어요 with 이/가 and location 에 → numbers and counters → present -아/어요 → 을/를 and 에서 → past → 도 and time expressions → negation, -고 싶다, -(으)세요 → future and -(으)ㄹ 수 있다 → adjective modifiers -(으)ㄴ → connectives -고, -아/어서, then -(으)면 → -아/어 보다, -아/어 주세요, comparison 보다.
- **[C]** Positions the curricula agree on: Hangul first; copula before any verb conjugation; present before past; 이/가 before 을/를; noun modifiers and -(으)면 in the second half; casual 반말 absent from every beginner list seen except HTSK lesson 6.
- **[C]** Positions the curricula split on, which are free design choices: when 을/를 arrives (TTMIK delays it to Level 2), where -고 싶다 sits (TTMIK lesson 13, Seoul list unit 21), where numbers and counters go, whether future follows past directly, and whether conjunction words (그리고, 그래서) precede connective endings (-고, -아서).

## The English course should recycle articles and plurals across every level

**What the notes establish about Korean-speaking learners of English**

- **[R]** Where the L1 lacks an equivalent form, accuracy "nearly always" stays below 90%. Korean is coded as lacking articles and plural -s; Korean learners show consistently high past -ed accuracy ([Murakami & Alexopoulou 2016](https://core.ac.uk/download/42340394.pdf)).
- **[R]** In speech, 46.7% of 1,553 errors by 32 Korean college students (TOEIC 600-900) were in the noun phrase and 18.6% in the verb phrase ([Won & Kim 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/13/7/695/htm)). In high-school writing, verb form led at 26%, with articles and determiners at 16% and prepositions at 15% ([Ha 2025](https://scholarworks.sookmyung.ac.kr/item/47eb31f1-c700-4070-9555-897c88141e1b); abstract only). The spoken noun-phrase category was not broken down into articles versus plurals.
- **[R]** Generic article uses stayed difficult for 77 advanced Korean adults ([Park 2013](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/5542/1/Master-2013-print.pdf)). After explicit teaching, relative-clause production stayed below about 60% in 137 Korean university students ([Chu 2012](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201203939212871.pdf)).
- **[R]** Learners produce many listed items one CEFR level later than syllabuses place them: 19 of 53 items in 416 writing scripts ([Jones, British Council](https://www.britishcouncil.org/sites/default/files/glyn_jones_layout.pdf)).
- **[R]** Speaking trails reading for Korean test-takers: EF EPI 2025 reading 540 versus speaking 489, from a self-selected sample ([EF EPI fact sheet](https://www.ef.com/assetscdn/WIBIwq6RdJvcD9bc8RMd/cefcom-epi-site/fact-sheets/2025/ef-epi-fact-sheet-south-korea-english.pdf)).
- **[R]** Of the pronunciation problems, only vowel insertion in consonant clusters has study-level support (32 adults, mean 12 years of English study; [Shin & Iverson 2014](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201420947476109.pdf)). The r/l, f/p, th and stress items are **[P]** lists.
- **[P]** Dropped subjects, missing dummy *it*, and missing possessive determiners are predicted from Korean structure by a linguist, not measured in learner data; the same author proposes teaching nouns with overt determiners from the start ([Ceong 2021](https://journals.uvic.ca/index.php/WPLC/article/view/20164/9109)).
- **[C]** Syllabuses already spiral the hard areas: articles at A1 and again at B1, quantifiers at A1, A2 and B1 ([test-english contents](https://test-english.com/grammar-points/contents/); one commercial site's order, not the English Grammar Profile).

**Comparison with the current course.** The notes do not list the 11 existing English lessons, so no lesson-by-lesson comparison is possible here. The only stated fact is that the course has no dedicated noun, verb or sentence-building lessons, which leaves out the two largest documented error zones for Korean speakers.

## Three recognition questions per lesson fall short of the sixteen-item anchor

**Scope.** The comparison below is with the per-lesson 3-question multiple-choice check. The app's drills, dialogues and review are known here only by name, so every practice recommendation is conditional on what those features already do.

**What the practice evidence says**

- **[R]** Practice builds mainly the skill it trains. In a meta-analysis of 30 studies, comprehension-based instruction won on receptive tests immediately (d = 1.09) while production-based instruction held a small durable edge on delayed production tests (d = -0.21) ([Shintani, Li & Ellis](https://espace.curtin.edu.au/bitstream/20.500.11937/54469/2/253836.pdf)). Accuracy transfers partly across skills; speed does not ([DeKeyser & Suzuki 2025](https://yuichisuzuki.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/PreprintDeKeyser-R.-M.-Suzuki-Y.-2025.-Skill-acquisition-theory.-In-B.-VanPatten-G.-D.-Keating-S.-Wulff-Eds.-Theories-in-second-language-acquisition-An-introduction-4th-ed.-pp.-157-182-.pdf)).
- **[R]** After identical training, production scores ran well below recognition scores: 0.30 to 0.49 on short answer versus 0.52 to 0.64 on multiple choice, a between-experiment comparison ([Pan et al. 2019](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PTLOR_2019W.pdf)).
- **[R]** The one hard practice-volume number: turning a rule into a usable procedure "was essentially complete after the first 16-item block", with further practice adding speed ([DeKeyser & Suzuki 2025](https://yuichisuzuki.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/PreprintDeKeyser-R.-M.-Suzuki-Y.-2025.-Skill-acquisition-theory.-In-B.-VanPatten-G.-D.-Keating-S.-Wulff-Eds.-Theories-in-second-language-acquisition-An-introduction-4th-ed.-pp.-157-182-.pdf)). No source gives an optimal lesson length or number of new points per session.
- **[R]** Format matters in log data from 291 students: eventual accuracy after retries was 97.0% for multiple choice, 95.8% for drag-and-drop and 82.0% for typed gap-fill, so learners can eliminate their way through the first two ([Woollaston et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.01592)). This is an observational preprint, and drag-and-drop is only a proxy for word tiles.
- **[R]** Blocked first, then mixed. Mixing two similar tenses hurt or did nothing within one session but helped across two sessions a week apart (d = 0.79 on production) ([Pan et al. 2019](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PTLOR_2019W.pdf)); a blocked-then-mixed schedule beat either alone for relative clauses ([Suzuki & Sunada](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/dynamic-interplay-between-practice-type-and-practice-schedule-in-a-second-language/C01D682D82EDBD00AF29965FCA57BAF3)).
- **[R, conflicting]** Spacing for grammar is unsettled: longer gaps helped retention in some studies, shorter gaps of 1 to 3 days helped speed in others ([Serrano 2022](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/download/35602/30649/76142)). No specific interval is proven.
- **[R]** Corrective feedback has a durable medium-to-large effect (d = 0.74 against controls); prompts 0.83, explicit correction 0.84 and recasts 0.53 did not differ significantly between groups, and younger learners benefited more ([Lyster & Saito 2010](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b)). The evidence is oral classroom feedback, so app use is an extrapolation. A second meta-analysis found implicit feedback effects were maintained better than explicit ones and that shorter treatments had larger effects, which conflicts with Lyster & Saito on treatment length ([Li 2010, abstract only](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ883422)).
- **[R, conflicting]** Explanation: a rule plus examples is held necessary before practice, and explicit L1-L2 contrast helped in one study ([DeKeyser & Suzuki 2025](https://yuichisuzuki.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/PreprintDeKeyser-R.-M.-Suzuki-Y.-2025.-Skill-acquisition-theory.-In-B.-VanPatten-G.-D.-Keating-S.-Wulff-Eds.-Theories-in-second-language-acquisition-An-introduction-4th-ed.-pp.-157-182-.pdf)); yet learners given no explanation caught up in processing-instruction research ([Rasuki 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1154080.pdf)), and a Korean-L1 meta-analysis reports implicit instruction stronger at beginner level from very small cells ([Schenck 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1293322.pdf)).
- **[R]** App evidence is thin. Nine adults after about 29 hours of Duolingo Turkish scored 33% on speaking and 37% on listening and reported too little grammar explanation and progress metrics that did not match ability ([Loewen et al. 2019](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A4D7C8F71782A37D258C19F357DDBCBE/S0958344019000065a.pdf/mobileassisted_language_learning_a_duolingo_case_study.pdf)). In a company-funded, uncontrolled Duolingo study, number of sessions predicted outcomes and weekly time did not ([Smith, Jiang & Peters 2024](https://rebrand.avantassessment.com/wp-content/uploads/The-effectiveness-of-Duolingo-in-developing-receptive-and-productive-language-knowledge-and-proficiency.pdf)).
- **[R]** Shadowing and dictation have no grammar evidence in the notes; the one shadowing study found no significant group effect on listening ([Sumiyoshi 2019](https://e-flt.nus.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/sumiyoshi.pdf)).

**How the candidate additions stand**

| Candidate | Support in the notes |
|---|---|
| Typed production | Strongest case: skill-specificity findings [R]; Korean particle accuracy is lower in speech than in writing ([Ahn 2015](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/handle/1773/34000)) [R] |
| Sentence-builder tiles | A bridge format, not an end state; needs distractor tiles and first-attempt scoring [P-inference from an observational preprint] |
| Sound-change rules | [R] for explicit teaching of nasalisation and aspiration; sample L1 unconfirmed |
| 이에요 vs 있어요; question words; want/like/can/have to; joining; comparing; adjectives before nouns | All present in the curricula read [C]; "want/like/can/have to" spans items that curricula place far apart, so split it |
| Pronouns | Standard at A1 in English syllabuses [C]; in Korean lists it appears only as "forms of I" in HTSK lesson 6 [C] |
| Listening | Justified as its own skill by skill-specificity [R]; not a grammar feature |
| Can-do checkpoints | [P-inference], supported indirectly by the mismatch between app metrics and ability that learners reported [R] |

**Prioritized changes to the app**

1. **Make sure every grammar point gets about 12 to 20 practice items ending in typed production**, by extending the 3-question check or by wiring the existing drills to each lesson. Escalate from multiple choice to tiles to typed gap-fill to a typed sentence, scored on first attempt only. [P-inference from R]
2. **Restructure the Korean course**: split Particles and Conjugating verbs, move 안 to just after past tense, pair counters with numbers, and move -자 and Texting to the end. [C]
3. **Put grammar into the spaced-repetition deck as production prompts, if the current review is recognition-only**: typed gap-fill on the particle, ending or article, drawn from varied sentences, mixed with confusable neighbours (은/는 vs 이/가; simple past vs present perfect) only after a blocked introduction. Use short early gaps, then longer ones; no interval is proven. [P-inference from R]
4. **Build the English course on the A1 spine above** with a standing article and plural check in every production item, early obligatory-subject lessons, and a recognition-plus-production placement check. [C + P-inference]
5. **Add the missing Korean lessons**: 있어요 with 이/가, question words, -고 싶다, 도 and 만, joining, modifiers, comparing, and a sound-change lesson. [C; R for sound changes]
6. **Upgrade feedback** to one hint and one retry, then the answer with a one-line reason; a plain correct-answer flash is an acceptable fallback. [P-inference from oral classroom R]
7. **Limit romanization to the Hangul lesson** and make it optional on flashcards after that. [C + P; no controlled evidence]
8. **Add can-do checkpoints based on unaided typed production**, and separate "introduced" from "mastered" so late-mastered forms never block progress. [P-inference]
9. **Keep explanations to one screen, contrastive with the learner's first language, with a "show rule" button during practice.** [P-inference; evidence conflicts at beginner level]
10. **Log first-attempt accuracy per grammar point** and test alternative lesson orders, since the app's single-L1 courses can produce the evidence the literature lacks. [P-inference]

## What the notes could not verify

- **No self-study or app evidence.** Every sequencing and practice study cited used classroom, laboratory or exam data. No study covered English speakers learning Korean, or Korean speakers learning English, in an app.
- **No whole-syllabus order experiment** and no experiment comparing chunks-first with grammar-from-day-one.
- **Korean curricula not retrieved:** Yonsei and Ewha grammar lists, the full Sejong syllabus, the 45 grade-1 and 45 grade-2 items of the International Standard Curriculum, Integrated Korean lessons 6 onward, and any official TOPIK grammar list. Sogang lists probably reflect a pre-2024 edition.
- **Korean acquisition gaps:** no findings on honorifics, irregular verbs, number systems or the 은/는 versus 이/가 choice; no outcome study of 요-first versus formal-first, and the usual rationale for either was not sourced; no controlled romanization study; Hwang's conclusions and abbreviation key were not read.
- **English curricula not retrieved:** English Grammar Profile level placements, the full Core Inventory (the list used is an incomplete subset), and named coursebook contents.
- **Korean-L1 English gaps:** no empirical study on third person -s, he/she confusion, present perfect versus past simple, plural -s omission frequency, or r/l and f/p; the Swan & Smith chapter on Korean speakers was not read.
- **Method gaps:** Processability Theory's stage list and Pienemann's papers; primary texts of Norris & Ortega, Spada & Tomita and Kang et al.; effect sizes in Li (2010); any study of retrieval practice for grammar; optimal lesson length; feedback timing; tiles versus other formats in a controlled design; whether typed production with automated feedback substitutes for conversation.
- **Unconfirmed source details:** the Babbel study's funding, the app rubric evaluation's authors and independence, and the author order of Pan et al.

## Conclusion

The question "what is the right order?" turns out to be the wrong lever. Order is under-determined by evidence and well-determined by convention, so the sensible move is to align the Korean course with the textbook consensus where the textbooks agree, choose freely where they split, and say so plainly instead of claiming a scientifically correct sequence. The better-supported levers sit elsewhere: in how many items a learner produces per grammar point, in whether those items require typing the form, and in how often late-mastered forms come back.

The two courses should not mirror each other. English-for-Koreans can lean on direct L1-specific data about articles, plurals and past tense, while Korean-for-English-speakers rests on one dissertation, a few small studies and textbook practice. Because each course has a single known first language and the app can log first-attempt accuracy per grammar point, it is positioned to run the order experiment that the notes show nobody has published.
